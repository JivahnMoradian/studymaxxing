Plain-English definitions of the key terms from every chapter (127 entries). Click a chapter tag to open the full lesson.

A
Absolute Advantage1.2 →
Being able to make something with fewer resources (or more output from the same resources) than someone else. A top lawyer who is also the fastest typist in the office has an absolute advantage in both jobs.
AD vs. the Demand for One Product3.1 →
The demand for pizza falls as pizza gets pricier because people switch to burgers. AD is about all goods at once, so there is nothing cheaper to switch to; its downward slope comes from other effects, such as higher prices eroding what savings can buy and making domestic goods less competitive against imports.
Aggregate Demand (AD)3.1 →
Total planned spending on a country's output, added up across the whole economy. Think of it as the sum of every shopping cart in the country: yours, your employer's, the city's and your overseas customers'.
Ample Reserves4.4 →
A system in which banks hold far more reserves than they are required to hold. The Fed then steers short-term rates mainly by changing the interest rate it pays banks on reserves, not by changing the quantity of reserves day to day.
Appreciation vs. Depreciation6.2 →
A currency appreciates when it becomes worth more in terms of another (a dollar buys more euros), and depreciates when it becomes worth less. If the dollar appreciates against the euro, the euro depreciates against the dollar. Appreciation makes foreign goods and travel cheaper for you but your exports more expensive for foreigners; depreciation does the opposite.
B
Bank Run4.1 →
Many depositors try to withdraw at the same time. Even a healthy bank cannot pay everyone at once, because its money is tied up in loans and bonds, so the fear of a run can cause one.
Base Year2.2 →
The year whose prices are used as the yardstick. Pick a different base year and the real numbers change, but the growth rates come out nearly the same.
Bond4.2 →
A loan you make to a government or company. It pays fixed interest and returns your principal on a set date. Its market price moves opposite to interest rates: when new bonds pay more, older bonds that pay less become worth less to buyers.
Budget Deficit vs. National Debt5.3 →
The deficit is a flow: the gap in one year when the government spends more than it collects in taxes. The debt is a stock: the total amount owed after all past deficits (less any surpluses). A deficit adds to the debt; a surplus reduces it. Think of the deficit as this year's overspending and the debt as the credit-card balance.
C
Capital and Financial Account6.1 →
The record of ownership moving across borders: foreigners buying a country's stocks, bonds, homes and companies (money in), and its residents buying foreign ones (money out).
Capital Flows6.3 →
Money moving across borders to buy stocks, bonds, property or businesses. Investors prefer higher returns, so when a country's interest rates rise relative to others, funds flow in, demand for its currency rises and the currency tends to strengthen.
Capital Mobility6.2 →
How easily money moves in and out of a country. When it is high, investors chase higher interest rates, which makes a peg hard to defend if domestic rates differ from the anchor country's.
Catch-Up Growth (Convergence)5.4 →
The tendency of poorer countries that invest and adopt existing technologies to grow faster than richer ones and narrow the gap. It is a tendency, not a guarantee: many poor countries have not caught up.
Central Bank Credibility5.2 →
How much the public believes the central bank will actually do what it says. Credibility is valuable because wage deals, prices and loan rates are set on what people expect the central bank to do. A believed target for inflation helps hold inflation down on its own.
Comparative Advantage1.2 →
Being able to make something at a lower opportunity cost than someone else, meaning you give up less of other things to produce it. This, not absolute advantage, decides who should do what.
Compound Interest4.2 →
Interest earned on your earlier interest, not just on your original deposit. Money growing at rate r for n years multiplies by (1 + r)n. It works for you on savings and against you on debt.
CPI (Consumer Price Index)2.4 →
The standard US inflation gauge. Statisticians price a fixed "basket" of what typical households buy (rent, food, gasoline, medical care...) and track its cost over time. Core Inflation leaves out food and energy because their prices swing a lot.
Crowding Out3.5 →
The concern that when the government borrows heavily, it competes with businesses and households for loans, pushing interest rates up and discouraging private spending and investment. How large this effect is, especially in a weak economy, is debated.
Currency Conversion Cost6.2 →
The "real" price when you convert money also includes a markup. Banks, card issuers, exchange kiosks and merchants each add fees or build a margin into the rate they give you.
Currency Manipulation (Accusation)6.4 →
A claim that a country is deliberately holding its currency down to help its exporters. The US Treasury can formally label a country a currency manipulator; when it did so for China in 2019, Goldman Sachs called the label mostly symbolic.
Current Account6.1 →
The broadest record of the flow of income between a country and the world. It adds up the trade balance, the investment income and wages earned abroad minus those paid to foreigners, and gifts such as money sent home by workers (remittances). It is usually close to the trade balance, but not identical to it.
Cyclical Unemployment2.3 →
Caused by a weak economy, and the kind that falls when the economy recovers.
D
Debt-to-GDP Ratio5.3 →
The national debt divided by the country's annual output. It is the standard way to compare debt across countries and over time, because what matters is the size of the debt relative to the income that is available to service it. A household with a $300,000 mortgage is in a very different position at a $40,000 income than at a $200,000 income. (Be careful which debt is meant: "gross" counts everything the government owes, including to its own agencies; "held by the public" or "net" subtracts the part that the government owes to itself.)
Demand1.3 →
How much of a good buyers want at each possible price. A higher price means less quantity demanded. A change in demand (a shift) comes from something other than the price: income, tastes, the number of buyers, or the prices of related goods.
Demand for Money4.3 →
How much cash and bank balances people want to hold. When interest rates are high, holding cash is costly because it earns little compared with bonds or savings accounts, so people want to hold less of it.
Demand-Pull vs. Cost-Push Inflation3.2 →
Demand-pull inflation comes from people wanting to buy more than the economy can supply (aggregate demand rising faster than capacity). Cost-push inflation comes from it costing more to make things (a negative supply shock). In real life the two are often mixed, and economists argue about the proportions.
Demand-Side Policy5.5 →
Changes in government spending, taxes or interest rates aimed at the total amount people and firms want to buy. It is used mainly to smooth out recessions and overheating in the short run.
Deposit Insurance4.1 →
A government guarantee that you get your money back if the bank fails. In the US the FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation) covers $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category (for example single accounts, joint accounts and retirement accounts are counted separately).
Discouraged Workers2.3 →
People who want a job but have stopped looking because they think none are available. They vanish from the headline rate.
Discretionary vs. Automatic3.5 →
Discretionary fiscal policy means lawmakers deliberately pass a new law (a stimulus package, a tax cut). Automatic Stabilizers need no new law: when the economy weakens, tax collections fall and benefits such as unemployment insurance rise on their own, which cushions the drop in people's incomes; in a boom the process reverses.
Diversification4.2 →
Spreading money across many assets so that no single failure ruins you.
E
Economic Growth5.4 →
A rise in an economy's output over time. For living standards what matters is growth of real (inflation-adjusted) GDP per person, not total GDP, since a country can grow in total simply by having more people.
Exchange Rate6.2 →
How much of one currency you must pay to get one unit of another. It can be quoted either way: if one euro costs $1.18, then one dollar buys about 0.85 euros (the two quotes are reciprocals). Always check which currency is being priced.
Expansionary vs. Contractionary3.5 →
Expansionary policy (more spending or lower taxes) aims to raise demand in a weak economy and usually widens the deficit. Contractionary policy (less spending or higher taxes) aims to reduce demand, for example to cool inflation or to bring the deficit down.
Expected Inflation5.1 →
The inflation rate that workers, firms and lenders assume when they sign wage deals, set prices and fix interest rates. When expectations rise, the whole short-run curve shifts up: the same unemployment now comes with more inflation.
Expected vs. Unexpected Inflation2.4 →
If everyone expects 3% inflation, interest rates and wages adjust for it. Unexpected inflation helps borrowers (they repay with cheaper dollars) and hurts lenders and people on fixed incomes.
F
Federal Funds Rate4.4 →
The interest rate banks charge each other for overnight loans. The Fed does not set it directly; it announces a target range (for example 3.75% to 4.00%) and uses its tools to keep the market rate inside that range.
Federal Funds Rate vs. Mortgage Rate4.3 →
The federal funds rate is the short-term rate banks charge each other overnight, which the Fed targets. A mortgage rate is a 30-year loan rate set in the market, and it tends to follow longer-term bond yields such as the 10-year Treasury, not the Fed's overnight rate directly.
Fiat Money4.1 →
Money that has value because the government says it is legal tender and people trust it, not because it is backed by gold or another commodity. The US dollar has been fiat money since August 15, 1971, when President Nixon closed the "gold window" and foreign governments could no longer swap dollars for gold.
Final vs. Intermediate Goods2.1 →
Only finished products count. The bread counts; the flour the baker bought does not, otherwise the same value would be counted twice. (The tidy way to avoid this is value added: each firm's sales minus what it paid other firms.)
Fiscal Policy3.5 →
Changes in government spending and taxes aimed at influencing overall demand, jobs and prices. It is run by the elected government (in the US, Congress and the President), unlike monetary policy, which is run by the central bank.
Fisher Effect5.2 →
If people expect higher inflation, lenders demand higher nominal interest rates to keep the same real return, so nominal rates rise roughly one-for-one with expected inflation. This is why mortgage and bond rates are very high in high-inflation countries.
Floating Exchange Rate6.2 →
The rate is set freely by buyers and sellers in the currency market, and moves every day. The US, the euro area, Japan, the UK and Switzerland all float.
Fractional-Reserve Banking4.1 →
A bank keeps only a fraction of deposits as cash or reserves and lends out the rest. Your deposit is a claim on the bank, not a pile of your own bills sitting in a vault.
Frictional Unemployment2.3 →
Short spells while people change jobs or enter the market. Healthy and unavoidable.
G
Gains from Trade1.2 →
After specializing and swapping, everyone can end up with more than they could produce alone. It happens whenever opportunity costs differ. Trade can still create winners and losers within a country, which is why trade policy is politically contested.
GDP (Gross Domestic Product)2.1 →
The market value of all final goods and services produced inside a country's borders in a year.
GDP Deflator2.2 →
A price index covering everything produced in a country (consumer goods, business equipment, government services). Nominal GDP divided by real GDP, times 100, gives the deflator. Compare it with the CPI, which tracks what typical households buy, including imports.
GDP per Capita2.1 →
GDP divided by the population. It is the standard shorthand for how rich a country is.
GDP vs. GNP2.1 →
GDP counts what is produced inside a country, whoever owns the factory. GNP counts what is produced by a country's own citizens and companies, wherever in the world they operate. A Japanese-owned car plant in Kentucky adds to US GDP because the cars are made in the US; the profits flowing back to the Japanese owners count in Japan's GNP, not America's. For most large countries the two are close, but they can differ a lot where foreign companies dominate (see Ireland below).
Global Supply Chain1.2 →
A product designed in one country, with parts made in several others and assembled in yet another. Each step goes to wherever it is cheapest or best done.
I
Impossible Trinity6.2 →
A country cannot have a fixed exchange rate, free movement of capital and independent interest-rate policy all at once; it must give up one. That is why countries sharing the euro cannot set their own interest rates.
Inflation Rate2.4 →
The yearly percentage rise in the general price level. If it is 4%, a basket of goods that cost $100 last year costs $104 now.
Interest on Reserve Balances (IORB)4.4 →
The interest rate the Fed pays banks on the reserves they keep at the Fed. Banks will not lend reserves for less than this rate, so it acts as a floor for the federal funds rate.
Interest Rate4.3 →
The price of using someone else's money for a period, quoted as a percentage per year. For a saver it is the reward for waiting; for a borrower it is the cost of borrowing.
Interest Rate vs. Growth Rate5.3 →
A debt-to-GDP ratio stabilizes or falls if the economy's growth rate is at least as high as the interest rate on the debt (before new borrowing). When interest rates are above the growth rate, the ratio rises on its own.
Interest-Rate Gap6.3 →
The difference between interest rates in two countries. A wide gap is one of the biggest drivers of currency moves: high-rate countries tend to see stronger currencies, low-rate countries weaker ones.
L
Labor Force2.3 →
Everyone who is employed plus everyone who is unemployed. Retirees, full-time students and people who are not looking are outside it.
Labor Force Participation Rate2.3 →
The share of working-age adults who are in the labor force, meaning they are either working or actively looking for work.
Laffer Curve5.5 →
The idea that, as a tax rate rises from zero, government revenue first rises and then falls once rates get so high that people work, invest or report less. The disagreement is not whether the curve exists in principle but where today's rates sit on it. Estimates of where the peak lies vary, and how much any given tax cut recoups through extra activity is argued over.
Lags4.4 →
Policy takes time to work. Economists commonly say the full effect of a rate change on spending and inflation takes many months, so the Fed acts on forecasts, not just today's data, and can overshoot or undershoot.
Liquidity Trap4.4 →
A situation where interest rates are already near zero, so further central-bank easing has little extra effect on spending. In that case fiscal policy tends to work more strongly.
Loanable Funds Market4.3 →
The market where savers (supplying money) meet borrowers (demanding it). If people save more, the supply rises and interest rates tend to fall. If governments and businesses borrow more, the demand rises and rates tend to rise.
Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS)3.2 →
The economy's full-capacity output, which is what it can sustain once wages and prices have fully adjusted. It is set by the number of workers, their skills, the machines and buildings they use, and technology. It does not depend on the price level, so doubling every price and every wage would not make the country able to produce more.
Long-Run Phillips Curve5.1 →
A vertical line at the natural rate. It says that, once expectations have caught up, a country cannot buy permanently lower unemployment with permanently higher inflation.
M
Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC)3.4 →
The share of each extra dollar of income that a household spends. If you receive $100 and spend $80, your MPC is 0.8.
Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS)3.4 →
The share of each extra dollar that is not spent. It is 1 minus the MPC, so in the example above the MPS is 0.2. For this purpose, paying down debt counts on the "not spent" side, even though it feels like a use of the money.
Monetary Policy in an Open Economy6.4 →
When a central bank cuts interest rates, borrowing and spending rise, but the currency also tends to weaken (investors earn less), which raises exports and import prices. Raising rates does the opposite. The currency channel can amplify the effect of the rate change.
Money4.1 →
Anything widely accepted for payment. It does three jobs: a medium of exchange (you can buy things with it), a unit of account (prices are quoted in it) and a store of value (it keeps its worth until you spend it, though inflation erodes that).
Money Illusion2.2 →
Judging by the number on the paycheck or the chart instead of by what it will buy.
Money Neutrality5.2 →
The idea that, in the long run, a change in the amount of money changes prices and nominal amounts (wages, rents, the numbers on price tags) but not real things such as how many goods are produced or how many people are employed. Doubling every dollar in the economy would, in the end, double prices and leave real incomes roughly where they were. In the short run, money can affect output and jobs; the debate is about how long "short" is.
Monthly Mortgage Payment4.3 →
For a fixed 30-year loan, the payment is set by the loan size and the rate. Small changes in the rate move the payment a lot, because interest is charged over 360 months.
Movement Along vs. Shift of AD3.1 →
A change in the overall price level moves us along a given AD curve. A change in anything else that affects spending (confidence, wealth, interest rates, taxes, foreign incomes) shifts the whole curve left or right.
Multiplier Effect3.4 →
The idea that an initial change in spending ends up changing total income by a larger amount. If a shop owner spends her extra $100 of revenue on a new coat, the tailor's income rises, and the tailor spends part of that, and so on. The textbook formula for the spending multiplier is 1 divided by the MPS, so an MPC of 0.8 gives a multiplier of 5 in a simplified model.
N
Natural Rate of Unemployment2.3 →
The rate that remains when the economy is healthy, made up of frictional and structural unemployment. The economy is at "full employment" when only the natural rate is left.
Net Exports (NX)6.3 →
What a country sells abroad (exports) minus what it buys from abroad (imports). Positive means a trade surplus, negative a deficit.
Net Interest5.3 →
What the government pays each year on its debt, after subtracting the interest it earns. It rises with both the size of the debt and the interest rate.
Net International Investment Position (NIIP)6.1 →
What a country owns abroad minus what foreigners own in it. A negative figure means the country is a net debtor to the world. It is a stock (a total at one date), whereas the current account is a flow (over a year).
Nominal2.2 →
Measured in the prices of the day. Your salary, a company's revenue, and headline GDP are all nominal figures.
Nominal vs. Real Interest Rate5.2 →
The nominal rate is the rate printed on the loan or savings account. The real rate is what it is worth after inflation: roughly the nominal rate minus inflation. A 5% savings rate with 3% inflation leaves you only about 2% better off in what you can buy.
Nominal vs. Real Return4.2 →
The nominal return is the number the bank or fund quotes. The real return is what is left after subtracting inflation, which is what your purchasing power actually does. Savings paying 4% while prices rise 3.7% a year grow your buying power by only about 0.3% a year.
O
Open Market Operations4.4 →
The Fed's main tool. It buys or sells government bonds to add or remove money from the banking system, which pushes short-term rates down or up. It also sets other rates, such as the one it pays banks on their reserves.
Opportunity Cost1.1 →
The value of the best alternative you gave up when you made a choice. It is not the sum of everything you passed on, only the single next-best option. It is often measured in time or foregone income rather than in a price tag.
P
Pass-Through6.3 →
How much of an exchange-rate change shows up in the prices you pay. A weaker currency feeds into import prices and, from there, into overall inflation, but not all at once and not one-for-one.
Policy Lags3.5 →
It takes time to notice a problem, pass a law and spend the money. By the time a measure takes effect, the economy may already have changed.
Potential Output3.3 →
The most the economy can produce year after year without inflation speeding up, using its workers and equipment at normal, sustainable levels. The unemployment rate at that level is called the natural rate of unemployment, which as of September 2026 is estimated to be around 4.2% for the US.
Present Value4.2 →
What a future sum of money is worth today. It is found by discounting: dividing by (1 + r)n. At 5%, $1,000 promised in 10 years is worth about $614 today.
Price Ceiling (e.g., Rent Control)1.3 →
A legal maximum price. If it is set below the equilibrium price, it creates a shortage, because the price cannot rise to clear the market.
Price Floor (e.g., Minimum Wage)1.3 →
A legal minimum price. If it is set above the equilibrium price, it creates a surplus, because the price cannot fall to clear the market. For a minimum wage, the surplus is workers who want jobs at that wage but cannot find them, also known as unemployment.
Prime Rate4.4 →
The benchmark rate banks use for many loans. By convention it sits 3 percentage points above the top of the Fed's target range, so when the Fed moves, the prime rate moves the same day or so, and variable-rate loans such as most credit cards follow.
Production Possibilities Curve (PPC)1.1 →
A simple picture of an economy's trade-off. It shows the most of one good (say, military equipment) the country can make for each possible amount of another (say, consumer goods), given its workers, factories and technology. More of one means less of the other.
Productivity5.4 →
Output per hour of work. It is the main source of lasting growth in living standards, because more output per hour, rather than simply more hours, is what lets average incomes rise.
Q
Quantity Theory of Money5.2 →
The claim that prices track the amount of money. Its equation, M x V = P x Y, says the money supply (M) times how often each unit changes hands per year (velocity, V) equals the price level (P) times real output (Y). If velocity and output are roughly steady, faster money growth means faster inflation.
Quota6.4 →
A legal limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported. Unlike a tariff, a quota raises no tax revenue; the higher price goes to whoever holds the right to import.
R
Real2.2 →
Adjusted for changes in prices, so that a "dollar" means the same purchasing power in every year being compared. Real GDP, real wages and real returns are what tell you if people are better off.
Real Wage2.2 →
Your pay divided by a price index. If prices rise faster than your pay, your real wage falls even though your paycheck went up.
Recession and Expansion2.4 →
A recession is a broad, significant decline in economic activity (jobs, income, spending, production). An expansion is the growth period between recessions. Together they form the business cycle.
Recessionary Gap vs. Inflationary Gap3.3 →
A Recessionary Gap means the economy is producing below its potential: factories are idle and unemployment is above the natural rate. An Inflationary Gap means the economy is producing above its potential, which is only possible temporarily (overtime, workers postponing retirement, factories running around the clock) and tends to push prices and wages up.
Rule of 704.2 →
A shortcut for doubling time. Divide 70 by the annual growth rate in percent. At 7% a year money doubles in about 10 years; at 2% inflation prices double in about 35 years. It is very accurate near 2% and still a good estimate at higher rates.
Rules vs. Discretion5.5 →
A rule commits a central bank or government to a set formula or target (for example "raise interest rates by a set amount when inflation exceeds 2%"). Discretion leaves policymakers free to respond case by case. Rules are predictable; discretion is flexible.
S
Scarcity1.1 →
There is never enough time, money or resources to do everything we want, so we have to choose. Scarcity applies to billionaires and governments as much as to students.
Self-Correction3.3 →
The idea that a gap eventually closes by itself. In a slump, high unemployment slowly pushes wages and costs down, which lets firms produce more at any price; in a boom, a tight job market pushes wages and costs up, which cools production. The debate is about speed, not whether the adjustment happens at all.
Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS)3.2 →
The amount businesses will produce at each overall price level while some of their costs (especially wages and contracts) are still fixed. Its key lesson: if the cost of their inputs changes, firms change how much they produce at any given price.
Short-Run Equilibrium3.3 →
The overall price level and level of output at which the amount people want to buy equals the amount firms want to produce right now. It can be a good place (a healthy, full-employment economy) or a bad one (a slump or an overheated boom).
Short-Run Phillips Curve5.1 →
The downward-sloping relationship between unemployment and inflation while people's expectations of inflation are held fixed. A booming economy with few unemployed workers tends to push wages and prices up faster; a weak economy pulls them down.
Shortage vs. Surplus1.3 →
A shortage means buyers want more than sellers offer at the current price, which pushes the price up. A surplus means sellers have more than buyers want, which pushes the price down.
Sources of Growth5.4 →
(1) More or better physical capital (machines, buildings, roads), (2) human capital (skills, education, health), and (3) technology and better ways of organizing production, together with institutions such as property rights and the rule of law that make investment worthwhile. Economists call the part of growth not explained by more capital and labor total factor productivity.
Specialization1.2 →
Each person or country concentrates on the activity where its comparative advantage is greatest.
Stagflation3.2 →
A combination of stagnation (weak output and rising unemployment) and inflation (rising prices) at the same time. It is the signature of a negative supply shock, and it is painful because the two problems are usually treated with opposite medicine.
Sticky Wages and Prices3.3 →
Wages and many prices do not adjust instantly when conditions change, because of contracts, habit and fear of hurting morale. Wage cuts are especially rare. Stickiness is the reason a slump can last for a long time rather than correcting overnight.
Stock4.2 →
A share of ownership in a company. Unlike a bond, it promises nothing fixed, so it carries more risk and has historically earned more over long periods.
Structural Unemployment2.3 →
A mismatch between workers' skills or location and the jobs available (a factory closes, an industry shrinks).
Supply1.3 →
How much sellers will offer at each price. A higher price means more quantity supplied. A change in supply comes from something other than price, such as production costs, technology, the number of sellers or supply disruptions.
Supply Shock3.2 →
A sudden change in production costs that hits many businesses at once, such as an oil price spike, a pandemic closing factories, or a shortage of a key part. A negative supply shock means higher costs and lower output; a positive one (for example a drop in oil prices) means the reverse.
Supply-Side Policy5.5 →
Measures aimed at raising the economy's capacity to produce in the long run: for example lower taxes meant to encourage work, saving and investment, less regulation, spending on education or infrastructure, and policies on immigration or competition. People of different political views favor different supply-side policies, and which ones work best is debated.
T
Tariff6.4 →
A tax on imported goods, paid by the importing firm when goods cross the border. It raises the price of the imported good and of competing domestic goods, raises revenue for the government, and can prompt other countries to retaliate.
Taylor Rule5.5 →
A well-known rule of thumb linking the central bank's interest rate to inflation and to how far the economy is from full employment output. It is used mainly as a reference point, not an automatic switch.
Temporary vs. Permanent Income3.4 →
People tend to spend more of a lasting rise in income (a raise) than of a one-time windfall (a rebate check), which they are more likely to save or use to pay off debt. This is one reason the MPC is not a single fixed number.
The Fed's Mandate4.4 →
Congress has told the Fed to promote maximum employment and stable prices. The Fed defines stable prices as 2% inflation a year, measured by the PCE price index (a broad gauge of what households spend). The two goals can pull in different directions, which is why Fed decisions are often debated.
The Four Buyers (C, I, G, NX)3.1 →
Consumption is household spending on things like groceries, cars and rent; investment is business spending on equipment and buildings, plus new home construction (buying shares or bonds does not count); government spending is purchases of goods and services such as roads, schools and soldiers' pay (pensions and benefit payments are not counted, because they are transfers rather than purchases); net exports are what foreigners buy from us minus what we buy from them.
Trade Deficit vs. Trade Surplus6.1 →
A deficit means a country buys more goods and services from abroad (imports) than it sells abroad (exports); a surplus is the reverse. The US runs a deficit; Germany and China run surpluses.
Trade-Off1.1 →
Choosing more of one thing means getting less of another. An hour spent studying is an hour not spent working or resting.
U
Unemployed2.3 →
Someone without a job who is available to work and has actively looked for one recently. Someone who has stopped looking does not count.
Unemployment Rate2.3 →
Unemployed people divided by the labor force. It can fall simply because people give up looking, so it should be read alongside the Labor Force Participation Rate.
Unplanned Inventories3.4 →
Goods that firms produced but could not sell as planned. When they pile up, firms cut production and hire fewer workers; when shelves empty faster than planned, firms raise production.
W
Wealth Effect3.1 →
When the value of what people own (homes, shares) falls, they tend to spend less, even if their paycheck has not changed. When their assets rise, they tend to spend more. This is one reason falling house prices or a stock crash can slow the whole economy.
Who Bears a Tariff ("Incidence")6.4 →
The tax is legally paid by the importer, but the cost is shared among foreign exporters (if they cut their prices), importers (lower profit margins), and domestic consumers (higher prices). How the burden is split is an empirical question, and studies of recent tariffs disagree.
