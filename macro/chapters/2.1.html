UNIT 2 — ECONOMIC INDICATORS & THE BUSINESS CYCLE

Circular Flow & GDP

Practice these problems →

What GDP Measures

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given period (usually a quarter or a year). Each word matters:

  • Market Value: goods are added up using their prices, so apples and iPhones can be combined.
  • Final: only goods sold to their end user count. Intermediate goods (flour sold to a bakery) are already included in the price of the final good (bread).
  • Produced: only current production counts, not the resale of things made earlier.
  • Within Borders: location matters, not nationality of the producer.
  • Period: GDP is a flow (dollars per year), not a stock.

The Circular Flow

The Circular Flow Model shows the economy as a loop between households and firms, linked by two sets of markets.

  • In product markets, households spend money and firms supply goods and services.
  • In factor (resource) markets, households sell labor, land and capital, and firms pay wages, rent, interest and profit.

Money flows one way round the loop while goods and resources flow the other way. Because every dollar spent is someone's income, total spending = total output = total income. That is why GDP can be measured in three equivalent ways.

The full model adds the three other sectors. Leakages (money leaving the household-firm loop) are saving, taxes and imports. Injections (money entering it) are investment, government purchases and exports.

Drawing It Yourself: put Households on the left and Firms on the right. Draw product markets on top and factor markets below, then a clockwise money loop (spending, revenue, factor payments, income) and a counter-clockwise real loop (goods, resources).

Three Ways to Measure GDP

1. Expenditure approach: add up spending on newly produced final goods and services.

Y = C + I + G + NX
NX = X - M

Here Y is GDP (the total value of final goods and services produced), and the four components are defined in the table below.

Component What It Includes What It Excludes
C: Consumption Household spending on durables, nondurables and services Purchases of new homes (that is investment)
I: Investment Business purchases of new capital (equipment, structures, software), new residential construction, changes in inventories Buying stocks and bonds (financial "investment")
G: Government Purchases Spending on goods and services (roads, soldiers, teachers' salaries) Transfer payments (Social Security, unemployment benefits)
NX: Net Exports Exports (X) minus imports (M)

Imports are subtracted because they are already included in C, I or G, but were not produced domestically.

2. Income approach: add up all income earned from producing output.

GDP = Wages + Rent + Interest + Profit  (+ Depreciation + Indirect Business Taxes)

The two items in brackets are adjustments the official statistics make: depreciation is a cost of production that is not anyone's income, and indirect taxes (such as sales taxes) are part of the price but are not income to a factor of production. AP questions usually give you only the four main income types.

3. Value-added approach: sum the value added at every stage of production, where

Value Added = Revenue - Cost of Intermediate Goods
Farmer sells wheat for $30; miller sells flour for $50; baker sells bread for $90:
30 + (50-30) + (90-50) = 90 = price of the bread

The sum of value added across all stages equals the price of the final good. This avoids double counting.

What GDP Leaves Out

Excluded Why
Used goods (a 2015 car) Counted when first produced. Only a dealer's commission is new production.
Financial transactions (stocks, bonds) They transfer ownership; nothing new is produced.
Transfer payments Money moves without any good or service being produced.
Intermediate goods Already in the price of the final good.
Non-market activity (housework, volunteering, child care by a parent) Not sold, so no market price.
Underground economy (illegal sales, cash jobs off the books) Unreported.
Leisure, environmental damage, income distribution Not reflected in GDP, which is a single total of market output.

Goods produced but unsold are counted as inventory investment in the year they are made, so GDP measures what was produced, not just what was sold.

GDP vs. GNP and GDP per Capita

GDP counts production inside the country's borders, by anyone. GNP (Gross National Product) counts income earned by the country's residents, wherever they are.

GNP = GDP + Income Earned Abroad by Residents - Income Earned Domestically by Foreigners
GDP = 2,000; residents earn 60 abroad; foreigners earn 85 here ⟹ GNP = 2,000 + 60 - 85 = 1,975

GDP per Capita = GDP ÷ population. It is the usual proxy for average living standards.

GDP per Capita = GDPPopulation

It is only a proxy: it ignores how income is distributed, leisure, non-market work, the underground economy, environmental costs, health and quality of life, and quality improvements. Spending on cleaning up a disaster raises GDP even though people are not better off.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Other Output and Income Measures: Subtracting depreciation (the wear and tear on capital) gives the net measures:

NDP = GDP - Depreciation
NNP = GNP - Depreciation

Disposable Income: the income households have left after paying taxes and receiving transfer payments. It is what they actually divide between consumption and saving (Unit 3).

Gross vs.
Net Investment: Gross investment I is all new capital purchased.
Net investment = I − depreciation is the change in the capital stock.
An economy with I below depreciation is shrinking its productive capacity.

Alternative Well-Being Measures: Purchasing-Power-Parity (PPP) adjusted GDP per capita corrects for price levels across countries. The Human Development Index (HDI) combines income with life expectancy and schooling. GDP also excludes the value of the depletion of natural resources, which "green" accounts try to subtract.
