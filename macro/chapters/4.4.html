UNIT 4 — THE FINANCIAL SECTOR

Monetary Policy & the Fed

Practice these problems →

The Federal Reserve

The Federal Reserve ("the Fed") is the US central bank. Its structure:

  • Board of Governors: 7 members in Washington, appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate to 14-year terms (which insulates them from politics).
  • 12 Regional Federal Reserve Banks, which supervise banks in their districts and provide research.
  • Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC): the group that sets monetary policy. It has 12 voting members: the 7 governors, the president of the New York Fed, and 4 other Reserve Bank presidents who rotate. It meets about eight times a year.

The Fed's dual mandate from Congress is maximum employment and stable prices (it defines this as 2% inflation). Its other jobs: supervise banks, act as a lender of last resort, and keep the payments system running.

Monetary Policy is the Fed's management of the money supply and interest rates to influence real GDP, unemployment, and inflation.

The Tools of Monetary Policy

Tool What the Fed Does Effect on Bank Reserves and the Money Supply
Open market operations (OMO) Buys government securities Reserves up, money supply up, i down
Sells government securities Reserves down, money supply down, i up
Discount rate (the rate on Fed loans to banks) Lowers it Banks borrow more, reserves up, money supply up, i down
Raises it Reserves down, money supply down, i up
Required reserve ratio Lowers it Excess reserves up, multiplier up, money supply up, i down
Raises it Excess reserves down, money supply down, i up

Open Market Operations are the Fed's main tool in the textbook (and AP) model: they are flexible and can be done in any size at any time. The reserve ratio is rarely changed because it disrupts banks' planning. In practice today, with ample reserves, the Fed moves the federal funds rate mainly by setting the IORB (see below); open market operations are used mostly to manage the quantity of reserves, and the discount rate is set just above the target range as a ceiling.

The Federal Funds Rate is the interest rate banks charge each other on overnight loans of reserves. The FOMC sets a target for it. When reserves are scarce and the Fed buys bonds, reserves become more plentiful, so banks lend them to each other at a lower rate; when it sells bonds, the rate rises (the next section explains what changes when reserves are ample). The federal funds rate is not the same as the discount rate, which is what the Fed itself charges banks.

The Reserve Market: Ample vs. Scarce Reserves

The federal funds rate is determined in the market for reserves. Reserves are the balances banks hold at the Fed. This graph has the quantity of reserves on the horizontal axis and the federal funds rate on the vertical axis.

  • Supply of Reserves: vertical, because the Fed controls it (through open market operations).
  • Demand for Reserves: banks hold reserves to meet requirements and to settle payments. Demand slopes down because a lower rate makes holding reserves cheaper. It is flat at two levels. At the top it is flat at the discount rate, because no bank will pay more than that to borrow reserves when it can borrow from the Fed at the discount rate. At the bottom it is flat at the interest rate on reserve balances (IORB), because no bank will lend reserves to another bank for less than the Fed pays it on those reserves.

The federal funds rate is where supply crosses demand, and what the Fed does depends on which part of demand that is.

Ample Reserves (Today's System): Banks hold so many reserves that supply crosses the flat part of demand. The federal funds rate then sits at the IORB, and ordinary open market operations change the quantity of reserves but not the rate. The Fed moves the rate by changing the IORB, which shifts the flat part of demand up or down.

Ample Reserves: Federal Funds Rate ≈ IORB

Scarce (Limited) Reserves (The Older System): Reserves are so limited that supply crosses the downward-sloping part of demand. Open market operations now move the rate: buying bonds shifts supply right and lowers the federal funds rate, and selling bonds shifts supply left and raises it.

When Were Reserves Scarce, and Why Did the Fed Move to an Ample System?

Before 2008 reserves were scarce. Banks held only about $45 billion in reserves, and the Fed paid them no interest on those reserves. Small open market operations, sometimes done daily, shifted the supply of reserves along the sloping part of demand and steered the federal funds rate to the target.

The 2008 crisis changed this. To fight the recession the Fed bought huge amounts of bonds (QE), which created trillions of dollars of reserves (over $2 trillion by the mid-2010s). Supply moved far to the right, onto the flat part of demand, so open market operations could no longer move the rate. In October 2008 the Fed also began paying interest on reserves, giving it a new tool: raising or lowering the IORB moves the rate even when reserves are plentiful.

It stayed that way. In January 2019 the FOMC announced that it would keep operating with ample reserves and control the federal funds rate with its administered rates (the IORB and the ON RRP rate) rather than with the quantity of reserves.

Drawing It Yourself: draw the demand curve as a downward slope with a flat piece at the top and a flat piece at the bottom, and a vertical supply line. In the ample case draw the supply line on the bottom flat part. In the scarce case draw it on the sloping part.

Fed Action Ample Reserves Scarce Reserves
Buy bonds (supply right) Quantity of reserves ↑, federal funds rate unchanged Reserves ↑, rate ↓
Sell bonds (supply left) Reserves ↓, rate unchanged (as long as supply stays on the flat part) Reserves ↓, rate ↑
Raise IORB (flat part of demand shifts up) Rate ↑ (rarely used)
Lower IORB (flat part of demand shifts down) Rate ↓ (rarely used)
Raise or lower the discount rate Rate unchanged, since the discount rate is only a ceiling Rate unchanged unless supply is on the top flat part

After the rate moves, the rest of the chain is the same as before: a lower federal funds rate lowers other interest rates, which raises investment and AD, and a higher rate does the reverse.

Which Graph to Draw: if the question talks about the money supply and the nominal interest rate, use the money market graph (Chapter 4.3). If it talks about reserves, the federal funds rate or the IORB, use the reserve market graph.

Expansionary and Contractionary Policy

  • Expansionary (Easy) Monetary Policy: raise the money supply to lower interest rates. Used to fight a recession (recessionary gap, high unemployment).
  • Contractionary (Tight) Monetary Policy: reduce the money supply to raise interest rates. Used to fight inflation (inflationary gap).

How Monetary Policy Affects the Economy

Policy works through the interest rate and investment:

Expansionary: ↑ MS ⟹ ↓ i ⟹ ↑ I ⟹ ↑ AD ⟹ ↑ Y, ↑ PL
Contractionary: ↓ MS ⟹ ↑ i ⟹ ↓ I ⟹ ↓ AD ⟹ ↓ Y, ↓ PL

Expansionary policy in a recession: the Fed buys bonds, MS shifts right, i falls, investment rises, and AD shifts right, closing the recessionary gap.

Contractionary policy against inflation: the Fed sells bonds, MS shifts left, i rises, investment falls, and AD shifts left, closing the inflationary gap.

Other channels: lower interest rates also raise spending on cars and homes, raise stock and house prices (a wealth effect), and (in an open economy) can weaken the dollar, raising net exports. Monetary policy is more powerful the more sensitive investment is to interest rates.

Sizing an Open Market Operation

When banks are fully loaned up, an OMO of size X changes reserves by X and the money supply by up to X × (1 ÷ rr).

Δ M = OMO × 1rr ⟹
OMO Needed = Δ M × rr
Target: raise M by $200 million, rr = 25%:
OMO = 200 × 0.25 = $50 million (buy bonds)

Check: 50 × (1 ÷ 0.25) = 50 × 4 = 200. This is the maximum effect: if banks hold excess reserves or the public holds more currency, the actual change in M is smaller.

Limits and Lags

  • Lags: the Fed must recognize the problem, decide, and then wait for investment to respond.
  • Weak Response: if firms are pessimistic, even low interest rates do not raise investment, and banks may hold excess reserves instead of lending.
  • Trade-Offs: against a supply shock that raises inflation and unemployment together, expansionary policy (which raises AD) fights unemployment but worsens inflation.
  • Zero Lower Bound: the federal funds rate cannot fall much below zero (see Go Deeper below).
Go Deeper: College-Level

Why Reserves Became Ample: After 2008 the Fed's large bond purchases (QE, below) created trillions of dollars of reserves, which moved the supply of reserves onto the flat part of demand. The Fed also offers the overnight reverse repo (ON RRP) facility, which pays a floor rate to other institutions such as money market funds. The money multiplier calculations in this chapter remain standard exam material, but in practice the interest-rate tools matter most.

Quantitative Easing and Forward Guidance: At the zero lower bound the Fed cannot cut the policy rate further, so after 2008 and 2020 it turned to other tools. In Quantitative Easing (QE) it bought long-term Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities to push down long-term interest rates. It also promised to keep rates low for a long time. This is called forward guidance: by telling households and firms what it will do in the future, the Fed lowers their expectations of future interest rates, which lowers long-term rates and encourages borrowing and spending today.

Central Bank Independence: because politicians prefer low interest rates before elections, independence helps the Fed credibly commit to low inflation.

The IS-LM Model (Output and the Interest Rate Together): AD-AS tells us what happens to the price level and output, but not exactly how monetary and fiscal policy move AD. The IS-LM model shows the mechanism. It holds the price level fixed and puts real GDP (Y) on the horizontal axis and the interest rate (r) on the vertical axis. It has two curves, one for the goods market and one for the money market, and they meet at a single point where both markets are in equilibrium.

The IS Curve (Goods Market): The IS curve shows every combination of r and Y at which planned spending equals output (the Keynesian cross in Chapter 3.4). It slopes down. A higher interest rate makes borrowing more expensive, so firms invest less (and households buy fewer houses and cars). Planned spending falls, and the multiplier amplifies the fall in output. A lower r does the reverse.

The LM Curve (Money Market): The LM curve shows every combination of r and Y at which money demand equals the money supply set by the Fed (the money market of Chapter 4.3). It slopes up. Higher income means more transactions, so people want to hold more money. With the money supply fixed, the interest rate has to rise to bring money demand back down to the supply.

Equilibrium: The economy is at the point where IS and LM cross, which gives the equilibrium output Y* and interest rate r*.

Drawing It Yourself: put Y on the horizontal axis and r on the vertical axis. Draw IS as a downward-sloping line and LM as an upward-sloping line, and label the crossing point E.

What Shifts the Curves: Anything that changes spending at a given interest rate shifts IS. Anything that changes the money supply (or money demand) at a given interest rate shifts LM. A change in r itself is a movement along a curve.

Event Curve That Shifts Output (Y) Interest Rate (r)
Expansionary fiscal policy (↑G or ↓T) IS right ↑ ↑
Contractionary fiscal policy (↓G or ↑T) IS left ↓ ↓
Expansionary monetary policy (Fed buys bonds) LM right ↑ ↓
Contractionary monetary policy (Fed sells bonds) LM left ↓ ↑
Business or consumer confidence rises IS right ↑ ↑

Fiscal Policy and Crowding Out: A rise in government purchases shifts IS right, which raises output. But higher income raises money demand, so the interest rate rises (E moves up along LM), and the higher rate reduces private investment. This is crowding out, the same idea as in the loanable funds market (Chapter 5.3). Output therefore rises by less than the simple multiplier predicts.

Monetary Policy: When the Fed buys bonds, the money supply rises and LM shifts right. At the old interest rate people now hold more money than they want, so they use it to buy bonds and lend, which pushes r down. The lower r raises investment, and the multiplier raises output (E moves down along IS). This is the chain you learned earlier: ↑MS ⇒ ↓r ⇒ ↑I ⇒ ↑AD.

Connection to AD-AS: A higher price level lowers the real money supply (M/P), so LM shifts left, r rises and output falls. This is the interest-rate effect from Chapter 3.1 and is why AD slopes down. A change in the price level is a movement along AD, while a policy that shifts IS or LM (at a given price level) shifts AD.

Liquidity Trap: At the zero lower bound the interest rate cannot fall further, so extra money does not push r down. The LM curve is flat there and increasing the money supply does not shift the equilibrium, which is why the Fed turned to QE and forward guidance after 2008.
