UNIT 5 — LONG-RUN CONSEQUENCES OF STABILIZATION POLICIES

The Phillips Curve

Practice these problems →

The Big Idea

The Phillips Curve shows the relationship between the inflation rate and the unemployment rate. In the short run the two move in opposite directions: policy that pushes unemployment down tends to push inflation up. In the long run there is no trade-off: unemployment returns to its natural rate whatever the inflation rate is. The model is AD-AS translated into the language of rates (inflation instead of the price level, unemployment instead of real GDP).

The Short-Run Phillips Curve (SRPC)

The SRPC slopes downward: lower unemployment goes with higher inflation. The logic comes straight from AD-AS. When output rises above potential, unemployment falls (firms hire more) and the price level rises faster (inflation). The economy is always somewhere on an SRPC.

The Long-Run Phillips Curve (LRPC)

The LRPC is a vertical line at the natural rate of unemployment (un). It is the Phillips-curve twin of the vertical LRAS: in the long run output returns to potential, so unemployment returns to un no matter what the inflation rate is.

Long-Run Equilibrium is where the SRPC crosses the LRPC. At that point actual inflation equals expected inflation and cyclical unemployment is zero.

Cyclical Unemployment = u - un
  • Left of the LRPC (u < un): output is above potential, an inflationary gap.
  • Right of the LRPC (u > un): output is below potential, a recessionary gap.

Demand Shocks: Movement Along the SRPC

A change in aggregate demand moves the economy along the SRPC. An AD increase raises output and the price level, so unemployment falls and inflation rises (up and to the left). An AD decrease does the opposite (down and to the right).

In the graph, B is the result of an AD increase from A, and C the result of an AD decrease.

Supply Shocks: Shifts of the SRPC

A change in short-run aggregate supply shifts the SRPC.

  • Adverse Supply Shock (SRAS shifts left, e.g. an oil-price spike): the SRPC shifts up (and right). Unemployment and inflation rise together, which is stagflation.
  • Favorable Supply Shock (SRAS shifts right, e.g. a productivity boom): the SRPC shifts down. Unemployment and inflation can fall together.

Note: this graph shows an adverse supply shock (the SRPC shifts up). A favorable supply shock would shift it down.

Expectations: Why the SRPC Shifts and the LRPC Does Not

The SRPC is drawn for a given level of expected inflation. Suppose policymakers hold unemployment below un. Inflation runs above what people expected, so wage contracts and price expectations are revised upward, and the SRPC shifts up. The economy ends at un again, but with higher inflation. This is the long-run self-correction of AD-AS in Phillips-curve form.

The reverse also holds. If unemployment is above un, inflation falls below expectations, expectations fall, and the SRPC shifts down until the economy returns to un at lower inflation. If a central bank is credible, expectations can fall without a long recession.

Changes in the Natural Rate: Shifts of the LRPC

Anything that changes the natural rate shifts the LRPC, just as it shifts LRAS in the opposite direction.

  • un rises (longer unemployment benefits, more skills mismatch, a higher minimum wage or stronger union power that keeps wages above the market-clearing level): LRPC shifts right.
  • un falls (better job-matching, job training, weaker barriers to hiring): LRPC shifts left.

Demographics matter too: groups such as young workers tend to have higher unemployment, so a changing age mix of the labor force can move the natural rate. Economists debate how much a minimum wage raises unemployment, so treat it as a possible factor, not a certain one.

Summary: AD-AS versus the Phillips Curve

Event AD-AS Result Phillips Curve Result
AD Increases Y up, price level up, u down Move up and left along SRPC
AD Decreases Y down, price level down, u up Move down and right along SRPC
SRAS Decreases (Adverse) Y down, price level up, u up SRPC shifts up
SRAS Increases (Favorable) Y up, price level down, u down SRPC shifts down
Expected Inflation Rises SRAS shifts left SRPC shifts up
Natural Rate Rises LRAS shifts left LRPC shifts right
Go Deeper: College-Level

The Expectations-Augmented Phillips Curve: Inflation depends on expected inflation, the gap between unemployment and its natural rate, and supply shocks (ν):

π = πe - β(u - un) + ν

Here β > 0 measures how sharply inflation responds to unemployment (the slope of the SRPC). With adaptive expectations (πe equals last year's inflation), holding u below un makes inflation rise by β(un − u) every year. Under rational expectations (Unit 5.2) people anticipate announced, credible policy, so disinflation can be nearly costless.

with πe = 3, β = 0.5, un = 5, u = 4:
π = 3 - 0.5(4 - 5) = 3.5

Okun's Law: An approximate rule of thumb says that each percentage point of cyclical unemployment (unemployment above the natural rate) costs about 2 percent of output.

Sacrifice Ratio: The cost of disinflation is measured by the percent of a year's output lost per percentage point of inflation reduced:

Sacrifice Ratio = Cumulative Output Loss (% of One Year's GDP)Reduction in Inflation (Percentage Points)

History: The 1970s combined oil shocks with rising inflation expectations (stagflation), which shattered the idea of a stable trade-off. Volcker's disinflation (1979-82) caused a deep recession but anchored expectations. Hysteresis is the idea that a long recession can itself raise un (as skills erode), shifting the LRPC right. On the bright side, the late 1990s showed the favorable case: a productivity boom shifted the SRPC down, giving low unemployment and low inflation together.
