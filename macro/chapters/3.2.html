Aggregate Supply

Two Aggregate Supply Curves

Aggregate Supply shows the real GDP that firms produce at each price level. Economists separate two time horizons because input prices (especially wages) adjust slowly but eventually do adjust.

Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS) Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS)
Shape Upward sloping Vertical at potential output
Why Some input prices (wages, contracts) are sticky, so higher output prices raise profit per unit Wages and all prices have fully adjusted; output depends only on real resources and technology
Output Can be above or below potential Equals potential output (Yf), the full-employment level
Unemployment Can be above or below the natural rate At the natural rate (frictional + structural only)

Potential output (Yf) is the real GDP the economy produces when all resources are fully employed, and unemployment equals the natural rate. The gap between actual and potential output is the output gap:

Output Gap (%) = Y - YfYf × 100
Y = 9,400,  Yf = 10,000 ⟹  9,400 - 10,00010,000 × 100 = -6%  (below potential)

Why SRAS Slopes Upward

When the price level rises and nominal wages are fixed by contracts or slow to change, firms' real costs fall and profits per unit rise, so they hire more labor and produce more. Three common explanations: sticky nominal wages (the main one), sticky prices (menu costs: some firms are slow to reprice), and misperceptions (firms mistake a general price rise for a rise in their own product's price). The real wage shows the stickiness:

Real Wage = WP × 100
(P = Price Index, Base = 100)
W = $22,  P = 110 ⟹  Real Wage = 22110 × 100 = $20 (base-year dollars)

A higher price level with a fixed nominal wage means a lower real wage, so firms hire more and produce more. A change in the price level is a movement along SRAS; anything else shifts it.

What Shifts SRAS

SRAS shifts when the cost of producing at a given price level changes.

Shifts SRAS Right (Costs Fall) Shifts SRAS Left (Costs Rise)
Lower nominal wages or lower input prices (oil, materials) Higher wages or input prices
Higher productivity or better technology Lower productivity
Lower expected inflation (wages set lower) Higher expected inflation (wages set higher)
Lower business taxes, new subsidies, less regulation Higher business taxes, more regulation
Favorable supply shock (bumper harvest, falling oil prices) Adverse supply shock (oil embargo, natural disaster)

A Supply Shock is a sudden event that changes costs across many industries. An Adverse shock shifts SRAS left and causes stagflation: output falls (higher unemployment) and the price level rises. Inflation that starts with higher costs is called cost-push inflation; inflation that starts with a rightward shift in AD is demand-pull inflation (3.3).

An adverse supply shock moves the economy from E1 to E2: a higher price level and lower real GDP, shown as a gap below Yf.

Drawing It Yourself: draw AD, SRAS and a vertical LRAS crossing at one point (E1). For a cost increase, draw a second SRAS to the left and mark the new equilibrium where AD crosses it, at a higher price level and lower output. For a cost decrease, shift SRAS to the right.

What Shifts LRAS

LRAS (and the PPC) shift when the economy's productive capacity changes:

  • More Resources: labor force growth (including immigration), more capital (physical or human).
  • Better Technology or more efficient institutions.
  • Changes in the Natural Rate: a lower natural rate of unemployment raises potential output.

Increases in capacity shift LRAS right (economic growth). Destroyed capital, shrinking labor force or lower productivity shift it left.

SRAS vs. LRAS Shifts

Event SRAS LRAS
Higher nominal wages (no change in productivity) Left No change
Higher price of oil (temporary) Left No change
Technological advance Right Right
More workers or capital Right Right
Higher expected inflation Left No change
Natural disaster destroys factories Left Left

Rule of Thumb: things that change costs shift SRAS only; things that change capacity (resources, technology) shift both. Changes in AD never shift either supply curve.

Go Deeper: College-Level

The Price-Surprise Supply Equation: The short-run supply relationship can be written as a price-surprise equation,

Y = Yf + b (P - Pe)

where b > 0 and Pe is the price level people expected. Output exceeds potential only when the actual price level is above the expected one. In the long run P adjusts to Pe, the surprise vanishes, and output returns to Yf.

Sticky Wages vs. Sticky Prices vs. Imperfect Information: Three classic models give an upward-sloping short-run curve: the sticky-wage model (wages set in advance on an expected price level), the sticky-price model (monopolistically competitive firms with menu costs), and the imperfect-information model (Lucas). They agree on the key policy implication that systematic, anticipated demand changes should move prices more than output once expectations catch up.

Growth Accounting for LRAS: Potential output depends on inputs and productivity through a production function such as Y = A × F(K, L), where A is total factor productivity.
