UNIT 5 — LONG-RUN CONSEQUENCES OF STABILIZATION POLICIES

Deficits, Debt & Crowding Out

Practice these problems →

Deficits versus Debt

The government's budget balance compares tax revenue (T) with total spending (G), where G includes purchases, transfer payments and interest on the debt.

Budget Balance = T - G
  • Budget Deficit: T < G (a negative balance) in a given year.
  • Budget Surplus: T > G.
  • Balanced Budget: T = G.

The National Debt is the total accumulated amount the government owes from past borrowing. The deficit is a flow (per year), and the debt is a stock (at a point in time). A deficit adds to the debt, and a surplus reduces it.

DebtEnd of Year = DebtStart of Year + Deficit
Start Debt = 8,000, Deficit = 300 ⟹  End Debt = 8,300

Primary Balance is the budget balance excluding interest on the debt. A government can run a primary surplus and still have an overall deficit if interest payments are large.

Cyclical vs. Structural Deficits: In a recession, tax revenue falls and transfers rise automatically (automatic stabilizers), creating a cyclical deficit. The Structural Deficit is what the budget balance would be at full employment. A deficit that persists in good times is structural.

The Debt-to-GDP Ratio

The size of the debt is best judged relative to the economy's ability to pay, so economists use the debt-to-GDP ratio.

Debt-to-GDP Ratio = National DebtNominal GDP × 100

The ratio rises when debt grows faster than nominal GDP, and falls when nominal GDP grows faster than debt. A government can have a rising debt in dollars but a falling ratio if the economy is growing quickly.

How the Government Finances a Deficit

  1. Borrowing from the Public: selling Treasury bonds to households, firms, banks and foreign investors. This is the main source and is what causes crowding out.
  2. Borrowing from the Fed / creating money: the central bank buys the bonds with newly created money (monetizing the debt), which expands the money supply and risks inflation.
  3. Raising Taxes or Cutting Spending in the future to repay.

The Burden of the Debt

  • Interest Payments: a larger debt means more of the budget goes to interest, leaving less for other uses (an opportunity cost).
  • Crowding Out of private investment, which slows capital accumulation and long-run growth.
  • Foreign Ownership: interest paid to foreigners is a claim on future domestic output.
  • Future Taxes or Reduced Spending to service or repay the debt.

Crowding Out in the Loanable Funds Market

In the loanable funds market the real interest rate (r) is the price of borrowing. Savers supply funds and borrowers demand them (firms for investment, and the government for its deficit).

When the government runs a deficit and borrows, the demand for loanable funds increases. The real interest rate rises. Higher rates reduce interest-sensitive private spending (investment and some consumption). This is crowding out.

Long-Run Consequences

Lower private investment means a slower accumulation of physical capital, so the economy's productive capacity (LRAS and the PPC) grows more slowly. Crowding out thus has a short-run effect (higher r, lower investment, which partly offsets the fiscal stimulus to AD) and a long-run effect (lower growth).

With less investment, the rightward shift of LRAS (LRAS1 to LRAS2) is smaller than it would otherwise have been.

Crowding in: the opposite of crowding out, where government spending raises private investment. For example, spending on infrastructure, education or research can raise the profitability of private projects or attract investment that would otherwise be too risky. It is more likely in a recession, when firms have unused capacity, and economists debate how common it is.

The Reverse of Crowding Out: a government surplus (or paying down debt) reduces demand for loanable funds, lowers r, and raises private investment.

National Saving

In a closed economy, national saving (S) equals investment (I). It is the sum of private and public saving:

S = (Y - T - C) + (T - G) = Y - C - G

A budget deficit makes public saving (T − G) negative, so national saving falls. This is equivalent to a leftward shift of loanable funds supply: the real rate rises and investment falls.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Ricardian Equivalence: Forward-looking households may recognize that a deficit-financed tax cut today means higher taxes tomorrow, so they save the tax cut. Private saving rises by as much as public saving falls, national saving is unchanged, and there is no crowding out. It helps explain why temporary tax rebates have low multipliers. Most economists think this holds only partially (liquidity-constrained households, finite lives, uncertainty).

Is Crowding Out Always Complete? If the economy has slack and interest rates are at the zero lower bound, deficits may not raise r at all. Open economies also borrow from abroad, which dampens the rise in r but creates a trade deficit (the "twin deficits", Unit 6).
