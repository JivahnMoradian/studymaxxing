UNIT 3 — NATIONAL INCOME & PRICE DETERMINATION

AD-AS Equilibrium & Self-Correction

Practice these problems →

Equilibrium in the AD-AS Model

The Short-Run Equilibrium is where AD crosses SRAS. It sets the economy's price level and real GDP. The Long-Run Equilibrium is where AD, SRAS and LRAS all cross at one point: output equals potential output (Yf) and unemployment equals the natural rate.

If the short-run equilibrium is not on LRAS, the economy has a gap:

Recessionary Gap Inflationary Gap
Real GDP Y < Yf Y > Yf
Unemployment Above the natural rate Below the natural rate
Resource Use Idle resources, falling pressure on wages Overheated, rising pressure on wages
Graph Short-run equilibrium to the left of LRAS Short-run equilibrium to the right of LRAS
Output Gap = Y - Yf

Effects of Shifts from Long-Run Equilibrium

Start from long-run equilibrium. Each curve shifts, one at a time, in the short run:

Shift Price Level Real GDP Unemployment
AD right (more spending) Up Up Down
AD left Down Down Up
SRAS right (lower costs) Down Up Down
SRAS left (higher costs, "stagflation") Up Down Up

Real GDP and unemployment move in opposite directions (Okun's law, covered in 2.3). The rise in the price level from an AD shift right is demand-pull inflation, and from an SRAS shift left it is cost-push inflation. If two curves shift at once, one variable is determinate and the other ambiguous:

Shifts Price Level Real GDP
AD right and SRAS right ? Up
AD left and SRAS left ? Down
AD right and SRAS left Up ?
AD left and SRAS right Down ?

Drawing It Yourself: (1) draw AD, SRAS and LRAS meeting at one point (E1); (2) decide which curve shifts and draw the new dashed curve; (3) mark the new equilibrium E2 where AD and SRAS cross; (4) read off the new price level (vertical axis) and real GDP (horizontal axis), and compare real GDP with Yf to label the gap.

Long-Run Self-Correction

Gaps do not last forever because nominal wages and input prices are flexible in the long run. The adjustment works through the labor market:

  • Recessionary Gap (high unemployment): wages and input prices fall, so SRAS shifts right until it intersects AD on LRAS. Price level falls, output returns to Yf, unemployment returns to the natural rate.
  • Inflationary Gap (low unemployment): workers bargain for higher wages, so costs rise and SRAS shifts left until it intersects AD on LRAS. Price level rises, output returns to Yf.

Key results after self-correction:

  • Output returns to Yf; unemployment returns to the natural rate. LRAS never moved.
  • A Demand shock has only a temporary effect on real GDP, but a permanent effect on the price level (money neutrality: demand changes the price level in the long run, not real output).
  • Self-correction can be slow, especially when wages are sticky downward. This is the argument for stabilization policy.

Policy vs. Self-Correction

Instead of waiting, the government or central bank can shift AD:

  • Recessionary Gap → Expansionary policy (more G, lower taxes, lower interest rates) shifts AD right to Yf.
  • Inflationary Gap → Contractionary policy shifts AD left to Yf.

Go Deeper: College-Level

A Numeric AD-AS Model: With linear curves, AD: Y = a − bP and SRAS: Y = c + dP, set them equal to solve for the equilibrium.
For example, AD: Y = 3,400 − 10P and SRAS: Y = 2,000 + 4P with Yf = 2,500.

3,400 - 10P = 2,000 + 4P ⟹  1,400 = 14P ⟹  P = 100
Y = 2,400
Output Gap = 2,400 - 2,500 = -100 ⟹  recessionary gap of -1002,500 × 100 = -4%

Self-correction: SRAS shifts right until AD hits Yf, so the long-run price level solves 2,500 = 3,400 − 10P, giving P = 90. Policy: on the current SRAS, Yf requires 2,500 = 2,000 + 4P, so P = 125.
AD must pass through (2,500; 125): at P = 125 today's AD gives 3,400 − 1,250 = 2,150, so AD must shift right by 2,500 − 2,150 = 350.
Self-correction ends at P = 90; policy ends at P = 125.

Adjustment speed and the wage Phillips relation: Wages respond to unemployment relative to the natural rate. If actual unemployment u exceeds un, wage inflation falls; if it is below un, wage inflation rises. Self-correction is therefore not instantaneous: output returns to potential as expected inflation adjusts. In Unit 5 this becomes the Phillips curve.

Hysteresis: As in Chapter 2.3, a deep recession can lower potential output itself, so self-correction is incomplete. This strengthens the case for stimulus in deep recessions.

Classical vs. Keynesian Views: Classical economists emphasize rapid wage-price flexibility (so gaps are short and policy unnecessary). Keynesians emphasize stickiness (so gaps are long and policy is valuable).

Policy Overshoot: Because policy works with lags, stimulus enacted in a recession can arrive after self-correction has already begun, pushing the economy into an inflationary gap. Policymakers therefore weigh the cost of waiting against the cost of mistiming.
