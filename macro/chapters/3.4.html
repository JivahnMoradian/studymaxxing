UNIT 3 — NATIONAL INCOME & PRICE DETERMINATION

Consumption, Saving & the Multiplier

Practice these problems →

Disposable Income, Consumption and Saving

Disposable Income (Yd) is the income households have left to spend or save after paying taxes (and counting transfer payments). Here Y is total income (GDP), T is taxes, TR is government transfer payments, C is consumption, and S is saving:

Yd = Y - T + TR
Yd = C + S

Households either consume (C) or save (S) every dollar of disposable income. This identity makes the marginal propensities add up to one:

MPC = Δ CΔ Yd
MPS = Δ SΔ Yd
MPC + MPS = 1
  • MPC (marginal propensity to consume): the fraction of each additional dollar of disposable income that is spent.
  • MPS (marginal propensity to save): the fraction saved. Both lie between 0 and 1.
  • APC = C ÷ Yd and APS = S ÷ Yd are average propensities (APC + APS = 1). They differ from the marginal ones.
Yd rises from 400 to 500 and C rises from 380 to 450:
MPC = 450 - 380500 - 400 = 0.7
MPS = 1 - 0.7 = 0.3

The Spending Multiplier

A change in spending (ΔG, ΔI, ΔC or ΔNX) creates income for someone, who spends the MPC share of it, creating income for someone else, and so on. The total change in real GDP is a multiple of the initial change.

Spending Multiplier = 11 - MPC = 1MPS
Δ Y = Multiplier × Δ Spending
MPC = 0.8 ⟹  Multiplier = 11 - 0.8 = 5
Δ G = 20 ⟹  Δ Y = 5 × 20 = 100

The Horizontal Shift of AD equals this ΔY (at a given price level). A larger MPC means a larger multiplier: more of each round is re-spent.

The Multiplier Process (Rounds of Spending)

Δ G = 100,  MPC = 0.8:
100 + 80 + 64 + 51.2 + ... = 1001 - 0.8 = 500

Each round is MPC times the previous round, so the rounds shrink and sum to a finite total. The cumulative effect after n rounds is (1 − MPCn) of the full effect, where MPCn means MPC multiplied by itself n times. With MPC = 0.8, six rounds deliver 1 − 0.86 ≈ 0.74, or about 74%, of the full 500.

Tax and Transfer Multipliers

A tax change works through disposable income: part of any tax change is absorbed by saving (the MPS share), so only the MPC share of the change shows up in first-round spending. That makes the tax multiplier smaller in magnitude than the spending multiplier, and negative:

Tax Multiplier = -MPC1 - MPC = -MPCMPS
Transfer Multiplier = MPC1 - MPC
MPC = 0.75 ⟹  Tax Multiplier = -0.750.25 = -3
Δ T = +20 ⟹  Δ Y = -3 × 20 = -60

A Tax Cut of ΔT = −20 raises GDP by 60; a transfer (such as unemployment benefits) of 20 does the same. The same dollar amount of government purchases raises GDP by more (4 × 20 = 80) because the entire amount is spent in round one.

The Balanced-Budget Multiplier

Raise G and T by the same amount, ΔG = ΔT. The spending multiplier is larger than the tax multiplier (in absolute value) by exactly 1, so GDP rises by exactly ΔG:

Δ Y = 11 - MPC Δ G - MPC1 - MPC Δ T
= 1 - MPC1 - MPC Δ G = Δ G
Balanced-Budget Multiplier = 1

Using Multipliers to Close a Gap

To close a recessionary gap or inflationary gap, you need the AD shift to equal the gap (in real GDP):

Δ G = GapSpending Multiplier
Δ T = GapTax Multiplier
MPC = 0.8,  Yf - Y = 200:
Δ G = 2005 = +40 or
Δ T = 200-4 = -50

Tax cuts need to be larger than spending increases to deliver the same boost. Multipliers in practice are smaller than these formulas because of income taxes, imports and higher prices (which leak spending away), and because of crowding out (3.5).

The Consumption and Saving Functions

A simple linear consumption function is:

C = a + MPC × Yd

where a is autonomous consumption (spending that does not depend on income) and MPC is the slope.
Since S = Yd − C, the saving function is S = −a + MPS × Yd.

C = 200 + 0.75Yd,  Yd = 1,000 ⟹  C = 200 + 750 = 950
S = 1,000 - 950 = 50

Where the consumption function crosses the 45° line (C = Yd) is the break-even income where saving is zero.
Below it households dissave (S < 0: they spend more than their income by drawing down savings or borrowing).

Break-Even Yd = a1 - MPC = aMPS

Moving along the function happens when disposable income changes. The whole function shifts up when wealth, confidence, or expected future income rises (a higher), and down when they fall. Those are the same factors that shift AD through C.

Drawing It Yourself: put Yd on the horizontal axis and C on the vertical. Draw a 45° dashed line, then the consumption line with a positive intercept (a) and slope less than 1. Mark where the two cross. For the saving function, draw a line that crosses the horizontal axis at the break-even income and starts at −a on the vertical axis.

Permanent Income and Life-Cycle Theories

Friedman's permanent-income hypothesis and Modigliani's life-cycle theory say households smooth consumption over time. The MPC out of a temporary change in income (a one-time rebate) is therefore small, so temporary tax cuts have smaller multipliers than permanent ones. This is a key reason empirical multipliers for rebates are below 1.

Average vs. Marginal Propensities

Because the consumption function has a positive intercept, APC > MPC and APC falls as income rises. Poorer households have higher APC.

The Keynesian Cross (Why Output Adjusts to Spending)

The multiplier can be shown in a single graph.
Put real GDP (Y) on the horizontal axis and planned aggregate expenditure (AE = C + I + G + NX, the total spending households, firms, the government and foreigners plan to do) on the vertical axis.
AE slopes up because spending rises with income, but with a slope smaller than 1 (the MPC, if we ignore taxes and imports), because people spend only part of each extra dollar.
The 45° line marks every point where spending equals output (AE = Y).
The economy is in equilibrium where AE crosses the 45° line.

Drawing It Yourself: draw a 45° line from the origin, then a flatter upward-sloping AE line that starts above zero (even with no income, people spend something) and crosses the 45° line at E.

Unplanned Inventories: Why is Y* the equilibrium?
If output is above planned spending (Y >
AE), firms cannot sell everything they produce and unsold goods pile up as unplanned inventories.
Firms respond by cutting production, so Y falls.
If output is below planned spending (Y <
AE), inventories run down below plan, so firms raise production and Y rises.
Output keeps adjusting until Y = AE.

Unplanned Inventory Change = Y - AE

The Multiplier in the Cross: An increase in autonomous spending, such as higher G, shifts the whole AE line up by ΔG. At the old Y* spending now exceeds output, inventories fall unexpectedly, and firms raise production. That extra output is extra income, which raises consumption, which raises spending again, so output keeps rising until AE crosses the 45° line at the new, higher equilibrium. The rise in Y is a multiple of ΔG because each round adds spending equal to the MPC times the previous round's income, the same rounds-of-spending process described earlier in this chapter.
