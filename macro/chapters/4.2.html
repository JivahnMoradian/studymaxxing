UNIT 4 — THE FINANCIAL SECTOR

Financial Assets & Time Value of Money

Practice these problems →

The Financial System in One Picture

Savers have funds they do not need now; borrowers (firms, households, governments) want to spend more than they have. The Financial System moves funds between them in two ways:

  • Financial Markets (the bond market, the stock market) where savers buy assets directly from borrowers.
  • Financial Intermediaries (banks, mutual funds, pension funds, insurance companies) that collect savings and lend or invest them. They pool risk, lower transaction costs, and provide liquidity.

A Financial Asset is a claim on future payments. A Financial Liability is the matching obligation. When you buy a bond you own an asset and the issuer owes a liability.

Bond vocabulary:

  • Face (Par) Value: the amount repaid at maturity.
  • Coupon: the fixed interest payment each year, equal to the coupon rate × face value.
  • Maturity: the date the face value is repaid. Longer maturities carry more risk and normally pay a higher rate.
  • Yield: the rate of return an investor earns on the price actually paid.

A Zero-Coupon Bond pays no coupons: it is sold at a discount and pays only its face value at maturity.

Risk, Return, and Liquidity

Every asset is judged on three things:

  • Return: the payment you expect to receive, as a percent of what you paid.
  • Risk: the uncertainty of that return (default risk, price risk).
  • Liquidity: how quickly and cheaply it converts to cash without losing value.

Higher expected return generally requires accepting more risk (the risk-return trade-off). Typical ordering from lowest to highest risk and return: Savings Deposit → Treasury Bond → Corporate Bond → Stock → Start-Up Stock.

Bond Prices and Interest Rates

Most bonds pay a fixed amount, so an existing bond's price depends on how its payments compare with what new bonds pay. That makes bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions.

Intuition: you hold a bond paying 5%. If the market interest rate rises to 8%, nobody will pay full price for your 5% bond, so its price falls. If the rate falls to 3%, your bond becomes more attractive and its price rises.

  • Market interest rate up → existing bond prices down.
  • Market interest rate down → existing bond prices up.
  • Longer Maturity → price swings more when rates change.
  • A bond downgrade (higher default risk) → buyers demand a higher yield, so the price falls.

If the bond is held to maturity the investor still receives all the promised payments. The price risk matters only if it is sold early.

Drawing It Yourself: for a bond-price graph, put the interest rate on the horizontal axis and the bond price on the vertical axis. Draw a downward-sloping curve, mark two rates and read off the two prices.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Diversification

Spreading wealth across many assets reduces risk. Total risk has two parts:

  • Firm-Specific (Idiosyncratic) Risk affects one company (a CEO scandal, a factory fire). Diversification eliminates it, because bad luck at one firm is offset by good luck at another.
  • Market (Systematic) Risk affects all companies (a recession, higher interest rates). Diversification cannot remove it.

A mutual fund or index fund lets small savers hold hundreds of stocks at low cost. Municipal bonds pay less than comparable bonds because their interest is exempt from federal tax.

Time Value of Money

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow because today's dollar can earn interest. Interest on interest is compounding, causing exponential growth.

Future Value: FV = PV (1 + r)n
Present Value: PV = FV(1 + r)n

where r is the interest rate per period (as a decimal) and n is the number of periods.

$100 at 7% for 20 years:
FV = 100 (1.07)20 ≈ $387

Present Value answers "what is a future payment worth today?". A higher interest rate lowers the present value of any future payment, because you need to deposit less today to reach the same amount.

PV of $1,000 in 3 years at 5%:
1,000(1.05)3 ≈ $863.84

The Rule of 70

The number of years for a quantity growing at g percent a year to double is roughly:

Years to Double ≈ 70g (In Percent)
Growth of 5% a year:
705 = 14 years to double

It works for money in the bank, a price level (inflation), or real GDP (growth). Two doublings quadruple, three give eight times, and so on.

Bond Pricing

A bond's price is the present value of its future payments, discounted at the market interest rate (the yield).

Bond Price = Coupon1 + r + Coupon(1 + r)2 + ... + Coupon + Face(1 + r)n

Example: a 2-year bond with a 5% coupon and $1,000 face value, when the market rate is 8%.

Price = 501.08 + 1,050(1.08)2
Price ≈ 46.30 + 900.21 = $946.51

The bond pays only 5% when new bonds pay 8%, so buyers will take it only at a discount (price below face value). If the market rate fell to 3%, the price would rise above face value.

Stocks

A share's value is the present value of its expected future dividends (and resale price). So stock prices rise when profit and dividend expectations rise or when interest rates fall (a lower discount rate), and fall when profits are expected to drop or interest rates rise.

Efficient Markets

The efficient markets hypothesis says prices already reflect all available information, so changes in price are unpredictable (a "random walk") and few investors can beat the market consistently after costs.

Bubbles and Stock Market Crashes

Prices also move on expectations about what others will pay, which is how speculative bubbles can occur: prices rise far above what expected dividends justify, until confidence breaks and a crash follows. Bubbles and crashes are the main evidence against strong forms of the efficient markets hypothesis.
