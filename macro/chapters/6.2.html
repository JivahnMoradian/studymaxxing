UNIT 6 — THE OPEN ECONOMY: INTERNATIONAL TRADE & FINANCE

The Foreign Exchange Market

Practice these problems →

Exchange Rates and How to Quote Them

The Exchange Rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. It can be quoted in either direction, and the two quotes are reciprocals:

Dollars per Euro = 1Euros per Dollar
e = 0.80 euros per dollar ⟹ 10.80 = 1.25 dollars per euro

In this chapter (and in the standard AP graph) the exchange rate e is measured in foreign currency per dollar (euros per dollar). To convert a foreign price into dollars, divide by e; to convert a dollar price into foreign currency, multiply by e.

€48 meal at e = 0.80 ⟹ 480.80 = 60 dollars
60-dollar item ⟹ 60 × 0.80 = €48

Appreciation and Depreciation

  • A currency appreciates when it becomes worth more in terms of another currency: one unit buys more foreign currency.
  • A currency depreciates when it becomes worth less.
  • If the dollar appreciates against the euro, then the euro depreciates against the dollar. When e (euros per dollar) rises, the dollar appreciates; when e falls, the dollar depreciates.
% Change in Value = New - OldOld × 100%

Percent changes are not symmetric: if e rises from 0.80 to 0.88 (the dollar appreciates 10%), then the euro's value in dollars falls from 1.25 to about 1.136, a drop of only about 9.1%.

The Foreign Exchange Market for Dollars

In a flexible (floating) exchange rate system, the exchange rate is set by supply and demand in the foreign exchange (forex) market.

  • Demand for Dollars comes from foreigners who want to buy US goods, services and assets. It slopes down: when the dollar is cheaper (a lower e), US goods are cheaper to foreigners, so they buy more and need more dollars.
  • Supply of Dollars comes from Americans who want to buy foreign goods, services and assets, and so must sell dollars to get foreign currency. It slopes up: when the dollar buys more euros (a higher e), foreign goods are cheaper to Americans, so they buy more and supply more dollars.
  • Equilibrium is where the quantity of dollars demanded equals the quantity supplied. Above it there is a surplus of dollars (the dollar falls); below it there is a shortage (the dollar rises).

What Shifts the Demand and Supply of Dollars

Event Demand for $ Supply of $ Dollar
Foreign tastes shift toward US goods Right No change Appreciates
Americans' tastes shift toward foreign goods No change Right Depreciates
Higher US interest rates (relative) Right Left Appreciates
Higher foreign interest rates Left Right Depreciates
Higher US inflation (relative): US goods more expensive Left Right Depreciates
Higher US income (more imports) No change Right Depreciates
Higher foreign income (more US exports) Right No change Appreciates
Expectation that the dollar will appreciate Right Left Appreciates

When both curves shift, the exchange rate change is determined, but the quantity of dollars traded is ambiguous.

Foreign demand for US goods rises: D shifts right, the dollar appreciates (e rises).

Americans buy more imports: S shifts right, the dollar depreciates (e falls).

The Two Markets Are Mirror Images

Every dollar–euro transaction appears in both the dollar market and the euro market. Anything that raises the demand for dollars raises the supply of euros (foreigners sell euros to buy dollars), so the dollar appreciates and the euro depreciates, and the two markets always agree.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Purchasing-Power Parity (PPP): The law of one price says an identical good should cost the same everywhere once converted to a common currency. For a basket with domestic price P (dollars) and foreign price P* (foreign currency), PPP implies the exchange rate:

e = P*P

If a Big Mac costs $5.00 in the US and ¥450 in Japan, PPP implies e = 90 yen per dollar.
If the actual rate is 140, the dollar is "overvalued" relative to PPP (the yen is undervalued).
Relative PPP says the percent change in e is approximately the inflation differential, so the currency of the higher-inflation country depreciates:

%Δ e ≈ π* - π

Real Exchange Rate: The nominal rate e is the price of currencies; the real exchange rate ε is the relative price of the two countries' goods:

ε = e × PP*

A higher ε means domestic goods are relatively expensive (so NX falls). Approximately, %Δε ≈ %Δe + π − π*.

Exchange Rate Regimes: Under a floating rate, the market sets e. Under a fixed (pegged) rate, the central bank buys or sells its currency and holds reserves to defend the peg. A deliberate lowering of the peg is a devaluation; a raising is a revaluation.

The Impossible Trinity: A country cannot have all three of the following at the same time: (1) a fixed exchange rate, (2) free movement of capital and (3) an independent monetary policy. To see why, suppose a country pegs its currency and lets capital move freely. If its central bank cuts interest rates to stimulate the economy, investors move their funds abroad in search of higher returns, which pushes the currency down. To defend the peg, the central bank must sell foreign reserves and buy its own currency, which shrinks the money supply and pushes interest rates back up. The attempt at an independent policy is undone.

A country therefore has to give up one of the three. Members of the euro area gave up (3), since one central bank sets policy for all of them. The US and the UK let their currencies float, giving up (1) while keeping (2) and (3). China has historically kept a tightly managed exchange rate and its own monetary policy, and used capital controls to limit capital flows, giving up (2).
