UNIT 4 — THE FINANCIAL SECTOR

The Money Market & Loanable Funds

Practice these problems →

Unit 4 uses two interest-rate markets. They answer different questions, so keep them apart:

Money Market Loanable Funds Market
What is traded Money itself (cash and checkable deposits) Saving lent to borrowers
Price on the vertical axis Nominal interest rate (i) Real interest rate (r)
Supply Money supply (set by the Fed): a vertical line Saving: upward-sloping
Demand Money demand: downward-sloping Borrowing for investment: downward-sloping
Used for Monetary policy (Fed actions) Saving, investment, deficits, crowding out

The Demand for Money

People hold money because it is liquid. There are three motives:

  • Transactions Demand: money is needed to buy things day to day. It rises with the price level and real income (more or pricier purchases).
  • Precautionary Demand: a cushion for emergencies.
  • Asset Demand: money is held as a store of value, instead of bonds or other assets.

The Opportunity Cost of holding money is the nominal interest rate i (in %): cash earns nothing, while a bond earns i. When i is high, people hold less money and put more in bonds; when i is low, they hold more. So Money Demand (MD) slopes down with the nominal interest rate.

MD Shifts Right (more money wanted at every i) when the price level rises or real GDP rises. It shifts left when the price level or real GDP falls, or when technology such as credit cards, mobile payments, and ATMs makes it easier to economize on cash.

The Supply of Money and Equilibrium

The Money Supply (MS) is controlled by the Fed, not by the interest rate, so it is a vertical line. Equilibrium is where MD crosses MS.

  • Above the equilibrium rate, people hold more money than the Fed supplies, so they use the extra money to buy bonds. This causes bond prices to rise, and i to fall.
  • Below the equilibrium rate, people want more money than exists, so they sell bonds. This causes bond prices to fall, and i to rise.

What Shifts the Equilibrium?

  • MS Right (the Fed buys bonds, lowers the reserve requirement, or lowers the discount rate) → i falls.
  • MS Left (the Fed sells bonds, raises the reserve requirement, or raises the discount rate) → i rises.
  • MD Right (higher price level or real GDP) → i rises.
  • MD Left (lower price level or real GDP, or technology such as credit cards, mobile payments, and ATMs that lets people economize on cash) → i falls.

Left: an increase in the money supply lowers i. Right: an increase in money demand raises i.

Because bond prices and interest rates move oppositely (Unit 4.2), anything that lowers i raises bond prices.

Drawing It Yourself: draw MS as a vertical line and MD as a downward-sloping curve. Label the vertical axis "Nominal Interest Rate" and the horizontal axis "Quantity of Money". Shift the curve that the event moves, then read off the new i.

The Loanable Funds Market

The Market for Loanable Funds brings together savers (supply) and borrowers (demand). The price is the real interest rate (r), the cost of borrowing after inflation.

  • Supply of Loanable Funds comes from saving: households, firms, the government (when it runs a surplus), and foreigners. A higher r rewards saving, so the curve slopes up.
  • Demand for Loanable Funds comes from borrowers who want to finance investment (and government deficits). A higher r makes projects costlier, so the curve slopes down.
Shifts Demand (D) Direction
Better business expectations or new technology; investment tax credit Right (more borrowing)
Government deficit increases Right
Lower expected profit Left
Shifts Supply (S) Direction
Households save more (thrift, higher expected future needs) Right
Government surplus (public saving) rises; foreign funds flow in Right
Households save less; tax on interest income rises Left

Left: a bigger deficit (or more investment demand) shifts D right: r rises. Right: more saving shifts S right: r falls and investment rises.

When the government borrows more, the higher r crowds out some private investment. We return to this in Unit 5.

National Saving in a Closed Economy

In a closed economy all output is spent on consumption, investment, or government purchases: Y = C + I + G. National Saving is the part of income not used for consumption or government purchases.

S = Y - C - G
S = I
(Closed Economy: Saving Funds Investment)

National saving has two parts. Private Saving is what households have left after taxes and consumption. Public Saving is the government's budget balance: positive for a surplus, negative for a deficit.

SPrivate = Y - T - C
SPublic = T - G
S = SPrivate + SPublic
Y = 18, C = 12, G = 3.6, T = 3.0
Sprivate = 18 - 3.0 - 12 = 3.0
Spublic = 3.0 - 3.6 = -0.6
S = 3.0 + (-0.6) = 2.4 = 18 - 12 - 3.6 ⟹
I = 2.4

"Investment" in macroeconomics means purchases of new capital goods (machines, factories, new houses, inventories). Buying a stock or bond is saving, not investment.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Nominal vs. Real Rates: The money market is drawn with the nominal rate i, and the loanable funds market with the real rate r (Chapter 2.4). Chapter 5.2 links the two.

Liquidity Preference: Keynes's theory of the interest rate is the money market above: i adjusts to equate money demand with the given supply.
Money demand is written as a function L(Y, i) that rises with income Y and falls with the interest rate i.
A common linear example is L = kY − hi, where k and h are positive constants.

Why the money market and loanable funds can both be right: The money market describes the short-run interest rate and prices (people choosing between money and bonds). Loanable funds describes the long-run real rate, set by saving and investment when output is at potential. In the long run, changes in the money supply affect prices, not the real rate.
