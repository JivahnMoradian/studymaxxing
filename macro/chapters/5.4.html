UNIT 5 — LONG-RUN CONSEQUENCES OF STABILIZATION POLICIES

Economic Growth

Practice these problems →

Measuring Economic Growth

Economic Growth is a sustained increase in an economy's ability to produce goods and services. It is measured as the growth rate of real GDP per capita, which adjusts for both inflation and population.

Growth Rate = New Value - Old ValueOld Value × 100
Real GDP per Capita = Real GDPPopulation
Real GDP: 12,500 → 13,000 ⟹  13,000 - 12,50012,500 × 100 = 4%

If real GDP grows faster than the population, GDP per capita (and the average standard of living) rises. A rough shortcut is: per capita growth ≈ real GDP growth − population growth.

Small Growth Differences Compound: The Rule of 70

Using the rule of 70 from Chapter 4.2 (years to double ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate in percent), at 2% growth, income doubles in about 35 years; at 7% it doubles in about 10 years. Over a lifetime, a country growing 3% a year ends up far richer than one growing 1%, which is why the long-run growth rate matters so much for living standards.

Productivity

Labor Productivity is output per worker (or per hour worked). It is the main driver of living standards.

Labor Productivity = Real GDPNumber of Workers

Real GDP growth is approximately the growth of productivity plus the growth of employment: %ΔY ≈ %Δ(Y/L) + %ΔL. Output per capita also depends on how large a share of the population works.

Sources of Growth

Output per worker depends on productivity, which is determined by:

  • Physical Capital per worker: machines, buildings, roads, software that firms and governments accumulate through investment.
  • Human Capital: the knowledge, skills, health and experience of workers, built through education, training and healthcare.
  • Technology (ideas): better methods of production and organization from research and development (R&D). Ideas can be used by many firms at once, and can be protected by proprietary rights such as patents and copyrights, which reward innovators.
  • Natural Resources: land, minerals and energy (useful but not essential, as resource-poor Japan shows).

The Aggregate Production Function shows that output per worker rises with physical capital per worker and human capital per worker (and technology), with diminishing returns to capital alone.

More capital per worker (k1 to k2) is a movement along the production function. Better technology shifts the whole function up.

Drawing It Yourself: put capital per worker on the x-axis and output per worker on the y-axis, draw an upward-sloping curve that flattens (diminishing returns), then draw a higher curve for better technology.

Growth in the PPC and LRAS

Economic growth is an outward shift of the PPC and a rightward shift of LRAS, as the economy's potential output rises. Growth is not moving from inside the PPC to the curve (that is recovery from a recession).

An economy that devotes more of today's resources to capital goods gives up consumer goods now but achieves a larger shift in the future. That trade-off is the central cost of growth.

Public Policy for Growth

  • Education and Training (human capital) and public health.
  • Infrastructure investment and incentives for private saving and investment (physical capital).
  • Support for R&D and patent protection (technology).
  • Property Rights, Rule of Law, stable institutions and open trade.
  • Raising labor force participation and growth in the labor force (immigration, childcare support).
  • Avoiding policies that crowd out investment (such as persistent large deficits, Unit 5.3).
Go Deeper: College-Level

Convergence: Because of diminishing returns, poorer countries with less capital have higher returns to capital and tend to grow faster (catch-up growth), if they have similar saving rates and institutions. This is conditional convergence: countries with weak institutions, low saving or poor education do not catch up, which is why some poor economies stay poor.
