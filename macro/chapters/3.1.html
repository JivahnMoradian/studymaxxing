UNIT 3 — NATIONAL INCOME & PRICE DETERMINATION

Aggregate Demand

Practice these problems →

What Aggregate Demand Is

Aggregate Demand (AD) shows the total quantity of domestically produced final goods and services (real GDP) that households, firms, government and foreigners plan to buy at each possible price level. The vertical axis is the price level (a price index such as the GDP deflator), and the horizontal axis is real GDP.

Total planned spending is the sum of the four spending components, the same ones used to measure GDP:

AD = C + I + G + NX
NX = X - M
  • C: consumption (household spending on goods and services).
  • I: investment (business spending on capital, new housing, and inventory changes). This is not buying stocks or bonds.
  • G: government purchases of goods and services (transfer payments such as Social Security are not part of G).
  • NX: net exports, exports (X) minus imports (M). Imports are subtracted because they are spending on foreign output.
C = 1,240,  I = 310,  G = 420,  X = 190,  M = 235 ⟹  AD = 1,240 + 310 + 420 + (190 - 235) = 1,925

Why the AD Curve Slopes Downward

A lower price level raises the quantity of real GDP demanded. This is not the micro substitution effect (the whole economy cannot substitute away from "everything"). Three macro effects explain the slope:

Effect A fall in the price level... ...so spending on
Wealth (Real-Balances) Effect raises the purchasing power of money and other fixed-value assets C rises
Interest-Rate Effect lowers money demand, which lowers interest rates I (and interest-sensitive C) rises
Exchange-Rate (Net-Export) Effect makes domestic goods cheaper relative to foreign goods (and tends to depress the currency) NX rises

A rise in the price level works in reverse: real wealth falls, interest rates rise, and domestic goods become relatively expensive, so the quantity of real GDP demanded falls.

Movement Along AD vs. a Shift of AD

  • A change in the price level causes a movement along a fixed AD curve (the three effects above).
  • A change in anything else that alters planned spending at a given price level shifts AD.

Drawing It Yourself: label the vertical axis "Price level" and the horizontal axis "Real GDP". Draw a downward-sloping line labeled AD1. For an increase in spending, draw a second line to the right (AD2), parallel to the first, and label it. For a decrease, shift it to the left.

What Shifts AD (Determinants of C, I, G, NX)

Component Increases AD (Shifts Right) Decreases AD (Shifts Left)
C Higher household wealth (stock or house prices up); higher consumer confidence / optimism about future income; lower income taxes (higher disposable income); lower household debt Lower wealth; pessimism; higher income taxes; heavy debt burdens
I Lower real interest rate; higher expected profits and business confidence; new technology that creates profitable projects; lower business taxes; low existing excess capacity Higher real interest rate; pessimistic expectations; higher business taxes; a lot of idle capacity
G Higher government purchases (roads, defense, schools) Lower government purchases
NX Higher foreign income; a weaker domestic currency (exports cheaper abroad, imports pricier at home); foreign tastes shifting to our goods Weaker foreign growth; a stronger domestic currency; trade barriers abroad

Exam shortcuts: a tax change shifts AD through C (income taxes) or I (business taxes); a change in the real interest rate shifts AD through I; a change in the exchange rate shifts AD through NX.

The Size of the Shift

The first-round change in spending is magnified because one person's spending is another's income. The horizontal shift in AD equals the initial change in spending times the spending multiplier (derived in 3.4):

Shift in AD = Multiplier × Initial Change in Spending
Multiplier = 4,  Δ I = -30 ⟹  AD shifts left by 4 × 30 = 120

AD Is Not the Micro Demand Curve

Market Demand Aggregate Demand
Vertical Axis Price of one good Price level
Horizontal Axis Quantity of one good Real GDP
Reason for Slope Substitution and income effects Wealth, interest-rate, exchange-rate effects
Shifters Tastes, Income, Related goods' prices, Expectations, Demographics (TIRED) Changes in C, I, G, NX
Go Deeper: College-Level

Why the three effects are not equally important: Most economists think the wealth effect is small (money and nominal assets are a small share of wealth), while the interest-rate effect is the strongest in practice. The exchange-rate effect matters more for open economies with large trade shares.
