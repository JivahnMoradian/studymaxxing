Net Exports & Capital Flows

Practice these problems →

Exchange Rates, Net Exports and Aggregate Demand

Net exports are a component of aggregate demand, AD = C + I + G + NX, so anything that moves the exchange rate moves AD.

  • When the dollar appreciates, US goods cost more to foreigners and foreign goods cost less to Americans. Exports Fall, Imports Rise, NX Falls, and AD shifts left.
  • When the dollar depreciates, exports rise, imports fall, NX rises, and AD shifts right.
Dollar Appreciates ⟹ X ↓, M ↑ ⟹ NX ↓ ⟹ AD ↓ ⟹ Y ↓, PL ↓, Unemployment ↑
Dollar Depreciates ⟹ X ↑, M ↓ ⟹ NX ↑ ⟹ AD ↑ ⟹ Y ↑, PL ↑, Unemployment ↓

Dollar appreciation: NX falls, AD shifts left, output and the price level fall in the short run.

Dollar depreciation: NX rises, AD shifts right, output and the price level rise in the short run.

Because a change in NX is a change in autonomous spending, the total shift in AD is larger than the change in NX itself once the spending multiplier is applied: ΔAD = ΔNX × multiplier.

MPC = 0.75 ⟹ Multiplier = 11 - 0.75 = 4 ⟹ ΔNX = -30 shifts AD left by 30 × 4 = 120

Other Determinants of NX: Besides the exchange rate, NX depends on domestic income (richer households import more), foreign income (richer foreigners buy more exports), relative price levels and tastes.

Capital Flows and Real Interest Rates

Financial capital flows toward the country with the higher real interest rate, because assets there pay more. If the US real interest rate rises relative to the rest of the world:

  • Foreigners buy more US assets, so the demand for dollars rises.
  • Americans buy fewer foreign assets, so the supply of dollars falls.
  • The dollar appreciates, net capital outflow falls, and NX falls.

Use real interest rates (nominal rate minus expected inflation) to compare countries. Net Capital Outflow (NCO) depends negatively on the domestic real interest rate: a higher r makes domestic assets more attractive, so residents send less capital abroad and foreigners send more in.

The Open-Economy Loanable Funds Model

In an open economy, domestic saving can fund domestic investment or purchases of foreign assets. Borrowers at home compete with foreign borrowers:

S = I + NCO
NX = NCO = S - I

The model has three linked markets. (1) Loanable funds: supply is national saving S (slopes up in r); demand is I + NCO (slopes down). (2) NCO schedule: NCO falls as r rises. (3) Foreign exchange: the quantity of dollars supplied is NCO (a vertical line, because NCO does not depend on the exchange rate), and the demand for dollars comes from NX, which slopes down in the real exchange rate (a lower e makes US goods cheaper, so foreigners buy more).

The equilibrium r* in the loanable funds market sets NCO in the middle panel, which fixes the vertical supply of dollars in the right panel and so determines the real exchange rate e*.

Go Deeper: Why a Tariff or Quota Does Not Change NX in the Long Run

The first-round effect (the standard AP answer) is that a tariff or quota cuts imports, so NX rises, AD shifts right, and the dollar appreciates.
But in this model NX = NCO = S − I, and a tariff changes neither national saving nor investment, so the equilibrium level of NX cannot change.
The appreciation makes US exports more expensive until exports fall by exactly as much as imports: NX goes back to its original value, while trade volumes (both imports and exports) are smaller.
Only a change in S, I or capital flows can change NX in the long run.

A budget deficit lowers national saving: S shifts left, r rises, NCO falls, the vertical supply of dollars shifts left, and the real exchange rate rises. NX falls ("twin deficits"). (AP-style graphs show the same deficit as a rightward shift in demand for loanable funds; here it is treated as lower national saving, with the same result.)

Capital flight (or higher foreign interest rates) shifts the NCO schedule right: r rises, NCO rises, the dollar depreciates and NX rises.

Trade Deficits and Surpluses

Since NX = NCO = S − I, a trade deficit (NX < 0) means the country invests more than it saves, a net capital inflow and a CFA surplus. Foreigners acquire more domestic assets, and future income (interest, dividends) flows abroad. A trade deficit is not automatically bad: borrowing from abroad to fund productive investment can raise future output. A trade surplus means the country saves more than it invests and accumulates foreign assets.

Drawing It Yourself: start with the loanable funds panel and find the new r; read NCO off the NCO schedule; move the vertical supply of dollars in the FX panel; read the new e off the NX demand curve. NX always moves the same way as NCO.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Marshall-Lerner and the J-Curve: A currency depreciation raises NX only if import and export demand are sufficiently price-elastic (the sum of the two elasticities exceeds 1). In the short run, quantities adjust slowly while the price of imports rises at once, so after a currency depreciation NX can worsen before it improves, tracing a "J".

Small Open Economy: If a country is small relative to world capital markets, its real interest rate equals the world rate r*.
Domestic saving and investment then differ with no effect on r: NCO = S − I(r*), and changes in S or I only move NCO and the exchange rate.
