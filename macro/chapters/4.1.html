UNIT 4 — THE FINANCIAL SECTOR

Money, Banking & the Money Supply

Practice these problems →

What Is Money?

Money is any asset that is widely accepted as payment. It does three jobs:

Function Meaning Example
Medium of Exchange Accepted in payment for goods and services (ends the "double coincidence of wants" problem of barter) Paying for lunch with a $10 bill
Unit of Account A common yardstick for measuring and comparing value Prices on a menu are quoted in dollars
Store of Value Holds purchasing power from today until later Cash in a drawer or a checking account
What Is the Double Coincidence of Wants?

In a barter economy, a trade works only if each person has what the other wants and wants what the other has. A wheat farmer who wants shoes must find a cobbler who happens to want wheat, and that match is rare. Money solves this: the farmer sells wheat to anyone for money, then spends the money on shoes, so no one needs a perfect match.

Good money is portable, durable, divisible, uniform, and in limited supply. Money is a store of value but is not the best one: it earns little or no interest, and inflation erodes its purchasing power. That is the price of its liquidity, the ease with which an asset converts into spending power.

Money comes in three kinds:

  • Commodity Money has value of its own (gold coins, cigarettes in a prison camp).
  • Commodity-Backed Money is paper redeemable for a commodity (a gold certificate; the US dollar when it was on the gold standard until the early 1970s).
  • Fiat Money has no intrinsic value and is money by government decree and public acceptance (the US dollar, the euro).

Measuring the Money Supply

Economists group assets by how liquid they are.

Measure Includes
M1 Currency in circulation, checkable (demand) deposits, and traveler's checks
M2 M1 plus savings deposits, small time deposits (CDs under $100,000), and retail money market funds
Monetary base Currency in circulation plus bank reserves

Credit cards are not money: a card swipe is a loan, and the debt is repaid with money later. Stocks, bonds, houses, and gold are assets but are not part of M1 or M2. Depositing cash into a checking account does not change M1 (currency falls by as much as checkable deposits rise).

Note. In 2020 the Fed began counting savings deposits inside M1, because it removed the limit of six withdrawals or transfers per month that savings accounts previously had, making them nearly as liquid as checking accounts. The AP exam and most textbooks still use the traditional split above, so we do too.

Bank Balance Sheets (T-Accounts)

A bank's balance sheet lists what it owns (assets) and what it owes (liabilities). Assets always equal liabilities plus net worth (also called equity or capital).

  • Assets: reserves (cash in the vault and deposits at the Fed), loans, and securities.
  • Liabilities: deposits (the bank owes this money back to its customers). Your deposit is the bank's liability but your asset.

Banks practice fractional reserve banking: they keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend out the rest. The Fed sets the required reserve ratio (rr).

Required Reserves = rr × Deposits
Excess Reserves = Total Reserves - Required Reserves

A bank that wants to lend can lend at most its excess reserves, because lending beyond them would leave it short of required reserves.

Example: First National has deposits of $500,000, reserves of $140,000, and a required reserve ratio of 0.20 (20%). How much can it lend?

Required Reserves = 0.20 × $500,000 = $100,000
Excess Reserves = $140,000 - $100,000 = $40,000

First National can lend at most $40,000. Securities (such as Treasury bills) are assets but are not reserves.

How Banks Create Money

Banks create money by lending. Follow a $1,000 cash deposit with rr = 20%.

  1. Bank A takes the deposit: reserves +$1,000, deposits +$1,000. It must hold 20% × $1,000 = $200 and can lend the other $800.
  2. The borrower spends the $800, and the seller deposits it in Bank B, which holds $160 and lends $640.
  3. The process repeats, with each round 80% as large as the last.

The rounds form a geometric series, 1,000 + 800 + 640 + … , whose total is the initial deposit divided by the reserve ratio. That is the money multiplier.

Money Multiplier = 1rr
Maximum Change in Checkable Deposits = 1rr × New Reserves
Money Multiplier = 10.20 = 5
Max Change in Deposits = 5 × $1,000 = $5,000
Max New Loans = $5,000 - $1,000 = $4,000

The multiplier works in reverse: if the Fed drains $1,000 of reserves from fully loaned-up banks, deposits can shrink by up to $5,000. Equivalently, the monetary base is multiplied up into the money supply: new reserves ΔR can raise the money supply by up to ΔR ÷ rr.

Why the Simple Multiplier Overstates

The AP exam calls 1/rr the maximum multiplier. The real one is smaller because:

  • Banks may choose to hold excess reserves rather than lend everything.
  • The public may hold some of its money as currency instead of redepositing it (a "leakage" from the process).
  • Some potential borrowers may not want loans.

Drawing It Yourself: draw a T-account with Assets on the left and Liabilities on the right. For a deposit, record +Reserves and +Deposits. For the bank's loan, record −Reserves (up to its excess) and +Loans; the borrower's spending then shows up as +Reserves and +Deposits at the next bank.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Bank Runs and Deposit Insurance: Because banks hold only a fraction of deposits in reserves, they are vulnerable if many depositors demand cash at once. Deposit Insurance (the FDIC insures up to $250,000 per depositor) removes the incentive to run.

Reserves Today: Since 2008 the Fed pays interest on reserves, and since March 2020 the required reserve ratio has been set at 0%, so banks are constrained mainly by capital rules and by their own desire to hold reserves. Textbook money-multiplier reasoning is still the right way to build intuition for the AP exam.
