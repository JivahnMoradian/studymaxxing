UNIT 6 — THE OPEN ECONOMY: INTERNATIONAL TRADE & FINANCE

Policy in an Open Economy

Practice these problems →

Why the Open Economy Changes Policy Analysis

In a closed economy, stabilization policy works through the interest rate and investment. In an open economy there is a second channel: interest rates change capital flows, which change the exchange rate, which changes net exports. The exchange rate channel can reinforce or offset the domestic effect on AD. In all that follows, we assume the country's exchange rate is flexible.

Monetary Policy Under Flexible Exchange Rates

Expansionary Monetary Policy (the Fed buys bonds, lowers its target rate) works through two reinforcing channels:

MS ↑ ⟹ i ↓ (r ↓) ⟹ I ↑ and Capital Outflow ⟹ Dollar Depreciates ⟹ NX ↑ ⟹ AD ↑ ⟹ Y ↑, PL ↑
  • Lower r makes US assets less attractive: the demand for dollars falls and the supply of dollars rises, so the dollar depreciates.
  • The weaker dollar raises exports and lowers imports: NX rises, adding to the increase in investment.

Contractionary Monetary Policy (the Fed sells bonds, raises rates) is the mirror image: MS ↓, r ↑, I ↓, capital inflow, the dollar appreciates, NX ↓, AD ↓.

Left: the Fed increases the money supply and the interest rate falls. Right: lower US interest rates reduce the demand for dollars and raise the supply of dollars; the dollar depreciates.

The investment and net-export effects shift AD right, closing the recessionary gap.

Fiscal Policy Under Flexible Exchange Rates

Expansionary Fiscal Policy (higher G or lower T) raises AD directly, but financing the larger deficit pulls the other way through the exchange rate channel:

Deficit ↑ ⟹ Demand for Loanable Funds ↑ ⟹ r ↑ ⟹ I ↓ (Crowding Out) and Capital Inflow ⟹ Dollar Appreciates ⟹ NX ↓

The net AD effect is still positive but smaller than the closed-economy multiplier suggests: part of the stimulus leaks to higher imports and lower exports. This is also the twin deficits link: budget deficits tend to come with trade deficits.

Δ G = 40, MPC = 0.75 ⟹ Multiplier = 4 ⟹ Initial Shift = 160
If I falls 10 and NX falls 6:
(-10 - 6) × 4 = -64 ⟹ Net Shift = 160 - 64 = 96

Contractionary Fiscal Policy works in reverse: r falls, capital flows out, the dollar depreciates and NX rises, softening the fall in AD.

Policy r Capital Flow Dollar NX AD Effect
Expansionary Monetary Falls Outflow Depreciates Rises Rises (reinforced)
Contractionary Monetary Rises Inflow Appreciates Falls Falls (reinforced)
Expansionary Fiscal Rises Inflow Appreciates Falls Rises (partly offset)
Contractionary Fiscal Falls Outflow Depreciates Rises Falls (partly offset)

Policy abroad matters too: if the European Central Bank cuts rates, capital flows toward the US, the dollar appreciates, and US NX and AD fall.

Trade Policy: Tariffs, Quotas and the Exchange Rate

A tariff is a tax on imports; a quota is a legal limit on the quantity imported. Both raise the domestic price of the good, raise domestic output, lower domestic consumption, and cut imports. The differences: a tariff raises government revenue; a quota creates license rents (extra profit) for whoever holds the import licenses.

In the foreign exchange market, a tariff on imports reduces Americans' demand for foreign goods, so fewer dollars are supplied (the supply of dollars shifts left) and the dollar appreciates. Because imports fall, net exports rise and AD shifts right (output and the price level rise in the short run). The stronger dollar makes US exports more expensive, which partly offsets the gain. If trading partners retaliate with their own tariffs, the demand for dollars falls too and exports fall further.

A US import tariff: the supply of dollars shifts left and the dollar appreciates.

Go Deeper: What Happens to Net Exports in the Long Run?

The result above is the first-round effect (the standard AP answer).
Over time the exchange rate can undo it.
In the open-economy model, NX = NCO = S − I: net exports are pinned down by national saving and investment, and a tariff changes neither.
So the dollar keeps appreciating until exports have fallen by exactly as much as imports, and NX returns to where it started.
Trade volumes are smaller (both imports and exports are lower) and the gains from trade are lost.
This is why an import tariff acts like an export tax (Lerner symmetry).

With a flexible exchange rate, an import tariff leaves output unchanged as well. Under a fixed rate the central bank prevents the appreciation, so the tariff does raise NX and output (see the Impossible Trinity in Chapter 6.2 for why fixed rates constrain policy).

Tariff in the Import Market: With world price Pw and a per-unit tariff t, the domestic price rises to Pw + t. Domestic producers expand, domestic buyers cut back, and imports shrink. The tariff revenue is t times remaining imports; the two triangles are the deadweight loss.

Imports = Qd - Qs
Revenue = t × Imports After Tariff
DW Loss = ½ t (Qs' - Qs) + ½ t (Qd - Qd')
Qd = 120 - 4P, Qs = -20 + 6P, Pw = 10, t = 2 ⟹ P = 12
Qd = 72
Qs = 52
Imports = 72 - 52 = 20
Revenue = 2 × 20 = 40
DWL = ½(2)(12) + ½(2)(8) = 20

Before the tariff, imports were 80 − 40 = 40 at the world price of 10. The shaded blue rectangle is tariff revenue; the two red triangles are deadweight loss.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Trade Feedback Effects: When a large economy expands, it imports more, which raises foreign incomes, which raises foreign demand for its exports. The initial boost to domestic output is therefore amplified by this foreign repercussion. Conversely, imports are a leakage: the multiplier with imports (and taxes) is 1 / (1 − MPC(1 − t) + m), where t is the income-tax rate and m is the marginal propensity to import (the share of each extra dollar of income spent on imports). This is smaller than 1 / (1 − MPC).

Beggar-Thy-Neighbor Policies: A country that depreciates its currency to raise NX raises its AD at the expense of its trading partners' AD. If partners respond in kind ("currency wars" or retaliatory tariffs), everyone loses trade volume, as in the 1930s Smoot-Hawley episode.
