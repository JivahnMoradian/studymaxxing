UNIT 1 — BASIC ECONOMIC CONCEPTS

Scarcity, Opportunity Cost & the PPC

Practice these problems →

Scarcity and Trade-Offs

Scarcity means resources are limited while wants are unlimited, so every society must choose what to produce, how, and for whom. Resources (the factors of production) fall into four groups:

  • Land: natural resources (soil, oil, water).
  • Labor: the work effort of people.
  • Capital: manufactured goods used to produce other goods (machines, factories, software). This is physical capital, not money.
  • Entrepreneurship: organizing the other three and bearing risk.

Because resources are scarce, choosing more of one thing means choosing less of another. That is the trade-off at the heart of economics, and it is why macroeconomic questions such as "should we spend more on investment or on consumption?" are hard.

Opportunity Cost

The Opportunity Cost of a choice is the value of the next-best alternative given up. It is measured in the other good, not in dollars.

Opportunity Cost of 1 Unit of X = Units of Y Given UpUnits of X Gained
Give up 12 pants to gain 18 shirts: Opportunity Cost of 1 Shirt = 1218 = 23 pants

The Production Possibilities Curve (PPC)

A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all resources are fully and efficiently used, with technology fixed.

  • On the Curve → efficient and attainable (full employment, no waste).
  • Inside the Curve → attainable but inefficient: unemployed or misallocated resources. Macro link: a recession is a move inside the PPC.
  • Outside the Curve → Unattainable with current resources and technology.

Drawing It Yourself: label the axes with the two goods, draw a bowed-out curve from one axis to the other, then mark one point on it, one inside, and one outside.

Shape: Constant vs. Increasing Opportunity Cost

  • Straight-Line PPC → Constant opportunity cost (resources are equally good at making both goods).
  • Bowed-Out PPC → Increasing opportunity cost (resources are specialized, so each extra unit of one good costs more of the other). This is the realistic case.

Economic Growth

Growth is an outward shift of the PPC. It comes from more resources, better technology, or a better-educated workforce. A technology gain in only one good pivots the curve along that good's axis.

Why It Matters for Macro: an economy that devotes more of today's resources to capital goods (investment) gets a larger outward shift of its PPC in the future. That choice costs consumer goods today. Moving along the PPC is a trade-off; shifting the PPC is growth.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Opportunity Cost is the slope: The absolute value of the PPC's slope at a point is the Marginal Rate of Transformation (MRT):

MRT = |Δ YΔ X|

On a bowed-out PPC the slope steepens as you move toward more X, so the MRT rises, which is the law of increasing opportunity cost.

Allocative Efficiency: Productive Efficiency means being on the PPC. Allocative Efficiency means being at the point on the PPC that society values most. At that point the marginal benefit of the last unit of each good, relative to the other, equals its MRT.

Investment and Growth: Compare two economies on the same PPC today. The one that picks a point with more capital goods invests more, so its PPC shifts out further next year. This is the simplest model of why high-saving economies grow faster, and it returns in Unit 5.
