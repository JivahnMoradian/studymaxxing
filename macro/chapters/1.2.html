UNIT 1 — BASIC ECONOMIC CONCEPTS

Comparative Advantage & Trade

Practice these problems →

Absolute vs. Comparative Advantage

  • Absolute Advantage: the ability to produce a good using fewer resources (or to produce more of it with the same resources) than another producer.
  • Comparative Advantage: the ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.

Trade is beneficial whenever opportunity costs differ, even if one country is better at everything. Each country should specialize where its opportunity cost is lowest, then trade.

Two Ways to Read the Data

Output Method (units produced per unit of resource): opportunity cost of a good = other good ÷ this good.

Input Method (hours needed per unit): opportunity cost of a good = hours for this good ÷ hours for the other.

Opportunity Cost of X (Output Method) = Output of YOutput of X
Opportunity Cost of X (Input Method) = Hours per Unit of XHours per Unit of Y
Output: Norland makes 24 wheat or 8 cloth ⟹ Opportunity Cost of 1 Wheat = 824 = 13 cloth
Input: Tara needs 6 hrs per kayak and 4 hrs per canoe ⟹ Opportunity Cost of 1 Kayak = 64 = 1.5 canoes

The opportunity cost of one good is always the reciprocal of the opportunity cost of the other, within a country.

Terms of Trade

The Terms of Trade are the price at which two countries swap goods. Both gain only if the price lies between the two countries' opportunity costs of that good. Each country buys the good for less than it would cost to make at home, and sells it for more than its own opportunity cost.

Opportunity CostExporter < Terms of Trade < Opportunity CostImporter

Country A's PPC is flatter in wheat terms than Country B's, so their opportunity costs differ and trade can help both.

Gains from Trade

With specialization and trade, each country can consume a bundle outside its own PPC, which was unattainable in isolation. Trade acts like a technology gain.

Drawing It Yourself: for each country, draw its linear PPC, mark the autarky (no-trade) point on it, then mark the post-trade consumption point outside the line.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Exchange Rates and Trade Patterns: Prices in different countries are quoted in different currencies, so you must convert before comparing.
A country will export a good only if, after conversion, it is cheaper at home than abroad.
Let e = dollars per euro.
The US sells raspberries at $6 and chocolate at $20;
Belgium sells them at €24 and €16.

  • US raspberries are cheaper than Belgian ones when $6 < €24 × e, so e > 0.25.
  • Belgian chocolate is cheaper than US chocolate when €16 × e < $20, so e < 1.25.

When 0.25 < e < 1.25, each country is the cheaper seller of one good, so the US exports raspberries and Belgium exports chocolate: trade runs in both directions. Outside that range one country is cheaper at both goods (for example, if e < 0.25 Belgium undercuts the US on both), so it sells to the other and buys nothing back.

Why Trade Doesn't Need Equal Gains: Where in the opportunity-cost range the price lands only splits the gains between the countries. Any price in the range leaves both better off than isolation.
