UNIT 5 — LONG-RUN CONSEQUENCES OF STABILIZATION POLICIES

Money Neutrality, Quantity Theory & Expectations

Practice these problems →

Money Neutrality

Money Neutrality is the long-run result that a change in the money supply changes nominal variables (the price level, nominal wages, the nominal interest rate) but leaves real variables (real GDP, employment, the real wage, the real interest rate) unchanged.

  • In the short run, sticky wages and prices mean more money raises AD and real output rises (money is not neutral).
  • In the long run, wages and prices adjust. Output returns to potential and only the price level is higher.

In the graph, an increase in the money supply shifts AD right (E1 to E2: output above Yf). Over time nominal wages rise, SRAS shifts left, and the economy ends at E3 on LRAS with the same output and a higher price level.

The Quantity Theory of Money

The Equation of Exchange links money to spending. Velocity (V) is the average number of times a dollar is spent on final goods and services per year.

M × V = P × Y
V = P × YM

Here M is the money supply, P the price level, and Y real GDP, so P × Y is nominal GDP. The equation is an identity: velocity is defined to make it true. The Quantity Theory adds an assumption: V is stable and Y is determined by real factors (labor, capital, technology). Then the price level moves in proportion to the money supply.

M = 600, V = 5, Y = 1,500 ⟹  P = M × VY = 3,0001,500 = 2.0

Money Growth and Inflation

Taking growth rates of both sides (approximately):

%Δ M + %Δ V ≈ %Δ P + %Δ Y

If V is constant, inflation equals money growth minus real output growth:

Inflation ≈ %Δ M - %Δ Y

So in the long run the growth rate of the money supply determines the inflation rate: inflation (deflation) results from increasing (decreasing) the money supply too rapidly for a sustained period. This is why economists call inflation a monetary phenomenon. Hyperinflations (very high sustained inflation) are always accompanied by extremely fast money growth, usually to finance government deficits.

Each country (A, B, C) lies on the line "inflation = money growth" when real growth is zero and V is constant. Higher money growth means proportionally higher inflation.

Interest Rates: The Fisher Effect

The Real Interest Rate (r) is the nominal rate adjusted for inflation. Lenders and borrowers set the nominal rate (i) using expected inflation (πe), which gives the Fisher equation.

i = r + πe

After the fact, the ex post real rate uses actual inflation:

rEx Post = i - π
Loan at i = 8%, πe = 3% ⟹  r = 5%.
if actual π = 5%: rex post = 3%

Fisher Effect: when expected inflation rises one-for-one with sustained money growth, the nominal interest rate rises by the same amount in the long run, leaving the real rate unchanged. In the short run, an increase in the money supply lowers the nominal rate (the liquidity effect). In the long run, higher inflation expectations raise it.

When inflation turns out higher than expected, borrowers gain and lenders lose (the real value of the debt falls). When it is lower than expected, the reverse happens.

Expectations, Credibility and Policy

  • Adaptive Expectations: people form expectations from past inflation. Changes in policy take time to affect expectations, so disinflation tends to be costly.
  • Rational Expectations: people use all available information, including announced policy. A Credible announcement can change expectations immediately, so inflation falls with little lost output.
  • Credibility comes from a central bank's record and independence. A credible bank can lower inflation at a lower cost (a smaller rise in unemployment).
  • Anticipated policy affects the price level more than output. Unanticipated policy surprises can move real output in the short run.
Go Deeper: College-Level

The Classical Dichotomy: In the classical model real variables (output, relative prices, the real rate) are determined by real forces and nominal variables by the money supply. Money is a veil.

The Inflation Tax: Printing money to finance spending is a tax on money holders: seigniorage. Real money balances lose value at the rate of inflation, which is how hyperinflations fund government spending. Velocity also rises in hyperinflation because people spend money quickly before it loses value.

The Taylor Rule: A simple guide for the nominal federal funds rate (i) responds to inflation and the output gap. Taylor's original version uses a 2% equilibrium real rate (r*) and a 2% inflation target (π*):

i = π + r* + 0.5(π - π*) + 0.5 (Output Gap in %)
π = 3, π* = 2, Gap = 1, r* = 2:
i = 3 + 2 + 0.5(1) + 0.5(1) = 6%

The coefficient on inflation in total is 1.5, which is greater than 1. This is the Taylor principle: the nominal rate rises by more than the rise in inflation, so the real rate rises and cools demand. For example, if inflation rises by 1 percentage point, the rule raises the nominal rate by 1.5 points, so the real rate (i − π) rises by 0.5 points, which slows spending and brings inflation back down. If the rule gives a negative rate, the Fed is constrained by the zero lower bound. Households and banks can hold cash at a 0% return, so the nominal rate cannot fall much below zero, and in a deep recession the Fed has to rely on tools such as QE and forward guidance instead (see Chapter 4.4).
