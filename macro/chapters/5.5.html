UNIT 5 — LONG-RUN CONSEQUENCES OF STABILIZATION POLICIES

Supply-Side Policy & Policy Debates

Practice these problems →

Demand-Side versus Supply-Side Policy

  • Demand-Side Policies change aggregate demand: government purchases, taxes and transfers aimed at spending (fiscal policy), and changes in the money supply and interest rates (monetary policy). They are used to stabilize the business cycle.
  • Supply-Side Policies aim to raise aggregate supply and potential output by improving incentives and productive capacity. They work mainly on LRAS (and SRAS), so they are about long-run growth.

Typical supply-side policies:

  • Lower Marginal Tax Rates on labor income, business profits or capital gains, to raise incentives to work, save and invest.
  • Deregulation that lowers firms' costs and barriers to entry.
  • Education, Job Training and Infrastructure spending (human and physical capital).
  • Research and Development support (tax credits, grants, patents).
  • Reducing barriers to trade and labor mobility.

Many policies, such as tax cuts, have both demand-side effects (short run: more disposable income and spending) and supply-side effects (long run: changed incentives and more capital).

Effects in AD-AS and on the PPC

Successful supply-side policy shifts LRAS to the right (potential output rises) and usually shifts SRAS to the right (lower costs). With AD unchanged, real GDP rises and the price level falls, and unemployment falls. On the PPC graph it corresponds to an outward shift.

Supply-side policy can lower inflation and unemployment together, while demand-side policy faces the short-run trade-off of the Phillips curve (Unit 5.1). The cost is that supply-side effects are usually slow, uncertain, and may reduce tax revenue or raise inequality.

The Laffer Curve

The Laffer Curve shows tax revenue as a function of the tax rate. At a 0% rate revenue is zero. At 100% nobody has an incentive to work or report income, so revenue is also zero (the tax base disappears). Revenue therefore reaches a maximum somewhere in between (t*).

Note: the points labeled 20 and 80 are only an example. No one can say with certainty that the revenue-maximizing tax rate lies between them.

  • Left of t*: raising the rate raises revenue (the higher rate outweighs the shrinking base).
  • Right of t* (the "prohibitive range"): raising the rate lowers revenue because the tax base shrinks by more. Cutting rates there raises revenue.
  • Two different rates (like A and B) can raise the same revenue.

Supply-side advocates argue that cutting very high tax rates can increase work effort and investment enough to partly or fully pay for the cut. Most evidence suggests that in countries like the US, rates are on the left side, so cuts lose revenue (though less than a static estimate suggests).

Tax Revenue = Tax Rate × Tax Base

Static versus Dynamic Scoring: A static estimate of a tax cut assumes the tax base does not respond. Dynamic Scoring includes the base's response (work, saving, investment), so the revenue loss is smaller. The measured response is the elasticity of taxable income.

Rules versus Discretion:

  • Discretion: policymakers choose their actions case by case based on current conditions.
  • Rules: policymakers commit in advance to a fixed procedure (e.g. a Taylor rule from Chapter 5.2, a constant money growth rate, an inflation target, a balanced budget requirement).
Rules Discretion
Strength Predictable and credible; avoids political pressure and time inconsistency Flexible: can respond to unusual shocks (financial crises, pandemics)
Weakness Cannot handle unforeseen events; may be wrong in a new situation Can be abused for short-term gains; makes expectations uncertain

Time Inconsistency: A central bank announces low inflation to keep expectations low. But once the public believes it, the bank is tempted to surprise them with expansionary policy, which lowers unemployment temporarily. The public anticipates this, so expected inflation rises and the result is higher inflation with no gain in output (the inflation bias). A credible commitment, rules, or central bank independence solves the problem.

Policy Lags: Long and uncertain lags (Chapter 3.5) are a main argument for rules, because discretionary policy can arrive at the wrong time.

Rational Expectations and the Lucas Critique: If people form rational expectations, anticipated policy changes are built into wages and prices at once, so only surprises move real output (policy ineffectiveness). The Lucas Critique warns that relationships estimated under one policy regime (such as the Phillips curve) can break down when the policy changes, because people alter their behavior.
