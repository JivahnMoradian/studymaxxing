6.4

Policy in an Open Economy

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

In an economy that trades with the world, government and central-bank decisions work through the exchange rate as well as through domestic spending and jobs. Interest-rate changes move capital and the currency, and tariffs (taxes on imports) change prices, trade flows and sometimes the currency itself, so who ends up paying for them is one of the most argued-over questions in economics.

Key Terms

  • Tariff: A tax on imported goods, paid by the importing firm when goods cross the border. It raises the price of the imported good and of competing domestic goods, raises revenue for the government, and can prompt other countries to retaliate.
  • Quota: A legal limit on the quantity of a good that may be imported. Unlike a tariff, a quota raises no tax revenue; the higher price goes to whoever holds the right to import.
  • Who Bears a Tariff ("Incidence"): The tax is legally paid by the importer, but the cost is shared among foreign exporters (if they cut their prices), importers (lower profit margins), and domestic consumers (higher prices). How the burden is split is an empirical question, and studies of recent tariffs disagree.
  • Monetary Policy in an Open Economy: When a central bank cuts interest rates, borrowing and spending rise, but the currency also tends to weaken (investors earn less), which raises exports and import prices. Raising rates does the opposite. The currency channel can amplify the effect of the rate change.
  • Currency Manipulation (Accusation): A claim that a country is deliberately holding its currency down to help its exporters. The US Treasury can formally label a country a currency manipulator; when it did so for China in 2019, Goldman Sachs called the label mostly symbolic.

Think About It

1. The US puts a 25% tariff on an imported appliance that cost the importer $400. If foreign sellers do not change their price, what do the importer and shoppers face?

Short answer

The importer pays $100 in tax on a $400 product, so its cost is $500. It can raise the shelf price by the full amount, absorb some of it in lower profits, or push the foreign seller to cut the price. Whatever share is not absorbed by the foreign seller or the importer reaches shoppers.

2. The Fed cuts interest rates. Why might that make a US vacation cheaper for foreign tourists but make imported goods more expensive for Americans?

Short answer

Lower US rates make dollar assets less attractive, so investors sell dollars and the dollar weakens. A weaker dollar means foreigners need less of their own currency to buy dollars (cheaper US holidays and US exports), but Americans need more dollars to buy foreign goods (more expensive imports).

3. A country puts a tariff on the goods of one trading partner. Name one way it could end up with fewer exports even though the tariff was on imports.

Short answer

The partner may retaliate with its own tariffs on the country's exports. Also, if the tariff raises the price of imported inputs, domestic exporters' costs rise; and a tariff can also push the currency up, which makes exports more expensive for foreigners.

Case Study: The US-China Tariffs and the Yuan (2018-19)

Starting in 2018 the US put successive rounds of tariffs on Chinese goods, and China retaliated. Economists Mary Amiti, Stephen Redding and David Weinstein found that the tariffs were passed through almost completely to US import prices: a 10% tariff raised the tariff-inclusive price by almost 10%, meaning foreign exporters had not cut their own prices and the cost fell on US importers and, in turn, buyers.

The exchange rate then became part of the story. On August 5, 2019 the yuan weakened past 7 per US dollar as the US threatened new tariffs. A weaker yuan makes Chinese goods cheaper in dollars and so can partly offset a tariff, though China's central bank said the move was not a response to the tariffs.

Why It Matters Outside US-China Trade: A tariff does not act alone. Currencies can move to offset it, which is why economists look at the exchange rate when judging who pays. If the exporting country's currency falls by about as much as the tariff, its goods cost buyers in the importing country roughly what they did before, and the burden shifts to the exporting country, whose residents now find imports and foreign travel more expensive.

Sources: Amiti, Redding and Weinstein, JEP 2019, ProMarket summary, CNBC, August 6, 2019.

Case Study: The 2025-26 US Tariff Changes (as of October 2026)

What Happened: In 2025 the US raised tariffs on most imports. According to a New York Fed analysis, the average tariff on US imports climbed from 2.6% to roughly 13% during 2025. On February 20, 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6 to 3 that the emergency-powers law (IEEPA) behind many of these tariffs does not authorize them. The administration responded with replacement tariffs under other laws.

What the Exchange Rate Did: The dollar fell about 10% in the first half of 2025, its worst half-year since 1973. A weaker dollar raises the dollar price of imports on top of any tariff, and makes US exports cheaper abroad, so it is a second factor behind price changes, alongside the tariffs.

Who Pays: The Competing Findings: A New York Fed analysis concluded that nearly 90% of the 2025 tariff burden fell on US businesses and consumers. By contrast, economist Caroline Freund's working paper found that foreign exporters cut their prices enough to absorb roughly 40 to 50% of the increases, with big exporters adjusting more than small ones.

The Competing Views: Supporters of tariffs argue that they protect domestic manufacturing and jobs, raise revenue, and give the US leverage in negotiations. Critics argue that they raise prices for consumers and businesses, hit lower-income households hardest, invite retaliation and weaken the trading system.

Why It Matters Outside the US: For anyone buying imported goods, a tariff may show up as a higher price tag, a smaller discount, a lower profit margin, or a quiet change in what is stocked, depending on who pays. For any country, it is wise to distinguish the headline tariff rate from the average rate actually paid, and to check what the currency is doing, because the dollar's move in 2025 was as large as many tariff changes.

Sources: Global Trade and Sanctions Law on the Supreme Court ruling, Wealth Professional on the NY Fed study, UC San Diego on Freund's paper, Outlook Business on the dollar.

The Takeaway

Open-economy policy works through two channels at once: the domestic one (spending and jobs) and the exchange-rate one (what a currency does to prices and trade). Tariffs do not have a single answer to "who pays": the evidence points to different splits depending on the period, the product and the size of the exporter. When you read the news, look at the average rate actually paid, who absorbed the price change, and what the currency did.
