5.3

Deficits, Debt & Crowding Out

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

A deficit is what a government borrows in one year; the debt is the pile of borrowing built up over many years. Whether the pile is a problem depends on how large it is relative to the economy, what it costs to carry, and what the borrowing displaced.

Key Terms

  • Budget Deficit vs. National Debt: The deficit is a flow: the gap in one year when the government spends more than it collects in taxes. The debt is a stock: the total amount owed after all past deficits (less any surpluses). A deficit adds to the debt; a surplus reduces it. Think of the deficit as this year's overspending and the debt as the credit-card balance.
  • Debt-to-GDP Ratio: The national debt divided by the country's annual output. It is the standard way to compare debt across countries and over time, because what matters is the size of the debt relative to the income that is available to service it. A household with a $300,000 mortgage is in a very different position at a $40,000 income than at a $200,000 income. (Be careful which debt is meant: "gross" counts everything the government owes, including to its own agencies; "held by the public" or "net" subtracts the part that the government owes to itself.)
  • Net Interest: What the government pays each year on its debt, after subtracting the interest it earns. It rises with both the size of the debt and the interest rate.
  • Crowding Out: When government borrowing competes with businesses and homebuyers for the same pool of savings, which can push interest rates up and reduce private investment in factories, equipment and housing. Less investment means a smaller capital stock and, over time, lower output and incomes than would otherwise occur. How large the effect is depends on the state of the economy and where the money comes from (domestic savers or foreign lenders).
  • Interest Rate vs. Growth Rate: A debt-to-GDP ratio stabilizes or falls if the economy's growth rate is at least as high as the interest rate on the debt (before new borrowing). When interest rates are above the growth rate, the ratio rises on its own.

The Big Picture: The Same Debt Looks Different Depending on the Measure

Compare headline numbers carefully. A country with a very high gross debt can have a much lower net debt if its government owns large financial assets. And the cost of the debt depends on interest rates: the same debt that costs 1% of GDP a year at low rates can cost 4% when rates are higher.

Think About It

1. A government has a deficit of $200 billion this year. Its debt was $1,000 billion. What is the debt now, and if GDP is $1,000 billion what is the debt-to-GDP ratio?

Short answer

The debt is $1,200 billion. If the economy has not grown, the ratio is 120%, up from 100%. If GDP had grown to $1,100 billion, the ratio would be about 109%. Growth in the denominator can offset borrowing in the numerator.

2. Why do some economists worry about a rising debt even when no one has trouble selling the government's bonds?

Short answer

Because of crowding out and interest costs. More government borrowing absorbs savings that could have funded private investment, which can lower future incomes, and larger interest payments leave less room in the budget for other things. Others reply that these effects are small when the economy has unused capacity (since borrowing then pulls in otherwise idle resources), and that debt is a problem mainly when rates exceed growth.

3. A government sells its bonds mostly to its own citizens, who keep their savings in them. Another sells most of its bonds to foreigners. Which is more exposed if lenders lose confidence?

Short answer

The one that relies on foreign lenders is generally more exposed, because it depends on money that can leave quickly and, if the debt is in a foreign currency, it cannot create the money to repay it. Domestic holders are more stable, though the debt is still a claim on future taxpayers.

Case Study: The United States, How Big and Does It Matter?

The Congressional Budget Office (CBO), Congress's nonpartisan budget scorekeeper, projected in February 2026 a federal deficit of $1.9 trillion in fiscal year 2026, or 5.8% of GDP. It expected debt held by the public to climb from 101% of GDP in 2026 to 120% in 2036, with net interest costs growing from 3.3% to 4.6% of GDP over the same period.

There is a real debate. One side, including CBO, argues that persistent large deficits when the economy is near full employment compete for savings, raise rates and eventually reduce incomes. The other side argues that borrowing in a slump does little harm and can even help by raising output and tax revenue. Both agree that the interest rate matters: the more the government must pay to roll over its debt, the sharper the trade-off with other priorities.

Why It Matters Outside the US: A government's interest bill is a real budget item, like your mortgage payment, and it moves with interest rates. If you hold a mortgage or a savings account, your rates and the government's borrowing costs are linked through the same market for loanable funds. That is why budget news and bond yields show up in loan and mortgage rates.

Sources: CBO: The Budget and Economic Outlook 2026 to 2036, CBO: Projections of deficits and debt under two scenarios with higher interest rates, EPI on public debt in a depressed economy.

Case Study: Japan, the Highest Debt Ratio Among Large Economies, With Low Interest Costs

Japan's gross government debt is about 230% of GDP (OECD, 2024), far above the US ratio. Yet Japan has borrowed at very low interest rates for roughly three decades. Much of the debt is held domestically, the Bank of Japan owns a large share of it, and the government also owns large financial assets: netting these out, one analysis puts Japan's net debt at roughly 77% of GDP in 2024, close to a comparable US figure of about 83%.

Why It Matters Outside Japan: The headline debt ratio is only a start. Who holds the debt, what currency it is in, what the government owns, and above all what interest rate it pays all determine whether a given ratio is manageable. A high ratio is easier to carry at low rates, and harder when rates rise.

Sources: The Conversable Economist on Japan's debt.

The Takeaway

Deficits add to the debt, and the cost of the debt depends on the interest rate, the GDP growth rate and who lends. Economists disagree about how worried to be, especially about whether borrowing crowds out private investment, but they agree that the arithmetic of interest costs gets harder as rates rise and debt grows. As a citizen, compare debt to GDP and the interest bill rather than the raw dollar figure.
