The Money Market & Loanable Funds

Real-World Relevance: Medium
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

An interest rate is the price of borrowing money, and like other prices it is set by supply and demand: savers supply funds, borrowers demand them. When more people want to borrow or fewer want to save, rates rise, and that changes what a house, a car or a business loan costs.

Key Terms

  • Interest Rate: The price of using someone else's money for a period, quoted as a percentage per year. For a saver it is the reward for waiting; for a borrower it is the cost of borrowing.
  • Loanable Funds Market: The market where savers (supplying money) meet borrowers (demanding it). If people save more, the supply rises and interest rates tend to fall. If governments and businesses borrow more, the demand rises and rates tend to rise.
  • Demand for Money: How much cash and bank balances people want to hold. When interest rates are high, holding cash is costly because it earns little compared with bonds or savings accounts, so people want to hold less of it.
  • Federal Funds Rate vs. Mortgage Rate: The federal funds rate is the short-term rate banks charge each other overnight, which the Fed targets. A mortgage rate is a 30-year loan rate set in the market, and it tends to follow longer-term bond yields such as the 10-year Treasury, not the Fed's overnight rate directly.
  • Monthly Mortgage Payment: For a fixed 30-year loan, the payment is set by the loan size and the rate. Small changes in the rate move the payment a lot, because interest is charged over 360 months.

Think About It

1. Households suddenly save much more. What should happen to interest rates, and who benefits?

Short answer

More savings means a larger supply of loanable funds, so interest rates tend to fall. Borrowers (home buyers, businesses) benefit from cheaper loans, while savers earn less on each dollar saved.

2. The Fed holds its policy rate steady but the 10-year Treasury yield jumps. Which way do mortgage rates go?

Short answer

Up. Mortgage rates follow long-term bond yields more closely than the Fed's overnight rate, so a higher 10-year yield usually means a higher mortgage rate even when the Fed does nothing.

3. You can afford a mortgage payment of $2,000 a month (principal and interest). Does a rise in rates from 3% to 7% change how big a loan you can get?

Short answer

Yes, by a lot. At 3%, $2,000 a month supports about a $474,000 loan; at 7%, only about $301,000, roughly a third less, because more of every payment goes to interest. (Figures are for a 30-year fixed loan.)

Case Study: Mortgage Rates, 2021 to 2026

In the first week of January 2021, the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate was 2.65%, the lowest in the survey's history, which goes back to 1971. Then inflation rose, the Fed began raising its policy rate in March 2022 and reached 5.25%-5.50% by July 2023, and mortgage rates followed: 7.79% by late October 2023 and 7.24% in late October 2026.

Compare what that meant for a $400,000 loan on a 30-year fixed mortgage:

  • At 2.65%: about $1,612 a month.
  • At 7.24%: about $2,726 a month, roughly $1,114 more, or about 69% higher, for exactly the same house.

A commonly cited side effect is that owners with older cheap mortgages hesitate to sell and take on a new loan at a much higher rate, which can reduce the number of homes for sale (analysts disagree about how much).

Mortgage rates also do not move in lockstep with the Fed. In late October 2026 the Fed's target range was 3.75%-4.00%, well below the 7.24% mortgage rate, because lenders price a 30-year loan off the 10-year Treasury yield, which was near 5%, plus a margin.

Why It Matters Outside the Housing Market: An interest rate is a price, and small-looking changes matter a lot over a long loan. If you are buying, a one-point rate change shifts the monthly payment by roughly 10%, so shopping for the best rate and comparing the total cost, not just the monthly payment, is worth the effort. If you are a saver, the same higher rates that hurt borrowers raise what savings accounts and bonds pay.

Sources: Freddie Mac, record low of 2.65% in the week ending January 7, 2021, Freddie Mac rate of 7.79% for the week ending October 26, 2023, Trading Economics, 7.24% on October 29, 2026, The Mortgage Reports, October 23, 2026 (10-year yield), Federal Reserve, July 26, 2023, Federal Reserve, October 28, 2026. Monthly payments are computed with the standard loan formula.

The Takeaway

Interest rates are prices set by the supply of savings and the demand for loans, and they are central to housing and borrowing costs. Watch the long-term rate, not only the Fed's headline rate, when you are about to take out a mortgage.
