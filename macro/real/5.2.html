Money Neutrality, Quantity Theory & Expectations

Real-World Relevance: Medium
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Over the long run, printing more money mostly raises prices rather than making a country richer, and what people expect a central bank to do matters almost as much as what it actually does.

Key Terms

  • Money Neutrality: The idea that, in the long run, a change in the amount of money changes prices and nominal amounts (wages, rents, the numbers on price tags) but not real things such as how many goods are produced or how many people are employed. Doubling every dollar in the economy would, in the end, double prices and leave real incomes roughly where they were. In the short run, money can affect output and jobs; the debate is about how long "short" is.
  • Quantity Theory of Money: The claim that prices track the amount of money. Its equation, M x V = P x Y, says the money supply (M) times how often each unit changes hands per year (velocity, V) equals the price level (P) times real output (Y). If velocity and output are roughly steady, faster money growth means faster inflation.
  • Nominal vs. Real Interest Rate: The nominal rate is the rate printed on the loan or savings account. The real rate is what it is worth after inflation: roughly the nominal rate minus inflation. A 5% savings rate with 3% inflation leaves you only about 2% better off in what you can buy.
  • Fisher Effect: If people expect higher inflation, lenders demand higher nominal interest rates to keep the same real return, so nominal rates rise roughly one-for-one with expected inflation. This is why mortgage and bond rates are very high in high-inflation countries.
  • Central Bank Credibility: How much the public believes the central bank will actually do what it says. Credibility is valuable because wage deals, prices and loan rates are set on what people expect the central bank to do. A believed target for inflation helps hold inflation down on its own.

The Big Picture: More Money, More Inflation

Across countries the link between money growth and inflation is one of the more reliable relationships in economics. A 1995 study by two Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis economists covered 110 countries over 30 years and found money growth and inflation highly correlated, while money growth and growth in real output were essentially uncorrelated (except in a subset of rich countries). Over short periods and in rich countries, the link is looser because the demand for money moves around, so central banks do not steer inflation by watching money growth alone. At extreme rates of money printing, however, the link is hard to miss.

Sources: Minneapolis Fed: Some Monetary Facts.

Think About It

1. A government facing a large bill pays for it by having the central bank create money. Where does the cost end up?

Short answer

On everyone who holds money or earns fixed incomes. More money chasing the same goods raises prices, so the government effectively collects a hidden tax (called an inflation tax) by shrinking the purchasing power of cash and savings.

2. You lend a friend money at 4% interest. Inflation then turns out to be 6% instead of the 1% you both expected. Who gains, you or your friend?

Short answer

Your friend. The real interest rate you earned is about 4% minus 6%, or negative 2%, so the money repaid buys less than what you lent. Lenders protect themselves by raising nominal rates when they expect higher inflation (the Fisher effect).

3. If a central bank announces a low-inflation target and everyone believes it, why might disinflation (bringing inflation down) cost fewer jobs?

Short answer

Workers and firms set wages and prices using their expectations. If they believe inflation will fall, they accept smaller raises and price rises right away, so inflation can fall without a long period of weak demand and extra unemployment.

Case Study: Zimbabwe's Hyperinflation (2007-2009)

Zimbabwe's economy was already in deep trouble in the 2000s, with sharply falling production and a government that could not raise enough taxes to cover its spending. It paid its bills by having the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe create money. Prices rose faster and faster, and the country printed ever larger notes, culminating in a 100 trillion Zimbabwe dollar bill. Wages and savings were worth little within days.

In early 2009 Zimbabwe gave up its own currency and switched to the US dollar and other foreign currencies, and the hyperinflation ended.

Why It Matters Outside Zimbabwe: In a hyperinflation the quantity theory is plain to see: money creation vastly exceeding the growth in goods leads to price rises, and the expectation of more inflation speeds up spending and pushes interest rates up (the Fisher effect). Most countries are nowhere near this, but the same logic explains why governments and central banks are expected to keep a firm line between financing the government and creating money, and why savers in high-inflation countries tend to hold foreign currency, gold or property instead of cash.

Source: Johns Hopkins Magazine, What hyperinflation looks like.

The Takeaway

In the long run, more money mainly means higher prices, not more wealth, and nominal numbers (wages, interest rates, prices) should always be judged against inflation. For households, the practical points are to look at real rather than nominal returns, to expect interest rates to rise when inflation is expected to rise, and to treat a central bank's credibility as an asset that makes everyone's planning easier.
