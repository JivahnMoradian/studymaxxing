AD-AS Equilibrium & Self-Correction

Real-World Relevance: Medium
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

An economy settles where total spending (aggregate demand) meets what businesses are willing to produce (aggregate supply). If that point leaves many people out of work, or pushes the economy beyond what it can sustain, wages and prices should eventually adjust and bring it back; the open question in real life is how long that takes and whether to wait.

Key Terms

  • Short-Run Equilibrium: The overall price level and level of output at which the amount people want to buy equals the amount firms want to produce right now. It can be a good place (a healthy, full-employment economy) or a bad one (a slump or an overheated boom).
  • Potential Output: The most the economy can produce year after year without inflation speeding up, using its workers and equipment at normal, sustainable levels. The unemployment rate at that level is called the natural rate of unemployment, which as of September 2026 is estimated to be around 4.2% for the US.
  • Recessionary Gap vs. Inflationary Gap: A Recessionary Gap means the economy is producing below its potential: factories are idle and unemployment is above the natural rate. An Inflationary Gap means the economy is producing above its potential, which is only possible temporarily (overtime, workers postponing retirement, factories running around the clock) and tends to push prices and wages up.
  • Sticky Wages and Prices: Wages and many prices do not adjust instantly when conditions change, because of contracts, habit and fear of hurting morale. Wage cuts are especially rare. Stickiness is the reason a slump can last for a long time rather than correcting overnight.
  • Self-Correction: The idea that a gap eventually closes by itself. In a slump, high unemployment slowly pushes wages and costs down, which lets firms produce more at any price; in a boom, a tight job market pushes wages and costs up, which cools production. The debate is about speed, not whether the adjustment happens at all.

Think About It

1. Your employer's sales have dropped 10%. It can either cut everyone's pay by 5% or lay off about 1 in 20 workers. Which do many employers choose, and what does that say about unemployment in recessions?

Short answer

Many employers prefer layoffs or freezing pay to cutting it, because pay cuts hurt morale and can trigger resignations of the best workers. This reluctance to cut wages is why unemployment rises in a recession rather than wages simply falling to keep everybody employed.

2. The unemployment rate is far below its normal level, employers cannot fill vacancies, and they start offering higher pay. What is likely to happen to prices, and what does it say about whether the economy can stay in this state?

Short answer

Higher pay raises businesses' costs, and they tend to pass it on in higher prices. This is an inflationary gap: the economy is producing above its sustainable level, and the rising wages and prices are what eventually pull it back.

3. If the economy corrects itself eventually, why do governments and central banks bother to respond to a recession?

Short answer

Because "eventually" can mean years of lost jobs and income for real people. Policy is meant to shorten the slump. Economists disagree about how well it works and about how quickly the economy would recover on its own.

Case Study: The Great Depression, or How Slowly Self-Correction Can Work

Between 1929 and 1933, US real output fell about 29%, consumer prices fell about 25%, and unemployment rose from roughly 4% to about 25%. By the logic of self-correction, mass unemployment and falling prices should have pulled wages and costs down and revived hiring. Recovery came, but only slowly: the economy bottomed in March 1933, suffered a second downturn in 1937, and did not return to full employment until around 1941, during the war buildup.

Economists have competing explanations. One view stresses that spending collapsed: between late 1930 and early 1933 the money supply shrank by almost a third, and during repeated banking panics the Fed did not step in as a lender of last resort. Another, associated with Harold Cole and Lee Ohanian, holds that New Deal policies that kept wages high and restricted competition held back hiring, though other economists dispute the size of this effect.

Why It Matters Outside the 1930s: The economy's tendency to heal itself is real but can be slow, and nobody can be sure how slow in advance. That uncertainty is at the heart of many policy debates: how much to rely on waiting, and how much to use spending, tax or interest-rate policy to speed things up. If you are job hunting in a recession, the economy's "long run" is not much comfort.

Sources: St. Louis Fed: How Bad Was the Great Depression?, Federal Reserve History: The Great Depression, Cole and Ohanian (Minneapolis Fed Working Paper 597), Fed projections, September 16, 2026.

The Takeaway

Economies tend to drift back toward their sustainable level of output, because high unemployment eventually restrains wages and tight job markets eventually push them up. But wages and prices adjust slowly, so a slump can be long and costly, and the real debate is over how much to rely on waiting and how much to try to speed things along.
