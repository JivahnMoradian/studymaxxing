Consumption, Saving & the Multiplier

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

When you get extra income, you split it between spending and saving. Because one person's spending is another person's income, a first round of spending can set off further rounds, which is why a dollar of new spending can add more than a dollar to the economy. How big that effect is depends on how people actually behave, which is hard to predict.

Key Terms

  • Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC): The share of each extra dollar of income that a household spends. If you receive $100 and spend $80, your MPC is 0.8.
  • Marginal Propensity to Save (MPS): The share of each extra dollar that is not spent. It is 1 minus the MPC, so in the example above the MPS is 0.2. For this purpose, paying down debt counts on the "not spent" side, even though it feels like a use of the money.
  • Multiplier Effect: The idea that an initial change in spending ends up changing total income by a larger amount. If a shop owner spends her extra $100 of revenue on a new coat, the tailor's income rises, and the tailor spends part of that, and so on. The textbook formula for the spending multiplier is 1 divided by the MPS, so an MPC of 0.8 gives a multiplier of 5 in a simplified model.
  • Why Real Multipliers Are Smaller: The textbook formula assumes the only leak is saving. In real life, money also leaks to taxes, to imports (spending that goes to foreign producers instead of local ones), and to higher prices rather than more output. Economists therefore estimate multipliers from data, and the estimates differ widely depending on the situation and the method.
  • Temporary vs. Permanent Income: People tend to spend more of a lasting rise in income (a raise) than of a one-time windfall (a rebate check), which they are more likely to save or use to pay off debt. This is one reason the MPC is not a single fixed number.
  • Unplanned Inventories: Goods that firms produced but could not sell as planned. When they pile up, firms cut production and hire fewer workers; when shelves empty faster than planned, firms raise production.

Think About It

1. You get an unexpected $1,000. One person uses it to pay down a credit card, another buys a new couch. From the economy's point of view in the short term, what is the difference?

Short answer

The couch is spending: it becomes income for the store, its workers and its suppliers, who spend part of it in turn. This has a multiplying effect, raising GDP by more than the original $1,000. Paying down debt is not new spending: it reduces what the person owes, which helps them, but it does not directly set off further rounds of spending. So the MPC is higher for the first person than for the second.

2. A factory employing 1,000 people closes in a small town. Why might the shops, restaurants and landlords in town feel the loss more than the wage bill alone suggests?

Short answer

Because the multiplier works in reverse. The laid-off workers cut their spending, which reduces the incomes of local businesses and their employees, who then cut their own spending, and so on. The total loss of local income is larger than the lost wages.

3. Who is likely to have a higher MPC: a household living paycheck to paycheck, or a household with a large savings cushion? Why does this matter for designing a stimulus?

Short answer

The paycheck-to-paycheck household usually spends most of an extra dollar, because it has needs it could not previously afford. The household with a cushion is more likely to save it. So a payment to lower-income households tends to raise spending more per dollar, although who exactly spends depends on circumstances (see the case study).

Case Study: What People Actually Did with Stimulus Checks

In 2008 and again in 2020-21 the US government sent cash directly to households, and economists measured what people did with it.

2008: About $100 billion went to roughly 130 million taxpayers. Because the timing depended on the last digits of Social Security numbers, which are effectively random, researchers (Jonathan Parker, Nicholas Souleles, David Johnson and Robert McClelland) could compare households that had just been paid with similar households that had not yet. They found that households spent about 12-30% of the payment on nondurables (food, fuel and the like) within three months, and about 50-90% once durable goods, mainly vehicles, are included.

2020: Payments of $1,200 (March 2020), $600 (December 2020) and $1,400 (March 2021) totaled $814 billion by the end of 2021. A survey by Olivier Coibion, Yuriy Gorodnichenko and Michael Weber found that people reported spending about 40% of the first payment, saving about 30% and using about 30% to pay down debt.

The two results are not directly comparable. One uses actual spending records and the other uses self-reports, and in spring 2020 many stores were closed and the saving rate jumped, so there was less to spend money on.

Why It Matters Outside the Textbook: The MPC is not a fixed number. It depends on who receives the money, whether they think it is temporary, how much debt they carry, and what they are able to buy. That is why economists differ on how much a given stimulus adds to the economy, and why a government cannot simply multiply a payment by a formula. The same logic applies to a bonus or a tax refund of your own: a "windfall" tends to be spent differently from regular income.

Sources: NBER Digest on the 2008 stimulus payments (Parker, Souleles, Johnson, McClelland), NBER Digest on 2020 stimulus payments (Coibion, Gorodnichenko, Weber), Fortune on the three rounds of payments, totaling $814 billion, BEA Personal Income and Outlays, April 2020.

The Takeaway

Spending and saving decisions are at the heart of the economy: what you do with an extra dollar becomes someone else's income. The multiplier idea is real and useful, but its size depends on who gets the money, how they view it and what the economy looks like at the time, so it is best understood as a range rather than a single number.
