Aggregate Demand

Real-World Relevance: Medium
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Aggregate demand is the total amount of goods and services everyone in an economy (households, businesses, the government and foreign buyers) wants to buy at a given overall price level. When that total spending falls, businesses sell less and cut jobs; when it rises faster than the economy can produce, prices tend to rise.

Key Terms

  • Aggregate Demand (AD): Total planned spending on a country's output, added up across the whole economy. Think of it as the sum of every shopping cart in the country: yours, your employer's, the city's and your overseas customers'.
  • The four buyers (C, I, G, NX): Consumption is household spending on things like groceries, cars and rent; investment is business spending on equipment and buildings, plus new home construction (buying shares or bonds does not count); government spending is purchases of goods and services such as roads, schools and soldiers' pay (pensions and benefit payments are not counted, because they are transfers rather than purchases); net exports are what foreigners buy from us minus what we buy from them.
  • Wealth Effect: When the value of what people own (homes, shares) falls, they tend to spend less, even if their paycheck has not changed. When their assets rise, they tend to spend more. This is one reason falling house prices or a stock crash can slow the whole economy.
  • Movement Along vs. Shift of AD: A change in the overall price level moves us along a given AD curve. A change in anything else that affects spending (confidence, wealth, interest rates, taxes, foreign incomes) shifts the whole curve left or right.
  • AD vs. the demand for one product: The demand for pizza falls as pizza gets pricier because people switch to burgers. AD is about all goods at once, so there is nothing cheaper to switch to; its downward slope comes from other effects, such as higher prices eroding what savings can buy and making domestic goods less competitive against imports.

Think About It

1. The stock market drops 25%, and your retirement account falls with it. Your pay is unchanged. Do you change how much you spend, and what happens if millions of people do the same?

Short answer

Many people would cut back somewhat, because they feel poorer and have less cushion; this is the wealth effect. If millions do it, total consumer spending falls, which is a leftward shift of aggregate demand. Businesses then sell less and may cut hiring.

2. Several major trading partners go into recession. You work for a US company that sells machinery abroad. What is the likely effect on your company and on the US economy?

Short answer

Foreign buyers have less income, so they buy fewer US exports. Net exports fall, which shifts US aggregate demand left, so your company may see fewer orders and possibly layoffs. This is how a downturn abroad spreads to workers at home.

3. Is a store's "20% off everything" sale the same thing as a drop in the economy's price level that moves us along the AD curve?

Short answer

No. One store's sale pulls shoppers away from other stores, so it is a change in relative prices. A movement along AD means the average price of everything in the economy changes together, so there is nothing cheaper to switch to; the change in spending comes instead from what people's money and savings can buy and how domestic goods compare with imports.

Case Study: The Collapse in Household Spending, 2007-09

Between 2007 and 2009, American households lost a large share of what they thought they owned. Between mid-2006 and mid-2009, average US home prices dropped roughly 30%, and the S&P 500 lost about 57% of its value between its October 2007 high and its March 2009 low. The net worth of US households fell by roughly $14 trillion.

People responded in the way the wealth effect predicts: they pulled back. The household saving rate rose from around 2% of income to over 6%. Falling spending meant falling sales, and businesses cut investment and jobs: real GDP fell about 4% from peak to trough, and unemployment rose from 5% to 10%.

This was not mainly a story about firms being unable to produce. Factories, offices and workers were available. What dried up was spending: aggregate demand shifted left, and output and jobs fell with it.

Why It Matters Outside the United States: Your home is probably your biggest asset, so what happens to house prices can change how freely you and your neighbors spend. When enough households feel poorer at the same time, the spending cut becomes a downturn that costs people their jobs, even people whose own finances were sound.

The Takeaway

Aggregate demand is the economy's total spending, and it is driven by many individual decisions that tend to move together: when people and businesses feel confident, spending rises; when they feel poorer or uncertain, it falls. Because one person's spending is another person's income, a drop in demand can spread quickly. Much of macroeconomic policy is about keeping that total spending steady.
