6.2

The Foreign Exchange Market

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

An exchange rate is simply the price of one currency in another, and like any price it moves with supply and demand. It decides what your holiday abroad costs, what imported goods cost at home, and whether a country's exporters and tourist industry thrive or struggle.

Key Terms

  • Exchange Rate: How much of one currency you must pay to get one unit of another. It can be quoted either way: if one euro costs $1.18, then one dollar buys about 0.85 euros (the two quotes are reciprocals). Always check which currency is being priced.
  • Appreciation vs. Depreciation: A currency appreciates when it becomes worth more in terms of another (a dollar buys more euros), and depreciates when it becomes worth less. If the dollar appreciates against the euro, the euro depreciates against the dollar. Appreciation makes foreign goods and travel cheaper for you but your exports more expensive for foreigners; depreciation does the opposite.
  • Floating Exchange Rate: The rate is set freely by buyers and sellers in the currency market, and moves every day. The US, the euro area, Japan, the UK and Switzerland all float.
  • Pegged (Fixed) Exchange Rate: A government or central bank commits to holding its currency at a set rate against another, usually by buying or selling currency whenever the rate drifts. Hong Kong, for instance, pegs to the US dollar.
  • Currency Conversion Cost: The "real" price when you convert money also includes a markup. Banks, card issuers, exchange kiosks and merchants each add fees or build a margin into the rate they give you.
  • Capital Mobility: How easily money moves in and out of a country. When it is high, investors chase higher interest rates, which makes a peg hard to defend if domestic rates differ from the anchor country's.
  • Impossible Trinity: A country cannot have a fixed exchange rate, free movement of capital and independent interest-rate policy all at once; it must give up one. That is why countries sharing the euro cannot set their own interest rates.

The Big Picture: A Weaker Dollar Made Europe More Expensive for Americans in 2025

The dollar fell about 10% against a basket of major currencies in the first half of 2025, its worst half-year since 1973, when major currencies began floating freely. Reasons cited by market commentators included uncertainty over US tariff policy, worries about US government debt and inflation, and expectations of Fed rate cuts. The euro rose about 13.8% against the dollar in those six months, from about $1.04 to about $1.18.

For a traveller the arithmetic is direct. A dinner costing €100 cost about $104 at the start of 2025 and about $118 by July, with no change in the restaurant's menu price. The reverse also holds: a Parisian buying a $100 US product paid about €96 in January and about €85 in July, so US goods got cheaper for Europeans. Exchange-rate moves can easily outweigh the price differences between countries that people usually shop around for.

Sources: Outlook Business on the dollar's first half of 2025.

Think About It

1. The euro goes from $1.04 to $1.18. You are booking a week in Italy. Is the trip cheaper or more expensive, and by how much for a €2,000 bill?

Short answer

More expensive, because the euro became more expensive in dollars: €2,000 costs about $2,080 at $1.04 and about $2,360 at $1.18, roughly $280 (13%) more for exactly the same trip. When the dollar weakens, foreign holidays and imports cost more; when it strengthens, they cost less.

2. At a shop in Lisbon the card machine asks: "Pay in US dollars or in euros?" Which should you choose, and why?

Short answer

Usually in euros (the local currency). Choosing dollars lets the merchant's provider do the conversion at its own rate, which tends to be worse than the rate your card network and bank would apply, and your card's foreign-transaction fee may still apply on top. Visa notes that you must be shown the amount in both currencies, the rate and any fees, and that you can decline.

3. Hong Kong's dollar is tied to the US dollar. If the Federal Reserve raises interest rates, what pressure does Hong Kong face?

Short answer

Hong Kong has to follow. If its rates stayed lower than US rates, investors would sell Hong Kong dollars to earn more in US dollars, pushing the currency off its peg. So defending a peg means giving up independent control of your own interest rates.

Case Study: The Swiss Franc Shock (January 15, 2015)

In September 2011, after investors rushed into the franc during the euro crisis, the Swiss National Bank (SNB, Switzerland's central bank) promised not to let the franc rise above CHF 1.20 per euro, and enforced this by printing francs and buying euros. By 2015 it had piled up foreign currency reserves of over CHF 500 billion, about 70% of Swiss annual output.

On January 15, 2015, with no warning, the SNB dropped the limit. The franc leapt by as much as about 30% against the euro and the dollar. Even after it calmed down to roughly CHF 1.05 to 1.08 per euro, Swiss products cost euro-area buyers at least 10% more than before. Swiss watchmakers' shares fell more than 16% on the day.

The costs showed up over the following year: the government's 2015 growth forecast was cut from 2.1% to 0.8%, unemployment rose, and hotel stays fell. There were winners, though: Swiss consumers' cross-border shopping rose about 8%, since foreign goods were suddenly cheaper in francs, and imports and foreign holidays became cheaper.

Why It Matters Outside Switzerland: A strong currency is good for buyers of foreign goods and bad for those who sell abroad or compete with imports. The same move that cuts a Swiss household's cost of living can cost its neighbours their jobs. It also shows that pegs and caps can end suddenly, with big losses for people who relied on them.

Sources: Swissinfo, "Counting the cost of the franc shock" (January 2016), CNBC.

Case Study: A Peg in Practice, the Hong Kong Dollar

Hong Kong has tied its currency to the US dollar since 1983. The Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA, its central bank) has promised to buy US dollars at HK$7.75 and sell them at HK$7.85, so the currency floats only within that narrow band.

The defence is automatic. If the Hong Kong dollar drops to the weak edge of 7.85, the HKMA has to step in, selling US dollars and taking Hong Kong dollars out of circulation. The shrinking supply of Hong Kong dollars in the banking system pushes up local interest rates and draws money back into the currency. This happened in summer 2025, when US interest rates were higher than Hong Kong's, so investors earned more by selling Hong Kong dollars for US dollars.

What people in Hong Kong get for this is a stable currency in a place that trades and borrows heavily in dollars; what they give up is a monetary policy set for their own conditions, since local rates must track US ones.

Why It Matters Outside Hong Kong: Many countries peg, or semi-peg, to the dollar or the euro. A peg gives holidaymakers, importers and borrowers certainty, but it ties local interest rates to a foreign central bank, and the cost shows up in mortgage rates and in how many dollars the central bank must hold in reserve.

Sources: The Standard (Hong Kong).

Case Study: When a Currency Collapses, Turkey 2021-22

In September 2021 Turkey's central bank, under pressure from President Erdogan, cut its key interest rate from 19% to 15%, even though inflation was already near 20%. Erdogan argued, against the usual view of most economists, that lower interest rates would reduce inflation. By November 2021 the lira had hit a record low of about 12.49 per US dollar, about 40% weaker for the year. Because Turkey imports energy and many raw materials, a cheaper lira raised their lira prices, and by October 2022 annual inflation reached 85.5%.

Why It Matters Outside Turkey: For a country that imports a lot, a falling currency is not a "cheap" bargain for its own residents. It is a price rise on imports and, indirectly, on almost everything else.

Sources: Al Jazeera, November 2021, CNBC, November 2022.

The Takeaway

Exchange rates are prices, and exchange rate fluctuations transfer money between groups: a strong currency benefits domestic consumers and importers but hurts exporters, while a weak currency hurts consumers and importers but benefits exporters, so it can stimulate economic growth. For your own wallet, the practical rules are simple: check which way the rate is quoted, expect the rate you actually get to include a markup, pay in the local currency on your card, and remember that a 10% currency swing can matter more than most of the bargains you hunt for.
