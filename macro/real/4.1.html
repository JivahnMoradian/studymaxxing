4.1

Money, Banking & the Money Supply

Real-World Relevance: Medium
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Money is whatever people will accept in payment, and most of it today is not paper cash but numbers in bank accounts. Banks keep only a fraction of your deposits on hand and lend the rest out, which is how the system creates money, and also why a bank can fail if too many people want their money back at once.

Key Terms

  • Money: Anything widely accepted for payment. It does three jobs: a medium of exchange (you can buy things with it), a unit of account (prices are quoted in it) and a store of value (it keeps its worth until you spend it, though inflation erodes that).
  • Fiat Money: Money that has value because the government says it is legal tender and people trust it, not because it is backed by gold or another commodity. The US dollar has been fiat money since August 15, 1971, when President Nixon closed the "gold window" and foreign governments could no longer swap dollars for gold.
  • Fractional-Reserve Banking: A bank keeps only a fraction of deposits as cash or reserves and lends out the rest. Your deposit is a claim on the bank, not a pile of your own bills sitting in a vault.
  • Bank Run: Many depositors try to withdraw at the same time. Even a healthy bank cannot pay everyone at once, because its money is tied up in loans and bonds, so the fear of a run can cause one.
  • Deposit Insurance: A government guarantee that you get your money back if the bank fails. In the US the FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation) covers $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category (for example single accounts, joint accounts and retirement accounts are counted separately).

Think About It

1. You deposit $1,000 at your bank. Is that $1,000 sitting in the vault with your name on it?

Short answer

No. Your bank keeps part of it as reserves and lends or invests the rest. What you own is a claim to $1,000 on demand, and the bank can honor it because on any normal day only a small share of customers withdraw at once.

2. You are opening a checking, savings or CD account and have $400,000 to deposit. What should you check, and what could you do?

Short answer

First, make sure the bank is FDIC insured (look for the FDIC sign or search the bank on the FDIC's BankFind tool), because deposit insurance is what makes the account safe if the bank fails. Coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, so with $400,000 in a single-owner account $250,000 is insured and the other $150,000 is not. If you are ever lucky enough to have more than $250,000 saved, split it across several FDIC-insured banks, or use different ownership categories (for example a joint account), so that each amount sits under its own limit.

3. Treasury bills, stocks and crypto sit in your account at a bank. Does FDIC insurance cover them?

Short answer

No. FDIC insurance covers deposits (checking, savings, money market deposit accounts and certificates of deposit), not stocks, bonds, mutual funds, Treasuries or crypto, even if the bank sold them to you. Those carry their own risks.

Sources: FDIC, Understanding Deposit Insurance, Federal Reserve History, end of gold convertibility.

Case Study: Silicon Valley Bank, March 2023

At the end of 2022, Silicon Valley Bank (SVB) held about $209 billion in assets, funded mostly by deposits from tech start-ups and the venture-capital firms that backed them. Their balances were far above the $250,000 insurance limit: the bank estimated that about 86% of its deposits were uninsured.

SVB had put about $117 billion of that money into bonds, much of it long-term and bought when interest rates were near zero. When the Fed raised rates in 2022, the market value of those older, low-interest bonds fell (see chapter 4.2), leaving unrealized losses of more than $15 billion on its held-to-maturity bonds alone. As long as the bank never had to sell them, the losses stayed on paper.

On March 8, 2023, SVB announced it had sold over $21 billion of securities (locking in losses) and needed to raise new capital. Depositors rushed to withdraw about $42 billion on March 9, with roughly $100 billion more requested the next morning. This represented about 81% of the bank's deposits in under two days. Regulators closed the bank on March 10, making SVB the largest US bank failure since 2008.

On March 12, regulators announced that all depositors, including those above $250,000, would have access to their money. Supporters said this stopped a panic from spreading to other banks. Critics argued that rescuing uninsured depositors encourages large depositors and banks to take risks, because they now expect to be bailed out. Both points are debated; the facts of the rescue are not.

Why It Matters Outside Silicon Valley: A bank can be solvent on paper and still fail if depositors lose confidence, because banks promise instant access to money they have lent out. For you, the practical rules are simple: keep each bank balance under the insurance limit (or spread it across banks or ownership categories), check that your account is a deposit and not an investment product, and remember that, by the FDIC's account, no depositor has lost a penny of insured funds since it was founded in 1933.

Sources: Wikipedia, Collapse of Silicon Valley Bank, CNBC on Fed Vice Chair Barr's testimony, FDIC, Understanding Deposit Insurance, FDIC Chairman's remarks on unrealized securities losses, February 2023.

The Takeaway

Most of what we call money is a promise from a bank, and the promise works because deposit insurance and the central bank stand behind it. That makes ordinary banks very safe for typical savers, and the rules worth remembering are the $250,000 limit and the difference between a deposit and an investment.
