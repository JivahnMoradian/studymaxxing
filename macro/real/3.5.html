Fiscal Policy

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Fiscal policy is the government's use of its spending and taxes to influence the economy: spending more or taxing less to support a weak economy, and (in principle) the reverse to cool an overheated one. It touches everyone, through your tax bill, benefits, public services and the national debt, and people disagree about when and how much to use it.

Key Terms

  • Fiscal Policy: Changes in government spending and taxes aimed at influencing overall demand, jobs and prices. It is run by the elected government (in the US, Congress and the President), unlike monetary policy, which is run by the central bank.
  • Discretionary vs. Automatic: Discretionary fiscal policy means lawmakers deliberately pass a new law (a stimulus package, a tax cut). Automatic Stabilizers need no new law: when the economy weakens, tax collections fall and benefits such as unemployment insurance rise on their own, which cushions the drop in people's incomes; in a boom the process reverses.
  • Deficit vs. Debt: The deficit is how much the government spends in one year beyond what it collects in revenue. The Debt is the total it owes from all past borrowing. Running a deficit adds to the debt; a surplus (collecting more than it spends) would reduce it.
  • Expansionary vs. Contractionary: Expansionary policy (more spending or lower taxes) aims to raise demand in a weak economy and usually widens the deficit. Contractionary policy (less spending or higher taxes) aims to reduce demand, for example to cool inflation or to bring the deficit down.
  • Crowding Out: The concern that when the government borrows heavily, it competes with businesses and households for loans, pushing interest rates up and discouraging private spending and investment. How large this effect is, especially in a weak economy, is debated.
  • Policy Lags: It takes time to notice a problem, pass a law and spend the money. By the time a measure takes effect, the economy may already have changed.

Think About It

1. In a deep recession, a government considers two options: a one-time tax rebate to households or a program to build roads and bridges. Which is likely to take effect faster, and which is more certain to be spent?

Short answer

A rebate can reach people within months, but households may save or use part of it to pay debt (see the 2008 and 2020 findings in the real-life section of chapter 3.4). Construction spending is certain to be spent once contracted, but projects usually take longer to get underway. Each has a different trade-off between speed and certainty.

2. The government runs a deficit of $1 trillion this year, and the debt was $30 trillion at the start of the year. What is the debt at the end of the year, all else equal?

Short answer

About $31 trillion. The deficit is the one-year shortfall, and the debt is the accumulated total, so each year's deficit is added to the debt (interest and other adjustments aside). A deficit makes the debt larger; it does not mean the debt is $1 trillion.

3. Why can a government's deficit grow during a recession even if lawmakers pass no new laws?

Short answer

Because of automatic stabilizers. Incomes and profits fall, so tax revenue falls, while more people claim unemployment and other benefits, so spending rises. The deficit widens by itself, which supports incomes during the downturn.

Case Study: The 2009 Stimulus and Why Economists Disagree on Its Size

In February 2009, with unemployment rising quickly, the US enacted the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act (ARRA), a package of tax cuts, aid to states, and spending on infrastructure and benefits. It was estimated at $787 billion when passed.

How much did it help? The Congressional Budget Office (CBO), which is nonpartisan, estimated that for the last quarter of 2011 the act had raised real GDP by 0.2% to 1.5% and lowered the unemployment rate by 0.2 to 1.1 percentage points relative to a scenario in which the act had never passed. The ranges are wide because economists cannot observe the economy without the stimulus, so they have to estimate what would have happened.

That uncertainty is the general problem of measuring the "fiscal multiplier," the amount of output generated per dollar of government spending. In a widely cited survey, economist Valerie Ramey concluded that the multiplier for temporary, deficit-financed government purchases in the US most plausibly falls between about 0.8 and 1.5, while the data cannot rule out 0.5 or 2.0. Multipliers tend to look larger when the economy has plenty of idle workers and equipment. Supporters of stimulus point to these results as evidence it helps in deep slumps; critics point to the low end of the ranges, the added debt and the delays before the money was spent.

Why It Matters Outside 2009: Debates about stimulus, infrastructure and tax cuts often rely on a single claimed number for what each dollar "creates." The honest answer is a range that depends on timing, design and economic conditions. When you hear a confident figure, ask what it is being compared with and how the estimate was made.

Sources: CBO: Estimates of ARRA's economic impact (2012), Ramey, "Can Government Purchases Stimulate the Economy?" (2011).

Case Study: Automatic Stabilizers, the Stimulus Nobody Votes on

Not all fiscal policy involves a headline vote. When the 2007-09 recession hit, incomes and profits fell, so tax collections dropped, while more people qualified for unemployment benefits and other support. The CBO estimated that these automatic changes added about $386 billion to the federal deficit in fiscal year 2012 alone, about 2.3% of potential GDP (the economy's capacity).

Automatic stabilizers work immediately, with no debate or delay. Laid-off workers keep part of their income, so they can keep paying rent and buying food, and the shops they use keep some of their customers. They also work in reverse: as the economy recovers, tax revenue rises and benefit payments fall. Economists generally agree that they stabilize the economy, and differ on how large the programs should be.

Why It Matters Outside This Episode: If you ever receive unemployment benefits, or if your employer's customers do, you are directly in contact with fiscal policy. It also explains a common source of confusion: a rising deficit in a recession is partly a symptom of the downturn, not only a result of choices lawmakers made.

Sources: CBO: The Effects of Automatic Stabilizers on the Federal Budget as of 2013.

Case Study: Pandemic Relief and the Inflation Debate, 2020-21

In 2020 and 2021 the US government approved relief on a scale not seen in peacetime: three rounds of direct payments worth $814 billion (see the real-life section of chapter 3.4), alongside expanded unemployment benefits and other aid. As reported at the time, stimulus payments lifted about 11.7 million people out of poverty and expanded unemployment benefits kept about 5.5 million from falling into it.

By 2021, however, inflation was rising, and economists argued about how much of it came from the relief. Researchers at the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco estimated in 2022 that pandemic income transfers raised inflation by roughly 3 percentage points by the end of 2021, while cautioning that the estimate was highly uncertain and at the high end of other studies. Others, such as Bernanke and Blanchard (see the real-life section of chapter 3.2), put more weight on supply shocks. In short, most economists accept that very large fiscal support can add to inflation when the economy cannot easily produce more, but they disagree on how large that effect was against the risk of leaving households without income.

Why It Matters Outside the Pandemic: Fiscal policy involves trade-offs, not free lunches. Support for households can limit hardship and speed up a recovery, but if it is too large relative to what the economy can supply it can contribute to higher prices. Which risk you consider bigger depends in part on how you weigh jobs and income now against prices later.

Sources: Fortune on the three rounds of payments, totaling $814 billion, Census poverty report of September 14, 2021, as reported by FOX, FRBSF Economic Letter, March 2022, Bernanke and Blanchard (PIIE).

The Takeaway

Fiscal policy is the government's tool for steering spending and incomes. Automatic stabilizers cushion downturns without anyone having to vote; discretionary packages can add more support but arrive with delays and uncertain effects. How much to use it, and how to weigh debt, jobs and prices against one another, is a matter of real disagreement, so treat confident claims in either direction with some caution.
