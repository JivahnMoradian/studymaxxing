The Phillips Curve

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

The Phillips curve is the idea that, in the short run, lower unemployment tends to come with higher inflation and vice versa. In the long run, there is no lasting trade: once people expect inflation, unemployment returns to its normal level and only the higher inflation remains.

Key Terms

  • Short-Run Phillips Curve: The downward-sloping relationship between unemployment and inflation while people's expectations of inflation are held fixed. A booming economy with few unemployed workers tends to push wages and prices up faster; a weak economy pulls them down.
  • Natural Rate of Unemployment: The level of unemployment the economy settles at when it is neither overheating nor in a slump, because there are always people between jobs and mismatches between workers and openings. The Federal Reserve's own forecasters put it at about 4.2% in the long run (September 2026 projections).
  • Expected Inflation: The inflation rate that workers, firms and lenders assume when they sign wage deals, set prices and fix interest rates. When expectations rise, the whole short-run curve shifts up: the same unemployment now comes with more inflation.
  • Long-Run Phillips Curve: A vertical line at the natural rate. It says that, once expectations have caught up, a country cannot buy permanently lower unemployment with permanently higher inflation.
  • Stagflation: High inflation and high unemployment at the same time. The simple trade-off says this should not happen; it does when the supply side of the economy takes a hit (such as an oil price spike) or when expectations of inflation have become entrenched.

Sources: FOMC projections, September 16, 2026.

Think About It

1. A central bank raises interest rates sharply to bring inflation down. What do you expect to happen to jobs in the short run, and why?

Short answer

Unemployment usually rises for a while. Higher rates reduce borrowing and spending, firms hire less, and the weaker job market slows wage and price increases. That is the short-run trade-off working in reverse: lower inflation is bought with a temporary loss of jobs.

2. In your country, everyone starts to expect 8% inflation a year. Your employer gives raises of 8% and your landlord raises rent 8%. Has anyone gained?

Short answer

No. When wages and prices both rise by what everyone already expected, real purchasing power and hiring are unchanged. That is why higher inflation cannot keep unemployment lower for long: it only helps while it is a surprise.

3. Why can an oil price spike produce both rising prices and rising unemployment at once?

Short answer

Higher energy costs raise firms' costs, so prices go up, and they also make some production unprofitable, so output and jobs fall. The whole short-run curve shifts up (worse on both counts), which a simple "pick a point on the curve" view of the trade-off cannot explain.

Case Study: The 1970s and Volcker's Disinflation

In the mid-1960s inflation was about 1% and unemployment about 5%. By about 1974 inflation was above 12% and unemployment above 7%, a combination many economists had thought impossible. Oil prices roughly quadrupled during the 1973-74 embargo and tripled again after the 1979 Iranian revolution, and people came to expect continuing inflation. Inflation averaged about 11% in 1979 and 13.5% in 1980.

When Paul Volcker became Fed chair in August 1979, he let interest rates rise steeply; the federal funds rate reached roughly 20% in late 1980. The result was two recessions in quick succession, and unemployment peaked near 11% in late 1982. But inflation fell to about 6% by early 1982 and under 4% in 1983. The price of beating inflation was nearly doubling the unemployment rate, a clear picture of the short-run trade-off and of why the Fed later cared so much about keeping inflation expectations anchored.

Why It Matters Outside the 1970s: The economy-wide cost of bringing inflation down depends partly on whether people believe the central bank will do it. If you keep hearing "inflation is the central bank's top priority" and your employer, landlord and mortgage lender all believe it, price and wage increases ease with less pain. If they do not, getting the same result takes more unemployment.

Sources: Federal Reserve History: Volcker's announcement of anti-inflation measures, Federal Reserve History: The Great Inflation, BLS Monthly Labor Review (annual inflation 1979-81).

Case Study: The Trade-Off After 2020, a "Soft Landing"?

Before the pandemic, the link between unemployment and inflation in the US had looked weak for years. After 2020 inflation rose to around 6% by mid-2022, and job openings per unemployed person roughly doubled. Standard models predicted that bringing inflation down would require a sharp rise in unemployment. That is not what happened: between mid-2022 and the end of 2023, vacancies and inflation dropped sharply, yet unemployment barely moved.

Economists disagree about why. One view is that cooling demand eliminated vacancies first, with little effect on unemployment. Another is that supply problems (backed-up supply chains, energy prices) were a large part of the inflation surge and eased by themselves, so less demand-cutting was needed.

Why It Matters Outside the US: The short-run trade-off is real but not a fixed menu. It moves around with supply shocks, expectations and the state of the job market. Be wary of anyone who promises that unemployment can be pushed down without any inflation, or inflation fixed without any job losses.

Sources: Cleveland Fed: Understanding Post-Pandemic Surprises in Inflation and the Labor Market, .

The Takeaway

In the short run, a hot economy tends to mean lower unemployment and higher inflation, and a cold one the reverse. But the trade-off is unstable: it shifts with supply shocks and with what people expect. Over the long run the economy returns to its natural rate of unemployment whatever the inflation rate, so the lasting effect of running the economy hot is just higher prices. That is why central banks work so hard to keep inflation expectations steady.
