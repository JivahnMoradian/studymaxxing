Financial Assets & Time Value of Money

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar later, because money can earn interest and prices tend to rise. Compound interest and inflation both work quietly over decades, which makes them two of the most useful ideas in personal finance, whether you are saving, investing or in debt.

Key Terms

  • Compound Interest: Interest earned on your earlier interest, not just on your original deposit. Money growing at rate r for n years multiplies by (1 + r)n. It works for you on savings and against you on debt.
  • Rule of 70: A shortcut for doubling time. Divide 70 by the annual growth rate in percent. At 7% a year money doubles in about 10 years; at 2% inflation prices double in about 35 years. It is very accurate near 2% and still a good estimate at higher rates.
  • Nominal vs. Real Return: The nominal return is the number the bank or fund quotes. The real return is what is left after subtracting inflation, which is what your purchasing power actually does. Savings paying 4% while prices rise 3.7% a year grow your buying power by only about 0.3% a year.
  • Present Value: What a future sum of money is worth today. It is found by discounting: dividing by (1 + r)n. At 5%, $1,000 promised in 10 years is worth about $614 today.
  • Bond: A loan you make to a government or company. It pays fixed interest and returns your principal on a set date. Its market price moves opposite to interest rates: when new bonds pay more, older bonds that pay less become worth less to buyers.
  • Stock: A share of ownership in a company. Unlike a bond, it promises nothing fixed, so it carries more risk and has historically earned more over long periods.
  • Diversification: Spreading money across many assets so that no single failure ruins you.

The Big Picture: Three Numbers Worth Remembering

  1. Rule of 70 for money: years to double = 70 divided by the percent rate.
  2. Rule of 70 for prices: the same rule tells you how fast your money loses half its buying power. At 4% inflation, prices double in 17.5 years; at 1%, in about 70.
  3. Rate of Return Minus Inflation: compare what an account pays to inflation, not to zero. As of August 2026 the FDIC's national average savings rate was 0.38%, while consumer prices rose 3.7% over the 12 months to September 2026, so money in a typical savings account was losing buying power.

Sources: Economics Network on the rule of 70, FDIC national average savings rate as of August 17, 2026, BLS September 2026 CPI as reported by Securities.io.

Think About It

1. A credit card charges 22% a year and you carry a balance without paying it down. Roughly how long until the debt doubles?

Short answer

70 divided by 22 is about 3 years. Compounding works on debt just as it does on savings, which is why high-interest balances grow so fast. For comparison, the Federal Reserve reports an average rate of 22.15% on card accounts that were charged interest in 2023.

2. You are offered a bond paying a fixed 2% when new bonds pay 4.5%. Would you pay full price for it?

Short answer

No. Nobody would pay full price for 2% when 4.5% is available, so the older bond's price must fall until its overall return catches up. That is the rule that bond prices move opposite to interest rates.

3. Your savings account pays 1% and inflation is 4%. Are you getting richer?

Short answer

No. Your balance grows by 1% but prices grow by 4%, so your real return is about minus 3% a year. After 10 years $10,000 buys what roughly $7,500 would have bought at the start.

Sources: Federal Reserve G.19 consumer credit release (2022 and 2023 rates). Calculations in the answers are simple compounding.

Case Study 1: Why Starting to Save Early Beats Saving More Later

Suppose two people both earn an average 6% a year on their retirement savings (a plausible long-run assumption for a balanced portfolio, though nobody can promise any number). Ignore fees and taxes.

  • Early Elena saves $3,600 a year from age 25 to 34, ten years, and then never adds another dollar. She puts in $36,000 in total.
  • Late Leo waits until 35 and then saves the same $3,600 a year for 30 years, to age 64. He puts in $108,000, three times as much.

At 65, Elena has about $273,000 and Leo about $285,000. Leo put in three times as much money and ends up only about 4% ahead. The reason is compounding: Elena's early dollars have 30 to 40 years to multiply, doubling roughly every 12 years at 6% (70 divided by 6 is about 12). Each dollar she saves at 25 becomes about 10 dollars by 65, while a dollar saved at 55 becomes only about 1.8.

Why It Matters Outside This Example: Time is worth more than size. Small amounts started in your twenties can match much larger amounts started in your thirties. The same math is why carrying credit card debt hurts: you are paying interest compounding at 20% or more instead of earning it.

The two savers are an illustration computed with year-end annual contributions and a constant 6% return; real returns vary year to year.

Case Study 2: 1% Inflation versus 4%

Inflation is the quiet cost of holding cash. Here is what $10,000 of purchasing power becomes under two inflation rates:

  • At 1% a year: after 10 years it buys what about $9,050 did; after 20 years about $8,200.
  • At 4% a year: after 10 years it buys what about $6,760 did; after 20 years about $4,560.

The rule of 70 gives the same message faster: at 4%, prices double in about 17.5 years, so your cash loses half its buying power in less than two decades. At 2%, the Fed's target, it takes about 35 years.

Today's numbers (as of October 2026): consumer prices rose 3.7% over the 12 months to September, while the FDIC's national average savings rate was 0.38% in August 2026. On $10,000 left in an average account for a year, that is about $38 of interest against about $370 of lost purchasing power. Higher-yield accounts, certificates of deposit and Treasury securities often pay several percent and can close much of that gap, but only if you look for them.

Why It Matters Outside This Example: Inflation is a tax on holding cash. Keep an emergency fund in a safe, insured account, but money you will not need for years has to earn at least the inflation rate to keep its value.

Sources: BLS September 2026 CPI as reported by Securities.io, FDIC national average savings rate, Economics Network on the rule of 70. Purchasing-power figures are computed by dividing by (1 + inflation)years.

Case Study 3: 2022, when "Safe" Bonds Lost 13%

Bonds have a reputation for safety, but 2022 showed that safe from default is not safe from price swings. The Fed began raising its target rate in March 2022 from near zero, and by July 2023 it stood at 5.25%-5.50%. New bonds started paying much more, so older bonds paying less lost value. The Bloomberg US Aggregate Bond Index, a broad measure of the US bond market, fell about 13% in 2022.

A simple example shows why. A 10-year bond with a $1,000 face value and a 2% coupon ($20 a year) is worth $1,000 when market rates are 2%. If rates rise to 4.5%, a buyer will pay only about $802 for it, a 20% loss, because the same $1,000 could buy a bond paying $45 a year. Longer bonds fall more (about 5% for a 2-year bond, 41% for a 30-year bond). Hold to maturity and you still get your $1,000 back; sell early and you take the loss.

Banks hold bonds too: the FDIC estimated about $620 billion of unrealized losses on securities at US banks at the end of 2022, which is what Silicon Valley Bank ran into in 2023 (see chapter 4.1).

Why It Matters Outside Bond Markets: When interest rates rise, the price of existing bonds, and often of other assets, tends to fall. If you own a bond fund, know its "duration" (roughly, how long its bonds take to pay you back; longer means more sensitive to rate changes), and match the bonds you hold to when you will need the money.

Sources: CNBC on 2022 bond losses, Federal Reserve, March 16, 2022, Federal Reserve, July 26, 2023, FDIC remarks, February 2023. Bond prices are computed from the standard present-value formula.

The Takeaway

Time and rates drive almost everything in personal finance: compounding rewards patience, inflation punishes idle cash, and bond prices fall when rates rise. Learn the rule of 70, always compare a return with inflation, and remember that nobody can promise a particular return.
