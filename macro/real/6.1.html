6.1

Balance of Payments

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

A country's balance of payments is its record of every payment between its residents and the rest of the world. Because every dollar that leaves must come back as either a purchase of goods or an investment in assets, "a trade deficit" and "foreigners are lending to or investing in us" are two descriptions of the same thing.

Key Terms

  • Trade Deficit vs. Trade Surplus: A deficit means a country buys more goods and services from abroad (imports) than it sells abroad (exports); a surplus is the reverse. The US runs a deficit; Germany and China run surpluses.
  • Current Account: The broadest record of the flow of income between a country and the world. It adds up the trade balance, the investment income and wages earned abroad minus those paid to foreigners, and gifts such as money sent home by workers (remittances). It is usually close to the trade balance, but not identical to it.
  • Capital and Financial Account: The record of ownership moving across borders: foreigners buying a country's stocks, bonds, homes and companies (money in), and its residents buying foreign ones (money out).
  • Why the Two Mirror Each Other: The current account and the financial account add up to roughly zero. A country spending more abroad than it earns (a current account deficit) must pay for the difference by selling assets or borrowing from foreigners (a financial inflow). A surplus country does the opposite: it lends or invests its extra earnings abroad.
  • Net International Investment Position (NIIP): What a country owns abroad minus what foreigners own in it. A negative figure means the country is a net debtor to the world. It is a stock (a total at one date), whereas the current account is a flow (over a year).

The Big Picture: The US as a Net Buyer of the World's Goods and Seller of Its Assets

The BEA estimates that the US current account deficit in 2025 was about $1.12 trillion, or 3.6% of GDP. Most of it comes from trade: the US bought about $1.24 trillion more goods than it sold, partly offset by a surplus of about $340 billion in services (travel, finance, software, education and so on). The mirror image is the financial account: foreigners kept buying US assets, so that by the end of 2025 the US owed the rest of the world about $22 trillion more than it owned abroad. These figures are large and often revised, but the direction is stable: a persistent deficit financed by foreign investment, which leaves the US a net debtor.

Sources: BEA, US International Transactions, 4th quarter and year 2025, BEA, 1st quarter 2026 and annual update, BEA, US International Trade in Goods and Services, December and annual 2025.

Think About It

1. You buy a $100 imported jacket. Where does that $100 end up?

Short answer

It goes to a foreign seller, who then has to do something with it. They can use it to buy US goods (an export), or buy US assets such as Treasury bonds, shares or property. Either way the dollar returns, which is why a trade deficit is always matched by foreign money flowing into US assets.

2. A friend says: "A trade deficit means a country is losing to foreigners." What is wrong, and what is right, about this?

Short answer

It is not a scoreboard. Households that buy more than they earn are borrowing or selling assets, and so is a country: imports can be paying for investment that makes the economy larger, or they can be paying for consumption funded by debt. What matters is what the borrowing is used for and whether it is sustainable, not the deficit itself.

3. China and Germany sell far more than they buy. Where does their extra income go?

Short answer

It is invested or lent abroad: buying foreign bonds, shares, property and companies. A surplus in the current account is always matched by an outflow of savings through the financial account, which is part of why countries like the US can run deficits and still find buyers for their debt.

Case Study: What the US Deficit Does and Does Not Mean

The Facts: In 2025 the US current account deficit was about $1.12 trillion.

The Worried View: Net debtor status means a growing share of US income and assets belongs to foreigners, and if foreign investors lost appetite for US assets the adjustment could be abrupt, with a weaker dollar, higher interest rates or both. Many economists attribute the deficit mainly to low saving, both public (large government budget deficits) and private, and recommend raising national saving.

The Relaxed View: Others argue that a deficit mainly shows the US is an attractive place to invest, with deep financial markets and a dollar the whole world uses. On this view the deficit reflects a high-investment economy rather than weakness.

The Trade-Policy View: The US administration has treated trade deficits as evidence of unfair practices and used them to justify tariffs (see chapter 6.4). Many economists reply that an overall deficit reflects the gap between what a country saves and what it invests, and that a deficit with one country says little, since the US can shift purchases elsewhere. In 2025 the deficit with China fell by about $93 billion while deficits with Vietnam and Taiwan rose by about $55 billion and $73 billion, and the overall deficit hardly moved.

Why It Matters Outside the US: Whenever you hear that a country "has a deficit" or "is running a surplus", ask three questions. Is it the trade balance or the broader current account? What is financing it, long-term investment or short-term borrowing? And is the cause a gap between saving and investment at home, which only domestic changes (more saving, less borrowing, or more investment) can close?

Sources: BEA 2025 international transactions, BEA trade 2025, Global Trade and Sanctions Law on the IEEPA ruling.

Case Study: The Other Side of the Ledger, Germany and China

Germany: In 2025 Germany's current account surplus was about €197 billion, down from about €251 billion in 2024, as exports fell for the third year in a row. The IMF still rates the surplus as larger than fundamentals justify, pointing to rules that restrain domestic investment and to a population that saves heavily for retirement.

China: China's goods trade surplus hit a record of about $1.19 trillion in 2025. Exports to the US fell, but shipments to other regions (Southeast Asia, Africa, Latin America and Europe) more than offset the drop. The IMF says China's surplus is now the largest in the world, and blames structurally high household saving linked to a thin social safety net, together with a fall in investment as the property boom ended. It recommends policies that boost Chinese consumption.

Why It Matters Outside Germany and China: Surpluses are the mirror of deficits, so they matter to everyone. A big surplus country sends its savings abroad (which supplies funds to the countries that borrow), but also sells so much that producers elsewhere feel competition, which is the source of many trade disputes. Economists usually recommend that surplus countries spend and invest more at home, and that deficit countries save more.

Sources: Bundesbank, German balance of payments December 2025, Al Jazeera on China's 2025 trade, CFR, "China's massive surplus".

The Takeaway

The balance of payments is double-entry bookkeeping for a country: what comes in through trade must go out as investment, and vice versa. A deficit is neither a defeat nor a free lunch. It is a statement that a country is spending more than it earns and borrowing the difference from the world, which can be perfectly sensible if the money builds productive assets and the lenders stay willing.
