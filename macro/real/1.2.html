Comparative Advantage & Trade

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

People, firms and countries gain by specializing in what they do at the lowest opportunity cost and trading for the rest. This works even when one side is better at everything, and it is why the things you buy are made by many hands in many countries.

Key Terms

  • Absolute Advantage: Being able to make something with fewer resources (or more output from the same resources) than someone else. A top lawyer who is also the fastest typist in the office has an absolute advantage in both jobs.
  • Comparative Advantage: Being able to make something at a lower opportunity cost than someone else, meaning you give up less of other things to produce it. This, not absolute advantage, decides who should do what.
  • Specialization: Each person or country concentrates on the activity where its comparative advantage is greatest.
  • Gains from Trade: After specializing and swapping, everyone can end up with more than they could produce alone. It happens whenever opportunity costs differ. Trade can still create winners and losers within a country, which is why trade policy is politically contested.
  • Global Supply Chain: A product designed in one country, with parts made in several others and assembled in yet another. Each step goes to wherever it is cheapest or best done.

Think About It

1. A top lawyer types faster than her assistant. Why does it still make sense for her to hire an assistant for typing?

Short answer

Because her hour is worth far more in legal work, so every hour she types costs her a lot of lost legal income. The assistant gives up very little by typing, so he has the comparative advantage in typing even though she has the absolute advantage. Both end up better off.

2. Country A can make both cars and wheat more cheaply than Country B. Can trade still help A?

Short answer

Yes. If A is much better at cars than at wheat, A gives up less by making cars and buying wheat from B, even though B is worse at both. Opportunity costs differ, so both countries can end up with more of both goods.

3. If trade makes the country as a whole better off, why do some workers still oppose it?

Short answer

The gains are spread thinly across many consumers as lower prices and more choice, while the losses fall heavily on particular workers and towns whose industries shrink. The country can gain overall while some people lose, and how to help those people is a main point of dispute in trade policy.

Case Study: Who Really Makes an iPhone?

In 2010, researchers (Kraemer, Linden and Dedrick) took apart an Apple iPhone 4 to see where its value went. Its estimated retail price was $549. The final product is stamped "Assembled in China", so one might guess China captured most of the value. It did not.

Apple captured about 58% of the price, for design, software and marketing, while Chinese labor and assembly accounted for about $10, or under 2%. This is comparative advantage at work: China's large pool of factory workers made assembly its cheapest job, while Apple's designers and engineers made design and branding the high-value job. A "made in" label says little about who earns the money.

Why It Matters Outside Apple: Nearly every product you own, from cars to clothes, is a team effort across borders. That lowers prices and gives you access to things no single country could make cheaply, but it also means disruptions, from a pandemic to a port closure, can spread quickly through the chain.

Sources: Kraemer, Linden and Dedrick, "Capturing Value in Global Networks" (2011), figures for the iPhone 4 in 2010, . The study's figures are estimates, from teardown cost data; actual shares differ by model and year.

Case Study: The "China Shock" and Who Pays for Trade

Trade makes a country richer on average, but not everyone shares evenly. China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001 and its exports to the US surged. Economists David Autor, David Dorn, Gordon Hanson and Daron Acemoglu estimated that rising Chinese import competition cut US employment by about 2.0 to 2.4 million jobs between 1999 and 2011, concentrated in manufacturing towns. Many of those workers never moved smoothly into new jobs; they ended up unemployed or dropped out of the labor force.

Other economists dispute the size, and consumers gained from lower prices. The honest summary: trade creates large overall gains, those gains are spread out, and the costs are concentrated and can last for years.

Why It Matters Outside the US: When you hear a debate about tariffs or trade deals, it is usually about who gains and who loses, not whether the total gain exists. Judge proposals by whether they address the people harmed, and not only the headline numbers.

Sources: Acemoglu, Autor, Dorn, Hanson and Price, "Import Competition and the Great US Employment Sag of the 2000s" (MIT Blueprint Labs), Cato Institute summary of the China shock debate, including critics and consumer-price research (a free-trade viewpoint).

The Takeaway

Trade is not a contest in which one side's gain is the other's loss. By specializing where their opportunity costs are lowest, people and countries produce more in total, and everyone can share in that. But the gains and losses fall on different people, so a good trade policy pays attention to both.
