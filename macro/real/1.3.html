Supply & Demand Refresher

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Prices are set by the tug of war between what buyers want and what sellers can supply. When something changes on either side, the price moves, as does the total quantity produced and consumed.

Key Terms

  • Demand: How much of a good buyers want at each possible price. A higher price means less quantity demanded. A change in demand (a shift) comes from something other than the price: income, tastes, the number of buyers, or the prices of related goods.
  • Supply: How much sellers will offer at each price. A higher price means more quantity supplied. A change in supply comes from something other than price, such as production costs, technology, the number of sellers or supply disruptions.
  • Equilibrium Price: The price at which the amount buyers want equals the amount sellers offer. The market tends to move there on its own.
  • Shortage vs. Surplus: A shortage means buyers want more than sellers offer at the current price, which pushes the price up. A surplus means sellers have more than buyers want, which pushes the price down.
  • Price Ceiling (e.g., Rent Control): A legal maximum price. If it is set below the equilibrium price, it creates a shortage, because the price cannot rise to clear the market.
  • Price Floor (e.g., Minimum Wage): A legal minimum price. If it is set above the equilibrium price, it creates a surplus, because the price cannot fall to clear the market. For a minimum wage, the surplus is workers who want jobs at that wage but cannot find them, also known as unemployment.

Think About It

1. Heavy rain destroys part of the coffee crop. What happens to the price of coffee and why?

Short answer

The price rises. The harvest shrinks, so supply falls (shifts left) while demand is unchanged. At the old price buyers want more than is available, which bids the price up until the amount wanted matches the smaller supply.

2. A law caps rent in a city below the market level. Who benefits and who loses?

Short answer

Tenants who already have a controlled apartment usually benefit, since they pay less. But at the capped price more people want apartments than landlords offer, creating a shortage, and the long-run supply of rental housing may fall, which can hurt newcomers. The details depend on how the law is designed.

3. A concert sells out in minutes and tickets resell for several times the face value. What does that tell you about the original price?

Short answer

It was set below the equilibrium price, creating a shortage. More people wanted tickets than there were seats at that price, so the resale market (or, at some venues, dynamic pricing) is what moves the price toward the level that matches demand to supply.

Case Study: Why Used Cars Got so Expensive in 2021

Used car prices usually move slowly. Over the 20 years before 2021, used cars added roughly nothing on average to overall inflation. Then in the spring of 2021 they spiked.

In the US Consumer Price Index, the used cars and trucks category rose 10.5% in June 2021 alone and 45.2% over the 12 months to June, in a month when overall consumer prices were up 5.4% over the year. Used cars were a large share of that headline jump.

A supply and demand story explains why. On the supply side, a global shortage of computer chips repeatedly halted new-car production, leaving dealers with record-low inventories. Fewer new cars meant fewer trade-ins and fewer vehicles feeding the used market. On the demand side, buyers who could not find a new car shifted to used ones, and demand was strong after the pandemic disruption. With supply shifted left and demand shifted right, the price had only one way to go. Wholesale prices, tracked by Cox Automotive's Manheim index, hit a record in January 2022, then fell about 6.4% by April 2022 as demand eased, though they were still up about 14% on the year.

Why It Matters Outside Cars: Prices are signals. A high price encourages buyers to wait or look for substitutes and encourages sellers to produce more, and as those responses build, the price comes down. It also shows why one market's supply problem can turn into a national inflation headline.

Sources: CBS News on the June 2021 CPI (used cars +10.5% on the month, +45.2% on the year; overall CPI +5.4%), CNBC via NBC New York, May 6, 2022 (Manheim index, chip shortage, 20-year average contribution). The demand-side explanation (pandemic income support) is a commonly cited factor; sources differ on how much each factor mattered.

Case Study: San Francisco's Rent Control Expansion

Rent control is a price ceiling. In 1994 San Francisco expanded rent control to additional rental housing, which gave economists Rebecca Diamond, Tim McQuade and Franklin Qian a natural experiment.

Tenants covered by the rules were about 20% more likely to stay at their address, saving roughly $2.9 billion in total through 2010. But landlords responded by selling to owner-occupiers, converting units to condominiums or redeveloping buildings, cutting the supply of rental housing by about 15%. With less rental housing, rents for everyone else rose: the authors estimate a citywide increase of about 5.1%, costing later renters roughly another $2.9 billion. Supporters point to the stability rent control gives long-time residents; critics point to the lost supply. Both are true: current tenants gain, and future ones pay more.

Why It Matters Outside San Francisco: A price cap lowers the price for those who get the product, but it does not make more of it. When the price cannot rise, supply stops growing or shrinks, and the shortfall lands on those still looking.

Sources: Diamond, McQuade and Qian, American Economic Review (2019), NBER Working Paper 24181 (abstract), Stanford GSB, "Rent control's winners and losers". The 1994 date and figures on controlled-unit counts and dollar savings are from the Stanford GSB summary of the study.

The Takeaway

Supply and demand explain more everyday prices than anything else in economics. When a price jumps, ask which side moved: did buyers want more, or did sellers produce less? And when the government sets a price, expect that it changes the quantity available as well. Well-intentioned policies can sometimes hurt the people they intend to protect, so think critically about who wins and who loses from any individual policy.
