6.3

Net Exports & Capital Flows

Real-World Relevance: Medium
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

When a country's currency gets weaker, its exports get cheaper abroad and imports get more expensive at home, so the same exchange-rate move that makes foreign tourists flock in also raises the price of imported food and fuel. Interest rates matter too: money flows toward the country that pays more, which pushes its currency up.

Key Terms

  • Net Exports (NX): What a country sells abroad (exports) minus what it buys from abroad (imports). Positive means a trade surplus, negative a deficit.
  • Weaker Currency and Net Exports: A weaker currency makes your exports cheaper for foreigners and imports more expensive for you, so it tends to push net exports up. A stronger currency does the reverse and tends to push net exports down. Effects are usually slow, because contracts, supply chains and habits take time to change.
  • Capital Flows: Money moving across borders to buy stocks, bonds, property or businesses. Investors prefer higher returns, so when a country's interest rates rise relative to others, funds flow in, demand for its currency rises and the currency tends to strengthen.
  • Interest-Rate Gap: The difference between interest rates in two countries. A wide gap is one of the biggest drivers of currency moves: high-rate countries tend to see stronger currencies, low-rate countries weaker ones.
  • Pass-Through: How much of an exchange-rate change shows up in the prices you pay. A weaker currency feeds into import prices and, from there, into overall inflation, but not all at once and not one-for-one.

Think About It

1. The yen falls from 110 to 150 per dollar. What happens to the price of a Japanese car in the US, and to the price of an American car in Japan?

Short answer

The Japanese car gets cheaper in dollars (a car priced at ¥3,000,000 costs about $27,300 at 110 yen per dollar but only $20,000 at 150, about 27% less), which helps Japanese exporters, while the American car gets more expensive in yen. A weaker currency makes your exports more competitive and imports more expensive.

2. The US central bank raises interest rates while Japan keeps its rates near zero. What happens to the dollar and the yen, and why?

Short answer

The dollar tends to strengthen and the yen to weaken. Investors can earn more in US assets, so they sell yen to buy dollars, which pushes up the dollar's price and lowers the yen's. That is the mechanism analysts cited for the yen's slide to its 2024 lows.

3. A country's currency drops 10%. Why does inflation not rise by 10% immediately?

Short answer

Only part of what people buy is imported, many importers absorb part of the rise in margins, contracts are fixed for a time, and goods made locally do not change price. The effect spreads gradually through the economy, which is why studies find smaller (but real) increases in overall prices.

Case Study: The Weak Yen and Japan's Tourist Boom

In 2019 one US dollar bought about ¥110. By 2025 it bought ¥150, and in June 2024 it reached ¥161, the yen's weakest in decades. The main driver was an interest-rate gap: the US federal funds rate was 5.25% to 5.5%, against 0% to 0.1% for Japan's. Investors earned much more on dollars, so they sold yen.

Visitors to Japan rose from 31.9 million in 2019 to a record 42.7 million in 2025. A weak yen is one reason: the same dollars or euros buy about a third more yen than in 2019, so hotels, meals and shopping seem cheap. (Other things helped too, such as the end of pandemic restrictions.) Visitor spending counts as an export, so it adds to net exports.

The other side is paid by Japanese households and importers. Japan buys much of its energy and food from abroad, and a weaker yen raises the yen price of imports like these.

Why It Matters Outside Japan: For any country, a falling currency is a transfer: exporters, tourist businesses and visiting foreigners gain; importers and residents who buy foreign goods or travel abroad lose. If you are the traveller, a weak foreign currency is a discount; if you live there, it is a price rise.

Sources: Nippon.com on 2025 visitor numbers and the yen, CNBC/NBC on the yen at 161.

Case Study: The Brexit Pound (2016)

On June 23, 2016 the UK voted to leave the European Union. In the first week, sterling lost about 11% of its value against the dollar and 8% against the euro, and stayed roughly 10% weaker for a long time afterwards. LSE economists estimated that the fall pushed up prices, adding about 1.7 percentage points to inflation over the next year and costing the average household about £404 a year because wages did not rise to match.

A weaker pound should in principle help exporters, because British goods become cheaper abroad. But an LSE analysis in 2018 found no evidence yet that export volumes had increased: trade flows adjust slowly, and the risk of future trade barriers discouraged firms from expanding.

Why It Matters Outside the UK: When a currency falls, the price rise reaches shoppers faster than the export gains reach workers. A weaker currency is a cost to consumers first, and any help to exporters comes later and more slowly.

Sources: LSE research (Sampson), "Britain is already paying a price for voting to leave".

The Takeaway

Currency moves redistribute: a weaker currency lifts exporters and tourism and raises import prices, a stronger one does the opposite. Interest-rate gaps are one of the biggest causes of these moves. If you are shopping, travelling, or running a business that buys from abroad, a currency swing can matter as much as a price change at the shop.
