Aggregate Supply

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Aggregate supply is how much the whole economy can produce at different price levels. In the short run, a sudden jump in the cost of something every business needs (oil, shipping, chips) can raise prices and cut output at the same time; in the long run, what the economy can produce depends on its workers, equipment and technology, not on the price level.

Key Terms

  • Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS): The amount businesses will produce at each overall price level while some of their costs (especially wages and contracts) are still fixed. Its key lesson: if the cost of their inputs changes, firms change how much they produce at any given price.
  • Long-Run Aggregate Supply (LRAS): The economy's full-capacity output, which is what it can sustain once wages and prices have fully adjusted. It is set by the number of workers, their skills, the machines and buildings they use, and technology. It does not depend on the price level, so doubling every price and every wage would not make the country able to produce more.
  • Supply Shock: A sudden change in production costs that hits many businesses at once, such as an oil price spike, a pandemic closing factories, or a shortage of a key part. A negative supply shock means higher costs and lower output; a positive one (for example a drop in oil prices) means the reverse.
  • Stagflation: A combination of stagnation (weak output and rising unemployment) and inflation (rising prices) at the same time. It is the signature of a negative supply shock, and it is painful because the two problems are usually treated with opposite medicine.
  • Demand-Pull vs. Cost-Push Inflation: Demand-pull inflation comes from people wanting to buy more than the economy can supply (aggregate demand rising faster than capacity). Cost-push inflation comes from it costing more to make things (a negative supply shock). In real life the two are often mixed, and economists argue about the proportions.

Think About It

1. A storm shuts down a major oil refinery and gasoline prices jump. Besides drivers, who else is affected, and why might output fall?

Short answer

Almost every business that moves goods or people uses fuel, so their costs rise at any given selling price. Some will charge more, and some will produce or deliver less. That is a leftward shift of short-run aggregate supply: higher prices and lower output together.

2. If a country's government printed twice as much money and every wage and price in the economy doubled overnight, could the country now produce twice as many goods?

Short answer

No. The workers, factories and technology would be the same, so real output would be the same. This is why the long-run supply curve is vertical: the economy's capacity depends on real resources, not on the price level.

3. Why would a government and a central bank find it hard to fight both the high prices and the lost jobs after a big oil price spike?

Short answer

Because the tools that cool prices (higher interest rates, less government spending) work by reducing spending, which deepens the loss of output and jobs, and the tools that support jobs (lower rates, more spending) push prices higher. There is no single move that fixes both.

Case Study: The 1973 Oil Embargo and Stagflation

In October 1973, during the Yom Kippur War, the Arab members of OPEC (the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries) began an oil embargo on the United States and raised prices. The embargo ended in March 1974, but the price increase stayed: crude oil that had cost about $2.90 a barrel cost $11.65 by January 1974, roughly four times as much.

The US economy was hit in the way a leftward shift in short-run aggregate supply predicts. A recession ran from November 1973 to March 1975, and unemployment reached 9% in May 1975. Yet consumer prices rose about 11% in 1974. Rising prices and rising unemployment at once became known as stagflation.

Economists debate how much of the 1970s inflation the oil shocks explain; the Fed's own review argues that inflation was already building before the embargo. What is widely accepted is that an input shock like this creates a hard trade-off: fighting the price rise tends to deepen the loss of output, and vice versa.

Why It Matters Outside the 1970s: A spike in the cost of fuel or any key input hits households twice, through higher prices and through weaker job markets. The 1970s remain the benchmark for what a supply shock can do.

Case Study: The Post-Pandemic Supply Chain Crunch, 2021-22

When economies reopened in 2021, spending rebounded faster than factories, ports and trucking could handle. Shipping costs soared, and a shortage of computer chips meant automakers could not finish vehicles: AlixPartners forecast that the shortage would cost the global auto industry about $210 billion in revenue in 2021. With few new cars available, used-car prices rose almost 30% in the twelve months to May 2021.

Economists differ on what drove the broader inflation of that period. Bernanke and Blanchard found that the early surge came mostly from shocks to particular markets, such as energy, food and goods shortages. Other research puts more weight on demand. Both views agree that a mismatch between what people wanted to buy and what could be produced and delivered mattered.

Why It Matters Outside the Pandemic: The products you buy depend on long chains of suppliers across the world, and a break in one link (a closed port, a missing chip) can raise prices and empty shelves even if nothing is wrong with your own economy. When prices rise, it is worth asking whether it is because people are spending more (demand-pull), because it is costing more to make things (cost-push), or both, since the policy responses differ.

The Takeaway

When prices rise, ask whether the economy is trying to buy more than it can produce (demand-pull) or whether it has become more costly to produce (cost-push). A supply shock raises prices and lowers output at the same time, and leaves policymakers with a painful choice. Over the long run, living standards depend on the economy's real capacity to produce: its people, equipment and ideas.
