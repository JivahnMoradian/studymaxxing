Inflation, Price Indices & the Business Cycle

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Inflation is a general rise in prices, so each dollar buys less. It matters because it quietly shifts wealth between savers and borrowers, and because the economy's ups and downs (the business cycle) decide whether jobs and incomes are growing.

Key Terms

  • Inflation Rate: The yearly percentage rise in the general price level. If it is 4%, a basket of goods that cost $100 last year costs $104 now.
  • CPI (Consumer Price Index): The standard US inflation gauge. Statisticians price a fixed "basket" of what typical households buy (rent, food, gasoline, medical care...) and track its cost over time. Core Inflation leaves out food and energy because their prices swing a lot.
  • Real vs. Nominal Interest Rate: The nominal rate is the rate on the loan or savings account. The Real rate is the nominal rate minus inflation, which is what you actually earn or pay in buying power.
  • Expected vs. Unexpected Inflation: If everyone expects 3% inflation, interest rates and wages adjust for it. Unexpected inflation helps borrowers (they repay with cheaper dollars) and hurts lenders and people on fixed incomes.
  • Recession and Expansion: A recession is a broad, significant decline in economic activity (jobs, income, spending, production). An expansion is the growth period between recessions. Together they form the business cycle.

Where the US Stands (October 2026)

  • The Fed's goal is 2% inflation a year, measured by a related index called the PCE price index (the Fed's own preferred gauge, not the CPI). That goal has been official since January 2012.
  • The latest CPI report (September 2026) showed prices 3.7% higher than a year earlier. Excluding food and energy, the "core" rate was 2.6%, and energy prices were up about 20%, which likely explains much of the gap between the two.
  • For comparison, the peak of the last big inflation episode was 9.1% in June 2022.

So by this yardstick, inflation is above the Fed's 2% goal but far below the 2022 peak. As a rough guide (not an official scale): around 2-3% is usually seen as normal, 4-5% starts to be noticed in household budgets, and 8-10% is widely treated as a crisis.

Sources: Fed Statement on Longer-Run Goals, BLS CPI, September 2026, June 2022 CPI.

Think About It

1. You have a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage, and prices and wages unexpectedly rise faster than anyone predicted. Who gains?

Short answer

You, the borrower. Your payment stays the same in dollars while your wages rise, so you repay the loan with dollars that are worth less. The lender loses in real terms. (Lenders protect themselves by charging higher rates when they expect inflation.)

2. Your savings account pays 1% and inflation is 3.7%. What is happening to your savings?

Short answer

Your real return is negative, about -2.7% a year. The balance grows in dollars but buys less each year. This is why it matters whether your interest rate is above or below inflation.

3. The official inflation rate is 3.7%, but you drive a lot and rent. Is your personal inflation the same?

Short answer

Probably not. The CPI averages a typical basket. If energy prices are rising fast, someone who spends more than average on gasoline or heating feels more inflation than the headline number. Your own inflation depends on what you actually buy.

4. The economy shrinks for two quarters in a row. Is it officially a recession?

Short answer

Not automatically. "Two quarters of falling GDP" is a popular rule of thumb, not the official test. In the US, a committee of economists at the National Bureau of Economic Research looks at jobs, income, spending and production, and announces the dates well after the fact.

Case Study: The 2022 Inflation Spike

In June 2022, US consumer prices were 9.1% higher than a year earlier, the biggest annual jump since November 1981. Gasoline, housing and food were the largest contributors, and food prices alone were rising at about 10% a year.

Because inflation hit the things people buy most often, it was felt far more sharply than a single statistic suggests. A household that drove a lot, rented, and spent heavily on groceries lived with higher personal inflation than the average. Savers with money in accounts paying near zero lost buying power quickly. Borrowers with fixed-rate loans benefited in real terms, while the Federal Reserve responded by raising interest rates, which made new borrowing more expensive. (How and why the Fed does that is covered in 4.4.)

Why It Matters Outside 2022: Inflation is not just a number in the news. It changes what your paycheck buys, what a fixed-rate loan really costs, and whether your savings are growing or shrinking in real terms. It is also why interest rates, not only prices, appear in headlines whenever inflation rises.

Sources: RealPage on June 2022 CPI, Aviation Pros summary of the BLS release, Euronews/Reuters on the June 2022 CPI.

Case Study: The Shortest Recession on Record

The US recession of 2020 lasted two months: the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) dated the peak in February 2020 and the trough in April 2020. That makes it the shortest on record; the previous shortest, in 1980, lasted six months. In April alone 20.5 million jobs were lost, so it was deep, but brief.

It also shows that "recession" is not defined by two negative quarters. The NBER looks at employment, income, spending and production across the whole economy, and announces turning points well after the fact.

Why It Matters Outside the Textbook: When a headline says "recession", ask who is calling it and by what test. Recessions are not just a pause in growth: they are periods in which jobs and incomes fall across the economy, and their depth and length vary enormously.

The Takeaway

Two numbers to keep in mind: about 2% is the Fed's inflation goal, and as of September 2026, inflation in the US is around 3.7%. Inflation affects people differently because it is an economy-wide average and people's consumption patterns differ significantly. Whether your income and your savings rate keep pace with inflation, and whether you are a borrower or a lender, matter when determining how you will be affected. Treat "recession" as an official judgment about broad economic activity, not a headline slogan.
