Economic Growth

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Over decades, the growth rate of output per person matters more than almost any other number in economics: a difference of a couple of percentage points a year, compounded, separates rich countries from poor ones and decides whether your children are much better off than you.

Key Terms

  • Economic Growth: A rise in an economy's output over time. For living standards what matters is growth of real (inflation-adjusted) GDP per person, not total GDP, since a country can grow in total simply by having more people.
  • Rule of 70: A shortcut for compounding. Divide 70 by a growth rate (in percent) to get the approximate number of years needed to double. At 2% a year, income doubles in about 35 years; at 7%, about 10 years.
  • Productivity: Output per hour of work. It is the main source of lasting growth in living standards, because more output per hour, rather than simply more hours, is what lets average incomes rise.
  • Sources of Growth: (1) more or better physical capital (machines, buildings, roads), (2) human capital (skills, education, health), and (3) technology and better ways of organizing production, together with institutions such as property rights and the rule of law that make investment worthwhile. Economists call the part of growth not explained by more capital and labor total factor productivity.
  • Catch-Up Growth (Convergence): The tendency of poorer countries that invest and adopt existing technologies to grow faster than richer ones and narrow the gap. It is a tendency, not a guarantee: many poor countries have not caught up.

The Big Picture: Compounding in Action

Growth rates look small and the results are large. At 1% growth per year, income per person doubles roughly every 70 years, about once in a lifetime. At 7%, it doubles every 10 years. Over 60 years the first path leaves a country about 1.8 times as rich; the second about 58 times.

Think About It

1. Country A grows 2% a year and Country B 4% a year, both starting at the same income. Roughly how long until each doubles, and what is the gap after 35 years?

Short answer

By the rule of 70, A doubles in about 35 years and B in about 17.5 years. After 35 years A has doubled its income while B has quadrupled it, so B is twice as rich. Small differences in growth rate matter a lot because they compound.

2. A country's total GDP grows 3% a year, but its population also grows 3% a year. Are people better off?

Short answer

No. Output per person is flat. Living standards rise only if output grows faster than the population, so economists focus on GDP per person.

3. Why does a worker with a better machine, or more training, produce more in an hour?

Short answer

Because productivity depends on how much capital and skill each worker has to work with, and on the technology available. A farmer with a tractor harvests far more than one with a hand tool. This is why investment in capital, education and new technology drives long-run growth.

Case Study: South Korea vs. Ghana, Same Start, Different Path

In 1960, South Korea and Ghana were about equally poor. According to World Bank data in constant 2015 US dollars, GDP per person was about $1,040 in South Korea and $1,100 in Ghana. By 2024 it was about $37,000 in South Korea and about $2,200 in Ghana. Korea's average growth rate of GDP per person was about 5.7% a year (a doubling time of roughly 12 years by the rule of 70); Ghana's was about 1.1% a year (a doubling time of roughly 65 years).

Korea's growth is usually explained by high investment (30 to 40% of GDP from the mid-1970s), a labor force that became more skilled and moved from farms to factories, an economy that pushed manufactured exports, and government policy that supported exports and built power, roads and ports. Economists still debate how much came from piling up capital and labor versus productivity gains.

Why It Matters Outside Korea: A country's long-run fortunes are decided by the compounding of its growth rate, not by its starting point. When you see that a country "grew 1% a year for 60 years", it did not simply stand still; it fell steadily behind. For your own life, the same idea applies to your savings: small differences in the annual return compound enormously.

Case Study: China and Argentina, Growth Fast and Slow

The same World Bank series shows China's GDP per person growing from about $917 in 1990 to about $13,100 in 2024, roughly 8% a year, which by the rule of 70 means a doubling about every nine years. Argentina's rose from about $7,400 in 1960 to about $12,800 in 2024, around 0.9% a year, a doubling time of around 80 years. Argentina was far richer than both Korea and China in 1960, and is now far behind. The reasons are debated, but include repeated inflation and debt crises.

Why It Matters Outside China and Argentina: Growth is not automatic, and it is not permanent. Fast catch-up growth is possible, but a lasting rich-country level of income requires sustained investment, stable policies and institutions. A country that falls into repeated crises can lose decades of progress.

The Takeaway

Growth in output per person, compounded over decades, determines living standards. Capital, skills, technology and good institutions are its sources, and the rule of 70 shows how much small differences in the growth rate matter. The gap between Korea and Ghana, which started roughly level in 1960, is an example. The causes are discussed and debated, but the size of the gap is not.
