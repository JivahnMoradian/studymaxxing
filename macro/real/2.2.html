Real vs. Nominal GDP & the Deflator

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

A price tag or a paycheck tells you how many dollars something costs, not how much you can buy. Real numbers strip out rising prices so you can see whether things have actually improved.

Key Terms

  • Nominal: Measured in the prices of the day. Your salary, a company's revenue, and headline GDP are all nominal figures.
  • Real: Adjusted for changes in prices, so that a "dollar" means the same purchasing power in every year being compared. Real GDP, real wages and real returns are what tell you if people are better off.
  • Base Year: The year whose prices are used as the yardstick. Pick a different base year and the real numbers change, but the growth rates come out nearly the same.
  • GDP Deflator: A price index covering everything produced in a country (consumer goods, business equipment, government services). Nominal GDP divided by real GDP, times 100, gives the deflator. Compare it with the CPI, which tracks what typical households buy, including imports.
  • Real Wage: Your pay divided by a price index. If prices rise faster than your pay, your real wage falls even though your paycheck went up.
  • Money Illusion: Judging by the number on the paycheck or the chart instead of by what it will buy.

Think About It

1. Your employer gives you a 3% raise in a year when prices rise 5%. Did you get a raise?

Short answer

In nominal terms yes, in real terms no. Your buying power fell by roughly 2% (3% minus 5%). Always compare a raise to inflation.

2. A company announces that its revenue grew 8% while prices in its industry rose 6%. How much real growth is that?

Short answer

Roughly 2%. Most of the increase is just higher prices. To judge a company's growth, ask how many units it sold, not only how many dollars it took in.

3. Headline nominal GDP rises 6% in a year. Did the economy produce 6% more?

Short answer

Not necessarily. Nominal GDP rises when prices rise, even if output stays flat. Only real GDP growth tells you whether more goods and services were produced.

Case Study: The Stock Market That Went Nowhere, 1966-1982

In January 1966 the Dow Jones Industrial Average, the best-known US stock index, stood near 990. In December 1982 it was around 990 again. Sixteen years later, an investor who looked only at the number saw no change.

Prices were not flat, though. Inflation averaged roughly 7% a year over that period. Dollars in 1982 bought a fraction of what they bought in 1966, and one analysis estimates an investor in the index lost about two-thirds of their purchasing power in real terms. The picture is softer once dividends are included and the broader S&P 500 is used, which earned about 6.8% a year in nominal terms, but even that was modest in real terms.

Why It Matters Outside the Stock Market: The same trap applies to a house you "sold for more than you paid", a fixed pension, or a savings balance that grows 1% a year. A number that looks flat or rising can hide a real loss. When you compare any two dollar amounts from different years, adjust for inflation first.

Sources: A Wealth of Common Sense on 1966-1982, Jason Zweig, "The 11-Year Itch".

Case Study: The $7.25 Minimum Wage

The US federal minimum wage has been $7.25 an hour since July 24, 2009, which is the longest period the wage has gone unchanged since the law took effect in 1938. The nominal wage has not moved. The real wage has.

Since July 2009 the CPI has risen about 56%, so $7.25 in 2009 is about $11.34 in September 2026 dollars: a worker at the federal minimum has about 36% less purchasing power per hour than in 2009. Supporters of a higher minimum point to this lost purchasing power. Critics focus on possible effects on jobs and small businesses, and economists debate how large those effects are. Many states and cities set higher minimums of their own.

Why It Matters Outside the Debate: Any amount fixed in dollars (a salary, a pension, a fine, a tax threshold, a loan limit) loses value silently as prices rise unless it is "indexed" to inflation. When you see a figure that has not changed for years, ask what it would be worth today.

Sources: EPI on the lost value, BLS CPI-U July 2009 index, BLS CPI release for September 2026. The 36% figure is our own calculation from the two index values.

The Takeaway

Nominal numbers tell you how big something is in today's money. Real numbers tell you what it is worth. Whenever someone quotes a growth rate, a raise, a return or a price from different years, ask: "Is that adjusted for inflation?"
