5.2

Money Neutrality, Quantity Theory & Expectations

Read the theory → Drill 5.2 →

A. The Equation of Exchange (MV = PY)

Problem 1. The money supply is 800, velocity is 4 and real GDP is 1,600. Find the price level.

Show solution
P = M × VY = 800 × 41,600 = 2

Problem 2. Nominal GDP is $15 trillion and the money supply is $3 trillion. Find velocity.

Show solution
V = P × YM = 153 = 5

Each dollar is spent 5 times per year on final goods and services.

Problem 3. The money supply is $2.4 trillion, velocity is 6 and the price level index is 1.2. Find real GDP.

Show solution
Y = M × VP = 2.4 × 61.2 = 12 trillion

Problem 4. The price level is 1.5, real GDP is 4,000 and velocity is 2.5. How large is the money supply?

Show solution
M = P × YV = 1.5 × 4,0002.5 = 2,400

Problem 5. With V = 4 and Y = 1,000 both fixed, the central bank raises M from 600 to 750. Find the price level before and after, and the percent change.

Show solution

Before: P = (600 × 4) ÷ 1,000 = 2.4.
After: P = (750 × 4) ÷ 1,000 = 3.0.
The change is 3.0 ÷ 2.4 − 1 = 25%, exactly the percent increase in M (750 ÷ 600 − 1 = 25%).
With V and Y fixed, prices move in proportion to money.

Problem 6. An economy has M = 900, V = 5 and Y = 1,500. (a) Find P. (b) Real GDP grows to 1,800 with M and V unchanged. Find the new P. (c) What M would keep P at its original level?

Show solution

(a) P = 900 × 5 ÷ 1,500 = 3.
(b) P = 4,500 ÷ 1,800 = 2.5 (more goods with the same spending means lower prices).
(c) M = P × Y ÷ V = 3 × 1,800 ÷ 5 = 1,080.

Problem 7. Compute velocity for each year and say whether it is constant.

Year M P Y
1 300 1.5 800
2 330 1.5 880
3 400 1.6 900
Show solution

Year 1: V = (1.5 × 800) ÷ 300 = 4.
Year 2: (1.5 × 880) ÷ 330 = 4.
Year 3: (1.6 × 900) ÷ 400 = 3.6.
Velocity was stable for two years and then fell, so the quantity theory's assumption of constant V is only an approximation.

B. Money Growth and Inflation

Problem 8. Money grows 8% per year, velocity is constant and real output grows 3%. What is the inflation rate?

Show solution

π ≈ %ΔM − %ΔY = 8 − 3 = 5%.

Problem 9. Money grows 12% per year, real output grows 2% and V is constant. Find inflation.

Show solution

π ≈ 12 − 2 = 10%.

Problem 10. A central bank targets 2% inflation. Real GDP grows 3.5% a year and V is constant. How fast should money grow?

Show solution

%ΔM ≈ π + %ΔY = 2 + 3.5 = 5.5% per year.

Problem 11. Velocity falls 2% per year, money grows 7% and real GDP grows 2.5%. Find inflation.

Show solution

%ΔM + %ΔV = %ΔP + %ΔY, so 7 − 2 = π + 2.5 and π = 2.5%.

Problem 12. Money and velocity are constant while real GDP grows 4% per year. What happens to the price level?

Show solution

With %ΔM = 0 and %ΔV = 0, the identity gives 0% = π + 4%, so π = −4%: deflation of 4% per year.
Faster output growth without more money means falling prices.

Problem 13. Money grows 20% and real GDP grows 4% with V constant. Compare the approximate and exact inflation rates.

Show solution

Approximate: 20 − 4 = 16%.
Exact: 1.20 ÷ 1.04 = 1.1538, so 15.4%.
The approximation is close, and gets worse with large growth rates.

Problem 14. A country's output is flat and V is constant, while its government prints money to grow M by 25% per month. What is the monthly inflation rate and what is the underlying cause?

Show solution

Inflation ≈ 25 − 0 = 25% per month.
The cause is excessive money growth (typically to finance a deficit): inflation is a monetary phenomenon.

C. The Fisher Equation

Problem 15. The real interest rate is 3% and expected inflation is 2%. Find the nominal rate.

Show solution
i = r + πe = 3 + 2 = 5%

Problem 16. A bank loan carries a nominal rate of 7% and expected inflation is 4.5%. What is the expected real rate?

Show solution

r = i − πe = 7 − 4.5 = 2.5%.

Problem 17. A bond pays 9% nominal and inflation turns out to be 6%. Find the ex post real return.

Show solution

rex post = 9 − 6 = 3%.

Problem 18. A lender expects 2% inflation and wants a 4% real return, so charges 6%. Inflation turns out to be 5%. Find the ex post real rate and say who gains.

Show solution

rex post = 6 − 5 = 1%, below the 4% intended.
The borrower gains and the lender loses, because inflation was higher than expected.

Problem 19. The real rate is 2.5% and stays constant. The central bank permanently raises money growth so that expected inflation rises from 3% to 6%. What happens to the nominal rate?

Show solution

Before: i = 2.5 + 3 = 5.5%.
After: i = 2.5 + 6 = 8.5%.
The nominal rate rises by 3 points, the full change in expected inflation (the Fisher effect).

Problem 20. The nominal rate is 1.5% and inflation is 4%. What is the real rate?

Show solution

r = 1.5 − 4 = −2.5%.
A negative real rate means lenders lose purchasing power and borrowers gain.

Problem 21. Country X has a nominal rate of 12% and expected inflation of 9%. Country Y has 4% and 1%. Which has the higher real rate?

Show solution

X: 12 − 9 = 3%.
Y: 4 − 1 = 3%.
The real rates are equal: the high nominal rate in X only compensates for high expected inflation.

D. Neutrality Reasoning

Problem 22. Classify each as real or nominal: (a) the dollar wage; (b) the real wage; (c) the price level; (d) real GDP; (e) the nominal interest rate; (f) the real interest rate; (g) the unemployment rate.

Show solution

Nominal: (a), (c), (e). Real: (b), (d), (f), (g). Money neutrality says that in the long run money changes only the nominal variables.

Problem 23. Overnight, every dollar bill, bank balance and wage in an economy is doubled (a one-time doubling of the money supply). What happens in the long run to the price level, nominal wages, real wages, real GDP and the real interest rate?

Show solution

The price level doubles and nominal wages double. The real wage, real GDP and the real interest rate are unchanged. This is money neutrality.

Problem 24. The Fed increases the money supply when the economy is at full employment. Describe the short-run and long-run effects with an AD-AS graph.

Show solution

Short run: AD shifts right: real GDP rises above Yf and the price level rises. Long run: nominal wages rise, SRAS shifts left, real GDP returns to Yf and the price level ends higher than before. Money is not neutral in the short run, but neutral in the long run.

Problem 25. True or false: "Printing money is a reliable way to raise real GDP permanently."

Show solution

False. Real GDP in the long run depends on labor, capital and technology (LRAS). More money raises output only temporarily, and permanently raises the price level.

Problem 26. The Fed increases the money supply. Using the money market, describe the effect on the nominal interest rate in the short run, and what happens in the long run if inflation expectations rise.

Show solution

Short run: MS shifts right, the nominal rate falls (liquidity effect). Long run: higher money growth raises expected inflation, so lenders demand compensation and the nominal rate rises through the Fisher effect: i rises by the change in πe.

Problem 27. Why is money neutral only in the long run?

Show solution

In the short run nominal wages and some prices are sticky, so a change in AD changes real output and employment. In the long run wages and prices adjust fully, restoring output to potential, so only nominal variables change.

E. Expectations and Credibility

Problem 28. Classify each as adaptive or rational expectations: (a) workers expect this year's inflation to equal last year's; (b) firms read the Fed's announced plan and revise price forecasts immediately; (c) a union bases wage demands on the average inflation of the last three years.

Show solution

(a) Adaptive. (b) Rational (uses all available information, including announcements). (c) Adaptive.

Problem 29. The Fed credibly announces it will cut money growth to lower inflation. With rational expectations, what happens to the cost of disinflation (the rise in unemployment)?

Show solution

Expectations fall immediately, so the SRPC shifts down quickly and inflation falls with little or no rise in unemployment. The more credible the Fed, the lower the cost.

Problem 30. Two central banks each want to cut inflation by 3 points. Bank A has a strong record; Bank B has repeatedly broken promises. Which faces the larger recession, and why?

Show solution

Bank B. The public does not believe it, so expected inflation stays high and wages keep rising; inflation falls only by pushing the economy along the SRPC to high unemployment. Bank A's credibility lets expectations adjust quickly.

Problem 31. Inflation is higher than anyone expected. Say who gains and who loses: (a) a homeowner with a fixed-rate mortgage; (b) a retiree whose savings are in fixed-rate bonds; (c) a worker whose wage was set in a contract last year.

Show solution

(a) Gains: repays in dollars worth less. (b) Loses: the bonds' real return falls. (c) Loses: the real wage falls because the nominal wage was fixed.

Problem 32. Explain why anticipated expansionary policy has a smaller effect on real output than unanticipated policy.

Show solution

If policy is anticipated, workers and firms raise wages and prices in advance, so SRAS shifts left at the same time AD shifts right. The price level rises with little change in real output. A surprise lets AD rise before wages and prices adjust, so real output rises in the short run.

F. The Taylor Rule (College Level)

(Problems 33–37 use i = π + r* + a(π − π*) + b(output gap), with r* = 2 and π* = 2 unless stated, and a = b = 0.5.)

Problem 33. Inflation is 3% and the output gap is +1%. Find the Taylor-rule rate.

Show solution
i = 3 + 2 + 0.5(3 - 2) + 0.5(1) = 6%

Problem 34. Inflation is 1% and the output gap is −3%. Find the rate.

Show solution
i = 1 + 2 + 0.5(1 - 2) + 0.5(-3) = 3 - 0.5 - 1.5 = 1%

Problem 35. In a severe recession inflation is 0.5% and the output gap is −6%. What does the rule suggest, and what constraint applies?

Show solution
i = 0.5 + 2 + 0.5(0.5 - 2) + 0.5(-6) = 2.5 - 0.75 - 3 = -1.25%

Rates cannot go much below zero (the zero lower bound), so the Fed sets the rate near 0% and may use other tools.

Problem 36. With a zero output gap, inflation rises from 2% to 4%. By how much does the rule raise the nominal rate, and why does that satisfy the Taylor principle?

Show solution

At π = 2: i = 2 + 2 + 0 = 4%.
At π = 4: i = 4 + 2 + 0.5(2) = 7%.
The nominal rate rises 3 points for a 2-point rise in inflation, a ratio of 1.5.
Since this is greater than 1, the real rate rises (from 2% to 3%), which cools demand.

Problem 37. A central bank uses a = 0.75 and b = 1.0 with r* = 2, π* = 2. Inflation is 4% and the output gap is −2%. Find the rate.

Show solution
i = 4 + 2 + 0.75(4 - 2) + 1.0(-2) = 6 + 1.5 - 2 = 5.5%
