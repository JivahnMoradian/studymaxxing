Economic Growth

Read the theory → Drill 5.4 →

A. Growth Rates and the Rule of 70

Problem 1. Real GDP rises from $12,500 billion to $13,000 billion. Find the growth rate.

Show solution

(13,000 − 12,500) ÷ 12,500 = 0.04, or 4%.

Problem 2. An economy's real GDP is $8,200 billion in 2022 and $8,364 billion in 2023. Find the growth rate.

Show solution

(8,364 − 8,200) ÷ 8,200 = 164 ÷ 8,200 = 2.0%.

Problem 3. Real GDP grows 5% while the population grows 1.5%. Estimate growth in real GDP per capita.

Show solution

Approximately 5 − 1.5 = 3.5% (the exact value is 1.05 ÷ 1.015 − 1 = 3.45%).

Problem 4. Real GDP is $7,500 billion with a population of 250 million. A year later real GDP is $7,800 billion and the population is 253 million. Find real GDP per capita in each year and its growth rate.

Show solution

Year 1: 7,500 billion ÷ 250 million = $30,000.
Year 2: 7,800 billion ÷ 253 million ≈ $30,830.
Growth = 30,830 ÷ 30,000 − 1 ≈ 2.8%.
(Real GDP grew 4%, but population grew 1.2%.)

Problem 5. Use the rule of 70 to find how long real GDP takes to double at (a) 2.5% growth, (b) 7%, (c) 1.4%.

Show solution

(a) 70 ÷ 2.5 = 28 years.
(b) 70 ÷ 7 = 10 years.
(c) 70 ÷ 1.4 = 50 years.

Problem 6. An economy grows 3.5% per year. By what factor does real GDP grow in 60 years?

Show solution

Doubling time = 70 ÷ 3.5 = 20 years.
In 60 years there are 3 doublings: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 times as large.

Problem 7. What growth rate would double a country's output in 14 years?

Show solution

70 ÷ 14 = 5% per year.

Problem 8. Two countries each have real GDP per capita of $10,000. Country A grows 2% per year and Country B grows 5%. Compare them after 35 years.

Show solution

A doubles every 70 ÷ 2 = 35 years: about $20,000.
B doubles every 70 ÷ 5 = 14 years, so in 35 years it doubles 2.5 times, a factor of about 5.5 (exactly 1.0535 ≈ 5.52): about $55,000.
The 3-point growth gap makes B nearly three times richer than A.

Problem 9. Real GDP is $9,000 billion and grows 3% per year. What will it be after 10 years?

Show solution

9,000 × 1.0310 ≈ 9,000 × 1.344 = $12,095 billion.
(The rule of 70 predicts doubling in 23 years, so 10 years gives a bit under 1.5 times.)

B. Productivity

Problem 10. Real GDP is $2,400 billion and employment is 40 million workers. Find labor productivity.

Show solution

2,400 billion ÷ 40 million = $60,000 per worker.

Problem 11. In Year 1 an economy produces 500 units with 20 workers. In Year 2 it produces 546 units with 21 workers. Find the growth rate of productivity.

Show solution

Year 1: 500 ÷ 20 = 25.
Year 2: 546 ÷ 21 = 26.
Growth = 1 ÷ 25 = 4%.

Problem 12. Real GDP grows 3.2% while employment grows 1.2%. Estimate productivity growth.

Show solution

%Δ(Y/L) ≈ %ΔY − %ΔL = 3.2 − 1.2 = 2.0%.

Problem 13. Productivity rises 2.5% and employment rises 0.5%. Estimate the growth in real GDP.

Show solution

%ΔY ≈ 2.5 + 0.5 = 3.0%.

Problem 14. Real GDP is $4,000 billion, population is 100 million and 45 million people are employed. Find GDP per capita and GDP per worker, and explain the difference.

Show solution

Per capita: 4,000 billion ÷ 100 million = $40,000.
Per worker: 4,000 billion ÷ 45 million ≈ $88,889.
Per worker is higher because only 45% of the population is employed.

Problem 15. An economy has 600 units of capital and 20 workers. Later it has 720 units and 22 workers. Did capital per worker rise?

Show solution

Before: 600 ÷ 20 = 30.
After: 720 ÷ 22 ≈ 32.7.
Capital per worker rose (capital deepening), so productivity should rise.

Problem 16. Output per worker for different amounts of capital per worker: 10 → 30, 20 → 50, 30 → 65, 40 → 75. Does the table show diminishing returns?

Show solution

Each additional 10 units of capital per worker adds 20, then 15, then 10 to output per worker. Yes: diminishing returns to capital. This is why growth fueled only by capital accumulation slows down, and sustained growth also needs technology.

C. Sources of Growth: Capital, Human Capital, Technology

Problem 17. Classify each as physical capital, human capital, technology, natural resource, or none of these (labor force). (a) A bakery installs a new oven. (b) A nurse earns an advanced degree. (c) A new oil field is discovered. (d) A firm patents a new battery design. (e) A company trains its employees in new software.

Show solution

(a) Physical capital. (b) Human capital. (c) Natural resource. (d) Technology (proprietary). (e) Human capital.

Problem 18. Classify each. (a) The government builds a bridge. (b) A vaccination campaign improves worker health. (c) Immigration raises the labor force. (d) A trade secret for a manufacturing process. (e) A new management method that cuts waste.

Show solution

(a) Physical capital (public infrastructure). (b) Human capital (health). (c) A larger labor force: it raises total output, but not output per worker. (d) Proprietary technology. (e) Technology (a better way to organize production).

Problem 19. Which raises output per worker, and which only raises total output: (i) a larger labor force, (ii) more capital per worker, (iii) better technology, (iv) a more educated workforce?

Show solution

(ii), (iii) and (iv) raise output per worker (productivity). (i) raises total output, but not output per worker (it can even lower it through diminishing returns).

Problem 20. Why do patents and copyrights encourage economic growth?

Show solution

New ideas are costly to produce but cheap to copy. A patent gives the inventor temporary exclusive rights (a proprietary technology), so they can earn a return on R&D. Without that reward, firms would invest less in innovation. The trade-off is that monopoly protection limits how widely the idea is used.

Problem 21. How does technology differ from physical capital in how it can be used?

Show solution

Physical capital is used by one firm at a time: a machine cannot run two factories. Ideas can be used by many producers at once without being used up, so the benefits can spread (spillovers) across the economy.

Problem 22. True or false: "A country must have abundant natural resources to grow rich."

Show solution

False. Natural resources help, but human capital, physical capital, technology and good institutions matter more. Resource-poor Japan and South Korea grew rich, while many resource-rich countries remain poor.

D. PPC and LRAS Shifts

Problem 23. Say whether the PPC shifts out, shifts in, or does not shift. (a) Discovery of a new technology for both goods. (b) An earthquake destroys many factories. (c) A recession puts people out of work. (d) A new university program raises worker skills. (e) The economy moves along the PPC to produce more capital goods.

Show solution

(a) Out. (b) In. (c) No shift: a move inside the PPC. (d) Out (human capital). (e) No shift: a movement along the curve. The graph below applies to (a) and (d): the PPC shifts out.

Problem 24. Say whether LRAS shifts right, left or does not shift. (a) Productivity growth from new software. (b) A rise in the money supply. (c) A large number of skilled immigrants arrive. (d) A permanent increase in the minimum wage raises the natural rate of unemployment. (e) Higher government spending.

Show solution

(a) Right. (b) No shift (money is neutral in the long run). (c) Right. (d) Left (higher natural rate means lower potential output). (e) No shift (it shifts AD).

Problem 25. Country A devotes most of its resources to capital goods this year, while Country B devotes most to consumer goods. Which will have the larger PPC in the future, and what does A give up?

Show solution

Country A. More capital goods means a larger capital stock and a bigger outward shift of its PPC. A gives up consumer goods today (the opportunity cost of growth).

Problem 26. The economy is in a recession. Is moving back to the PPC economic growth?

Show solution

No. Moving from inside the PPC to the curve is recovery (using idle resources). Growth is an outward shift of the PPC (a rightward shift of LRAS), which raises potential output.

Problem 27. How is a rightward shift of LRAS related to an outward shift of the PPC? Show how long-run growth affects the AD-AS graph.

Show solution

Both show higher potential output. An outward PPC shift corresponds to a rightward LRAS shift. In AD-AS, growth moves full-employment output from Yf1 to Yf2. With SRAS shifting right as well, the price level can stay stable or fall while output rises.

Problem 28. A technology improves only the production of Good X. Draw the effect on the PPC.

Show solution

The PPC pivots outward along the X-axis; the Y-axis intercept stays the same.

E. Policies for Growth

Problem 29. For each policy, say which source of growth it targets. (a) Funding public universities. (b) A tax credit for R&D spending. (c) Investment tax incentives for new equipment. (d) Stronger property rights. (e) Public highway construction. (f) Subsidized childcare for working parents.

Show solution

(a) Human capital. (b) Technology. (c) Physical capital. (d) Institutions that encourage investment and technology. (e) Physical capital (infrastructure). (f) Labor force participation.

Problem 30. What is the opportunity cost of policies that promote growth?

Show solution

Higher saving and investment need lower consumption today. Public spending on education or infrastructure uses resources (or requires taxes or borrowing) that could be used otherwise. Growth policy trades current consumption for future output.

Problem 31. Why might large persistent budget deficits slow long-run growth?

Show solution

Deficits raise demand for loanable funds, so the real interest rate rises and private investment is crowded out. Less physical capital accumulation means a smaller rightward shift of LRAS over time.

Problem 32. Show on a production function the difference between (a) a country that invests more and (b) a country that adopts a new technology.

Show solution

(a) More capital per worker is a movement along the production function from A to B, with diminishing returns. (b) New technology shifts the function up: at the same capital per worker, output per worker is higher.

Problem 33. Poor countries often have lower productivity. Give two policies they might use to catch up.

Show solution

For example: invest in education and health (human capital) and infrastructure (physical capital); adopt existing technology through open trade and foreign investment; secure property rights and rule of law. Catch-up is faster when such institutions are in place.
