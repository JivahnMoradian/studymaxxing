3.4

Consumption, Saving & the Multiplier

Read the theory → Drill 3.4 →

A. MPC and MPS from Data

Problem 1. When disposable income rises from 400 to 500, consumption rises from 380 to 450. Find the MPC and the MPS.

Show solution

MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYd = (450 − 380) ÷ (500 − 400) = 70 ÷ 100 = 0.7.
MPS = 1 − 0.7 = 0.3.

Problem 2. A household has the following data. Find the MPC and the APC at each income level.

Disposable income Consumption
1,000 900
1,200 1,060
1,400 1,220
Show solution

MPC = (1,060 − 900) ÷ 200 = 0.8 (also (1,220 − 1,060) ÷ 200 = 0.8).
APC = C ÷ Yd: 900 ÷ 1,000 = 0.90; 1,060 ÷ 1,200 ≈ 0.883; 1,220 ÷ 1,400 ≈ 0.871.
The APC falls as income rises, but the MPC is constant.

Problem 3. Disposable income rises by 250 and saving rises by 100. Find the MPC and MPS.

Show solution

MPS = 100 ÷ 250 = 0.4.
MPC = 0.6 (consumption rose by 150).

Problem 4. A household receives a one-time bonus of 500 and spends 450 of it. What is its MPC? How much does it save?

Show solution

MPC = 450 ÷ 500 = 0.9.
Saving = 500 − 450 = 50 (MPS = 0.1).

Problem 5. If the MPC is 0.65, what are the MPS and the spending multiplier?

Show solution

MPS = 1 − 0.65 = 0.35.
Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.35 ≈ 2.86.

Problem 6. True or false: "The MPC can be larger than 1 if households borrow."

Show solution

False for the marginal propensity in the standard model: MPC = 1 − MPS and MPS is between 0 and 1.
(The average propensity can exceed 1 when households dissave, i.e., spend more than their income.)

B. The Spending Multiplier

Problem 7. MPC = 0.8 and government purchases rise by 25. Find the multiplier and the change in real GDP.

Show solution

Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 5.
ΔY = 5 × 25 = +125.

Problem 8. The MPS is 0.4 and exports rise by 36. Find the multiplier and ΔY.

Show solution

Multiplier = 1 ÷ MPS = 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5.
ΔY = 2.5 × 36 = +90.

Problem 9. MPC = 2/3 and investment rises by 45. Find ΔY.

Show solution

Multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 2/3) = 3.
ΔY = 3 × 45 = +135.

Problem 10. A 30 increase in government purchases raises real GDP by 75. Find the multiplier, the MPC, and the MPS.

Show solution

Multiplier = 75 ÷ 30 = 2.5.
Then 1 ÷ (1 − MPC) = 2.5, so 1 − MPC = 0.4, MPC = 0.6 and MPS = 0.4.

Problem 11. The multiplier is 2. Real GDP rises by 168. By how much did autonomous spending change, and what is the MPC?

Show solution

Δspending = 168 ÷ 2 = 84.
Multiplier of 2 means MPS = 0.5, so MPC = 0.5.

Problem 12. Why does a larger MPC produce a larger multiplier?

Show solution

Each round of spending creates income, and the MPC share of it is spent again. A larger MPC means less leaks into saving each round, so more rounds of meaningful spending and a larger total.

Problem 13. Economy A has MPC = 0.6 and Economy B has MPC = 0.85. Each increases government purchases by 30. Compare the changes in real GDP.

Show solution

A: multiplier 2.5, ΔY = 75.
B: multiplier 1 ÷ 0.15 ≈ 6.67, ΔY = 200.
B's GDP rises much more.

C. Tax and Transfer Multipliers

Problem 14. MPC = 0.75 and taxes rise by 20. Find the tax multiplier and ΔY.

Show solution

Tax multiplier = −0.75 ÷ 0.25 = −3.
ΔY = −3 × 20 = −60.

Problem 15. MPC = 0.6 and taxes are cut by 40. Find ΔY.

Show solution

Tax multiplier = −0.6 ÷ 0.4 = −1.5.
ΔY = −1.5 × (−40) = +60.

Problem 16. MPC = 0.9 and the government raises transfer payments by 10. Find the transfer multiplier and ΔY.

Show solution

Transfer multiplier = 0.9 ÷ 0.1 = 9.
ΔY = 9 × 10 = +90.

Problem 17. MPC = 0.7. Compare the effect on real GDP of (a) a 30 increase in G and (b) a 30 tax cut.

Show solution

(a) Spending multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.3 = 3.33, ΔY = 100.
(b) Tax multiplier = −0.7 ÷ 0.3 = −2.33, ΔY = −2.33 × (−30) = +70.
Government purchases are 30 stronger because all of G is spent in round one, but only 70% of the tax cut is.

Problem 18. Explain why the tax multiplier is smaller in size than the spending multiplier.

Show solution

A spending increase of 1 raises demand by the full 1 in round one.
A tax cut of 1 raises disposable income by 1, but households save the MPS share, so only MPC is spent in round one.
Since the first round is smaller by MPC, the whole sequence is smaller by the same factor: |tax multiplier| = MPC × spending multiplier = spending multiplier − 1.

Problem 19. MPC = 0.5. The government raises purchases by 40 and raises taxes by 20. Find the net change in real GDP.

Show solution

Spending multiplier = 2: 40 × 2 = +80.
Tax multiplier = −1: 20 × (−1) = −20.
Net ΔY = +60.

D. Using Multipliers to Close Gaps

(Assume the price level is fixed so the AD shift equals the change in real GDP, and ignore crowding out.)

Problem 20. Real GDP is 200 below potential and MPC = 0.8. What change in government purchases would close the gap? What change in taxes?

Show solution

Spending multiplier = 5, so ΔG = 200 ÷ 5 = +40.
Tax multiplier = −4, so ΔT = 200 ÷ (−4) = −50 (a 50 tax cut).

Problem 21. Real GDP is 140 above potential and MPC = 0.7. Find ΔG and ΔT to close the inflationary gap.

Show solution

AD must shift left by 140.
Spending multiplier = 3.33, so ΔG = −140 ÷ 3.33 = −42.
Tax multiplier = −2.33, so ΔT = −140 ÷ (−2.33) = +60.

Problem 22. Actual real GDP is 6,730 and potential output is 7,000. MPC = 0.9. Find the gap and the ΔG and ΔT needed.

Show solution

Gap = 7,000 − 6,730 = 270 (recessionary).
Multiplier = 10: ΔG = +27.
Tax multiplier = −9: ΔT = 270 ÷ (−9) = −30.

Problem 23. An economy has MPC = 0.6, actual GDP of 6,040, and potential of 6,400. Find ΔG needed, then ΔT needed.

Show solution

Gap = 360.
ΔG = 360 ÷ 2.5 = +144.
Tax multiplier = −1.5, so ΔT = 360 ÷ (−1.5) = −240.

E. The Balanced-Budget Multiplier

Problem 24. MPC = 0.7. The government raises purchases by 50 and raises taxes by 50. Find ΔY.

Show solution

Spending: 3.33 × 50 ≈ +166.7.
Taxes: −2.33 × 50 ≈ −116.7.
Net ΔY = +50, equal to ΔG (balanced-budget multiplier = 1).

Problem 25. Show algebraically that the balanced-budget multiplier equals 1 for any MPC.

Show solution
Δ Y = 11 - MPC Δ G - MPC1 - MPC Δ G = 1 - MPC1 - MPC Δ G = Δ G

Problem 26. MPC = 0.9. The government raises purchases by 80 and raises taxes by 50. Find ΔY and the change in the budget balance.

Show solution

Spending: 10 × 80 = +800.
Taxes: −9 × 50 = −450.
ΔY = +350.
The deficit grows by 80 − 50 = 30 at the initial income level (revenue also rises with income if taxes depend on income, ignored here).

F. The Multiplier Process (Rounds of Spending)

Problem 27. The government spends an extra 200 and MPC = 0.75. Find the spending in each of the first four rounds and the total effect.

Show solution

Round 1: 200.
Round 2: 200 × 0.75 = 150.
Round 3: 112.5.
Round 4: 84.4 (84.375).
Total = 200 ÷ 0.25 = 800.

Problem 28. Taxes are cut by 80 and MPC = 0.6. How much is spent in round one? What is the total change in real GDP?

Show solution

Round 1 spending = 0.6 × 80 = 48 (the rest, 32, is saved).
Total = tax multiplier × ΔT = (−1.5)(−80) = +120 (check: 48 ÷ 0.4 = 120).

Problem 29. Government purchases rise by 300 and MPC = 0.9. What is the cumulative spending after three rounds? What is the total effect?

Show solution

Rounds: 300, 270, 243.
Cumulative = 813.
Total = 300 × 10 = 3,000, so the first three rounds deliver only 27.1% of the total.

Problem 30. MPC = 0.8. What share of the total multiplier effect is achieved after five rounds?

Show solution

Share = 1 − 0.85 = 1 − 0.32768 ≈ 67.2%.

G. Consumption and Saving Functions

Problem 31. C = 200 + 0.75Yd. Find consumption and saving when Yd = 1,000, and the break-even income.

Show solution

C = 200 + 0.75(1,000) = 950.
S = 1,000 − 950 = 50.
Break-even: Yd = 200 + 0.75Yd gives Yd = 200 ÷ 0.25 = 800.

Problem 32. C = 120 + 0.6Yd. Write the saving function and find saving at Yd = 500.

Show solution

S = Yd − C = Yd − 120 − 0.6Yd = −120 + 0.4Yd.
At Yd = 500: S = −120 + 200 = 80 (and C = 420).

Problem 33. The saving function is S = −90 + 0.2Yd. Find the MPC, the consumption function, and C and S when Yd = 700.

Show solution

MPS = 0.2, so MPC = 0.8.
Consumption function: C = 90 + 0.8Yd.
At Yd = 700: C = 90 + 560 = 650, S = −90 + 140 = 50.

Problem 34. C = 150 + 0.7Yd. Find the APC at Yd = 500 and at Yd = 1,200. What happens to APC as income rises?

Show solution

At 500: C = 500, APC = 1.0.
At 1,200: C = 990, APC = 990 ÷ 1,200 = 0.825.
APC falls as income rises (the autonomous component matters less), while the MPC stays at 0.7.

Problem 35. Household wealth increases, so autonomous consumption rises from 200 to 230 (the MPC stays at 0.75). Does the consumption function shift or is it a movement along it? Find the break-even income.

Show solution

Shift upward by 30 (a change in something other than Yd).
New break-even: 230 ÷ 0.25 = 920 (up from 800).

Problem 36. For each, say whether it is a movement along the consumption function or a shift: (a) a tax cut raises disposable income; (b) consumer confidence falls; (c) stock prices rise; (d) disposable income falls due to higher taxes.

Show solution

(a) Movement along (Yd changes). (b) Shift down. (c) Shift up (wealth effect). (d) Movement along.

H. Exam-Style Problems

Problem 37. An economy has MPC = 0.6, actual real GDP of 6,040 and potential output of 6,400. (a) Identify the gap. (b) Calculate the change in government purchases that would close it. (c) Show on an AD-AS graph. (d) What happens to the price level?

Show solution

(a) Recessionary gap of 360.
(b) Multiplier = 2.5, ΔG = 360 ÷ 2.5 = +144.
(c) AD shifts right (horizontally by 360) to LRAS.
(d) The price level rises.

Problem 38. Households become more confident and raise autonomous consumption by 22. MPC = 0.75. (a) What happens to AD? (b) By how much does real GDP change at a fixed price level? (c) What happens to the price level in the short run, and why is the actual increase in real GDP smaller than your answer to (b)?

Show solution

(a) AD shifts right.
(b) ΔY = 4 × 22 = +88.
(c) The price level rises, which moves the economy back up along SRAS, so real GDP rises by less than 88.
Higher prices also reduce real wealth and raise interest rates.

Problem 39. Explain two reasons why real-world multipliers are smaller than 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).

Show solution

(1) Leakages: income taxes and imports take part of each round of income out of the domestic spending stream (extra saving, too). (2) Price-level and interest-rate effects: higher prices reduce the real spending, and higher interest rates (crowding out) cut investment. Expectations of future tax hikes can reduce spending as well.
