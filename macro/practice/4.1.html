4.1

Money, Banking & the Money Supply

Read the theory → Drill 4.1 →

A. Functions of Money and the Money Supply Measures

Problem 1. Name the function of money illustrated in each case: (a) a café posts every price in dollars; (b) Maya keeps $500 of her paycheck in a drawer to spend next spring; (c) a customer pays for coffee with a $5 bill; (d) a landlord quotes rent as "$1,800 per month".

Show solution

(a) Unit of account. (b) Store of value. (c) Medium of exchange. (d) Unit of account (the dollar is the yardstick for rent).

Problem 2. Explain how money solves the "double coincidence of wants" problem of barter.

Show solution

In a barter economy a trade happens only if each person has what the other wants and wants what the other has. Money acts as a medium of exchange: a farmer sells wheat to anyone for money, then uses the money to buy shoes from a cobbler who may not want wheat. Specialization and trade become far cheaper.

Problem 3. Classify each as commodity, commodity-backed, or fiat money: (a) cigarettes used for payment in a prison; (b) a Federal Reserve note today; (c) a paper certificate that can be exchanged for an ounce of gold on demand; (d) cowrie shells used as currency.

Show solution

(a) Commodity (cigarettes have value of their own). (b) Fiat (no intrinsic value; accepted by law and custom). (c) Commodity-backed. (d) Commodity (shells were valued for their own use as ornament). Fiat money works only because everyone expects others to accept it.

Problem 4. Classify each as part of M1, M2 but not M1, or neither: (a) $60 of coins in your pocket; (b) a checking account balance; (c) a savings account; (d) a share of stock; (e) an $8,000 certificate of deposit (CD) with a one-year term; (f) a retail money market fund balance; (g) an unused $5,000 credit-card limit; (h) a gold bar.

Show solution

(a) M1. (b) M1. (c) M2 only. (d) Neither (a financial asset, not money). (e) M2 only (small time deposit, under $100,000). (f) M2 only. (g) Neither (a credit card is a way to borrow, not money). (h) Neither.

Problem 5. Use the data (billions of dollars) to find M1 and M2. Currency in circulation 220; checkable deposits 480; savings deposits 1,800; small time deposits 350; retail money market funds 600; value of all stocks held by households 9,000.

Show solution

M1 = currency + checkable deposits = 220 + 480 = $700 billion.
M2 = M1 + savings + small time deposits + retail money market funds = 700 + 1,800 + 350 + 600 = $3,450 billion.
Stock holdings are not counted.

Problem 6. In a different year, M2 is $4,980 billion. Currency is 400, checkable deposits are 920, small time deposits are 560, and retail money market funds are 780 (all billions). How large are savings deposits?

Show solution

M1 = 400 + 920 = 1,320.
M2 − M1 = 4,980 − 1,320 = 3,660, which equals savings + small time + money market funds.
Savings = 3,660 − 560 − 780 = $2,320 billion.

Problem 7. Currency in circulation is $360 billion and banks hold $240 billion of reserves. Find the monetary base. Then, if a customer deposits $10 billion of currency into a checking account, what happens to M1 and to the monetary base?

Show solution

Monetary base = 360 + 240 = $600 billion.
Depositing currency turns currency in circulation into bank reserves (−10 to currency, +10 to reserves).
M1 is unchanged for the moment (currency falls 10, checkable deposits rise 10) and the monetary base is unchanged (600).
Money is created only once the bank lends the new reserves.

Problem 8. Rank from most to least liquid: a house, a checking account, a share of stock, $20 in your wallet, a savings account.

Show solution

Most to least liquid: cash ($20)
→ checking account → savings account → share of stock (sold within days at a market price) → house (slow and costly to sell). Money (M1) is the most liquid category by definition.

B. Required and Excess Reserves

(Every bank below holds only reserves, securities, and loans as assets. Net worth is zero unless a table shows otherwise.)

Problem 9. First Prairie Bank has deposits of $300,000, reserves of $45,000, and loans of $255,000. The required reserve ratio is 11%. Find required reserves, excess reserves, and the most the bank can lend.

Show solution

Required = 11% × $300,000 = $33,000.
Excess = $45,000 − $33,000 = $12,000.
The bank can lend at most its excess reserves, $12,000.

Problem 10. Harbor Savings has deposits of $480,000, reserves of $96,000, and loans of $384,000. The required reserve ratio is 15%. Find required reserves, excess reserves, and the most the bank can lend.

Show solution

Required = 15% × $480,000 = $72,000.
Excess = $96,000 − $72,000 = $24,000.
The bank can lend at most its excess reserves, $24,000.

Problem 11. Delta Trust has deposits of $1,200,000, reserves of $150,000, and loans of $1,050,000. The required reserve ratio is 8%. Find required reserves, excess reserves, and the most the bank can lend.

Show solution

Required = 8% × $1,200,000 = $96,000.
Excess = $150,000 − $96,000 = $54,000.
The bank can lend at most its excess reserves, $54,000.

Problem 12. Mesa Bank has deposits of $640,000, reserves of $80,000, and loans of $560,000. The required reserve ratio is 12.5%. Find required reserves, excess reserves, and the most the bank can lend.

Show solution

Required = 12.5% × $640,000 = $80,000, exactly equal to reserves, so excess = $0.
The bank is fully loaned up and can make no new loans unless it gains reserves.

Problem 13. Lakeside Bank has deposits of $350,000, reserves of $49,000, and loans of $301,000. The required reserve ratio is 18%. Find required reserves, excess reserves, and the most the bank can lend.

Show solution

Required = 18% × $350,000 = $63,000, but reserves are only $49,000, a shortfall of $14,000.
Excess reserves are negative, so the bank can lend nothing and must raise reserves (call in loans, sell securities, or borrow) to comply.

Problem 14. A bank has deposits of $750,000 and required reserves of $90,000. What is the required reserve ratio?

Show solution

rr = required reserves ÷ deposits = 90,000 ÷ 750,000 = 12%.

Problem 15. A bank must hold $36,000 in required reserves and the required reserve ratio is 6%. How large are its deposits?

Show solution

Deposits = required reserves ÷ rr = 36,000 ÷ 0.06 = $600,000.

Problem 16. A bank's assets are reserves of $70,000, securities of $100,000, and loans of $500,000. Its only liability is deposits of $600,000. The reserve ratio is 10%. (a) What is its net worth? (b) How large are its excess reserves, and can it use the securities to make new loans without further action?

Show solution

(a) Net worth = assets − liabilities = $670,000 − $600,000 = $70,000.
(b) Required = 0.10 × $600,000 = $60,000;
excess = $70,000 − $60,000 = $10,000.
Securities are not reserves, so they do not count toward the requirement or lending capacity until sold (selling them for cash raises reserves).

C. Making Loans and T-Account Changes

Problem 17. A bank has deposits of $500,000, reserves of $80,000, loans of $420,000, and rr = 14%. (a) Find its excess reserves. (b) Show its T-account after it lends out all of its excess reserves.

Show solution

(a) Required = 0.14 × $500,000 = $70,000, so excess = $80,000 − $70,000 = $10,000.
(b) The loan moves reserves out and loans in: reserves = $70,000, loans = $430,000, deposits unchanged at $500,000.
The bank is now fully loaned up.

Problem 18. A bank is exactly fully loaned up with deposits of $400,000, reserves of $64,000, and rr = 16%. A customer deposits $25,000 in cash. (a) Show the T-account after the deposit. (b) Find the new excess reserves. (c) What is the maximum new loan?

Show solution

(a) Reserves rise to $89,000 and deposits to $425,000.
(b) Required = 0.16 × $425,000 = $68,000;
excess = $89,000 − $68,000 = $21,000, which is the deposit times (1 − rr): 25,000 × 0.84.
(c) The maximum new loan is $21,000.

Problem 19. Using the bank in Problem 17 (deposits $500,000, reserves $80,000, rr = 14%), suppose the bank lends $25,000, which is more than its excess reserves of $10,000. The borrower spends the money and the sellers deposit it at other banks. What problem does the bank face?

Show solution

Reserves fall to $55,000 while deposits stay at $500,000, so required reserves are still $70,000. The bank is $15,000 short of its requirement ($70,000 − $55,000). It would have to recall loans, sell securities, or borrow reserves. This is why a bank can lend no more than its excess reserves.

Problem 20. A bank has deposits of $800,000, reserves of $120,000, and rr = 12%. Customers withdraw $40,000 in cash. Find the bank's excess reserves before and after the withdrawals.

Show solution

Before: required = $96,000, excess = $120,000 − $96,000 = $24,000.
After: deposits = $760,000 and reserves = $80,000;
required = 0.12 × $760,000 = $91,200;
excess = $80,000 − $91,200 = −$11,200, a shortfall of $11,200.
Withdrawals drain reserves by the full amount but reduce required reserves by only 12% of it.

Problem 21. A bank with $18,000 in excess reserves uses all of it to buy Treasury securities instead of making loans. Show the change in its T-account and say what happens to the money supply.

Show solution

Reserves −$18,000, securities +$18,000; deposits and loans are unchanged. The bank's reserves are now the required amount only. No new deposits are created, so the money supply does not expand (the seller of the securities deposits the payment, but that merely transfers reserves among banks).

Problem 22. The Fed buys $50,000 of Treasury securities from a bank that was fully loaned up (rr = 7%). Show the bank's T-account change and the maximum new loan the bank can now make.

Show solution

The bank swaps securities for reserves: securities −$50,000, reserves +$50,000. Deposits are unchanged, so required reserves are unchanged and all $50,000 is excess. The bank can lend up to $50,000, and the banking system can expand deposits by up to $50,000 ÷ 0.07 ≈ $714,286.

Problem 23. A bank has deposits of $600,000, reserves of $108,000, loans of $492,000, and rr = 9%. A borrower repays $30,000 of a loan by writing a check on her account at the same bank. Show the new T-account and how excess reserves change.

Show solution

Loans fall to $462,000 and deposits fall to $570,000; reserves stay at $108,000.
Required reserves fall from $54,000 to $51,300, so excess reserves rise from $54,000 to $56,700, an increase of $2,700 (= 9% × $30,000).
Repaying loans destroys money; new lending creates it.

D. The Money Multiplier and Changes in the Money Supply

Problem 24. Find the money multiplier for each required reserve ratio: (a) 50%; (b) 25%; (c) 4%; (d) 100%.

Show solution

Multiplier = 1/rr.
(a) 1 ÷ 0.50 = 2.
(b) 1 ÷ 0.25 = 4.
(c) 1 ÷ 0.04 = 25.
(d) 1 ÷ 1 = 1.
With 100% reserve banking banks cannot lend, so the multiplier is 1 and banks create no money.

Problem 25. If the money multiplier is 40, what is the reserve ratio? What if the multiplier is 2.5?

Show solution

rr = 1 ÷ multiplier.
For 40: 1 ÷ 40 = 2.5%.
For 2.5: 1 ÷ 2.5 = 40%.

Problem 26. Banks are fully loaned up and the reserve ratio is 7.5%. The Fed adds $90 million of reserves to the banking system. What is the maximum change in checkable deposits (the money supply)?

Show solution

Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.075 ≈ 13.33.
Maximum ΔM = 90 × 13.33 = $1,200 million (= 90 ÷ 0.075).

Problem 27. Customers deposit $4,500 of currency that had been held outside the banking system. The reserve ratio is 30% and banks lend out all excess reserves. What are the maximum total change in checkable deposits and the maximum total new loans in the banking system?

Show solution

Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.30 ≈ 3.33.
Maximum total change in deposits = 4,500 ÷ 0.30 = $15,000.
Maximum new loans = deposits created − initial deposit = 15,000 − 4,500 = $10,500 (or 4,500 × 0.70 ÷ 0.30).

Problem 28. Banks have total deposits of $6,000,000 and are exactly fully loaned up at rr = 10%. The Fed cuts the reserve ratio to 5%. By how much can the banking system now expand deposits, at most?

Show solution

Reserves are $600,000.
At 5%, required reserves on the current deposits are $300,000, so excess = $300,000.
Maximum ΔD = excess ÷ rr = $300,000 ÷ 0.05 = $6,000,000.
Check: total deposits can reach $600,000 ÷ 0.05 = $12,000,000, which is $6,000,000 more than now.

Problem 29. Banks are fully loaned up with a reserve ratio of 4%. The Fed sells securities to banks and the banks' reserves fall by $25 million. By how much can the money supply change?

Show solution

ΔM = ΔReserves × 1/rr = −25 ÷ 0.04 = −$625 million.
The money supply contracts by up to $625 million (loans must be called in or not renewed).

Problem 30. The monetary base is $800 billion and the money supply (M1) is $3,200 billion. (a) What is the actual money multiplier (M ÷ monetary base)? (b) If the reserve ratio is 10%, what does the simple formula predict, and why is the actual value lower?

Show solution

(a) Actual multiplier = 3,200 ÷ 800 = 4.
(b) The simple formula predicts 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10.
The actual value is lower because banks hold excess reserves and the public holds currency (which sits outside banks and cannot be re-lent), so each round of lending is smaller than the simple model assumes.

E. Rounds of Deposit Expansion

Problem 31. An $800 cash deposit enters the banking system and rr = 25%. Banks lend all of their excess reserves each time, and every loan is redeposited. Fill in the first four rounds, then find the total change in deposits and in loans after all rounds.

Show solution
Round New deposit Required reserves (25%) New loan
1 $800 $200 $600
2 $600 $150 $450
3 $450 $112.5 $337.5
4 $337.5 $84.4 $253.1

Each round is 75% of the one before.
Over all rounds: total deposits = 800 ÷ 0.25 = $3,200 and total loans = 3,200 − 800 = $2,400 (total required reserves = 0.25 × 3,200 = 800, the original deposit).

Problem 32. A $1,500 deposit starts an expansion with rr = 30%. How large is the new deposit created in round 4, and how much have deposits increased cumulatively after four rounds?

Show solution

Each round is 70% of the last: round 4 = 1,500 × 0.70³ = $514.50.
Cumulative deposits = 1,500 + 1,050 + 735 + 514.50 = $3,799.50 (the formula 1,500 × (1 − 0.70⁴) ÷ 0.30 gives the same).
The limit as rounds continue is 1,500 ÷ 0.30 = $5,000.

Problem 33. A bank receives a new $600 deposit and the reserve ratio is 8%. Find the maximum total deposits created and the maximum total loans made in the whole banking system.

Show solution

Maximum total deposits = 600 ÷ 0.08 = $7,500.
Total loans = 7,500 − 600 = $6,900.

Problem 34. After a new $450 deposit, the banking system's checkable deposits eventually rise by a total of $3,000. Assuming banks lend all excess reserves, what is the reserve ratio?

Show solution

Total ΔD = initial deposit ÷ rr, so rr = 450 ÷ 3,000 = 15% (multiplier = 3,000 ÷ 450 ≈ 6.67).

Problem 35. New reserves of $900 enter the banking system. The required reserve ratio is 10%, but banks also choose to hold an additional 5% of deposits as excess reserves. What is the maximum increase in deposits, and how does it compare with the simple-multiplier prediction?

Show solution

Banks effectively hold 10% + 5% = 15% of deposits as reserves, so the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.15 ≈ 6.67 and ΔD = 900 ÷ 0.15 = $6,000.
The simple formula predicts 900 ÷ 0.10 = $9,000.
Voluntary excess reserves shrink the expansion by $3,000, which is why the textbook multiplier is only a maximum.

F. Exam-Style Multi-Step Problems

Problem 36. Westfield Bank has reserves of $48,000, securities of $70,000, loans of $640,000, and deposits of $700,000. The required reserve ratio is 4%. (a) Find required and excess reserves. (b) What is the most the bank can lend? (c) If this lending is redeposited and re-lent through the banking system, what is the maximum total change in the money supply? (d) The Fed now doubles the reserve ratio to 8%. What does Westfield have to do, and which way does this push the money supply?

Show solution

(a) Required = 0.04 × $700,000 = $28,000;
excess = $48,000 − $28,000 = $20,000.
(b) At most $20,000.
(c) Maximum ΔM = excess ÷ rr = $20,000 ÷ 0.04 = $500,000.
(d) Required reserves become 0.08 × $700,000 = $56,000, which exceeds reserves of $48,000: a shortfall of $8,000.
Westfield must sell securities or call in loans, and across the whole system the higher ratio forces deposits and the money supply to contract.

Problem 37. The banking system has $2,000 billion in checkable deposits and no excess reserves at a reserve ratio of 20%. The Fed lowers the ratio to 10%. (a) What is the multiplier before and after? (b) How much can the money supply increase, at most? (c) Is the answer to (b) a maximum or an exact prediction? Explain.

Show solution

(a) Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5 before and 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10 after.
(b) Reserves are 0.20 × 2,000 = $400 billion.
At 10%, deposits can reach 400 ÷ 0.10 = $4,000 billion, so the money supply can rise by $2,000 billion.
(c) It is a maximum: if banks hold extra reserves or households keep more currency, the increase is smaller.

Problem 38. A bank has deposits of $1,000,000 and reserves of $100,000, with rr = 10%. Rumors lead depositors to demand $250,000 in cash all at once. (a) How much can the bank pay from reserves? (b) Why is a fractional-reserve bank vulnerable to a run, and how does deposit insurance help?

Show solution

(a) Reserves are only $100,000, which is 10% of deposits, so the bank can pay just $100,000 immediately and is $150,000 short; the rest of its assets are tied up in loans that cannot be called in at once. (b) Because banks lend out most deposits, they cannot repay everyone simultaneously. Each depositor, fearing others will withdraw first, has an incentive to run, which can make a healthy bank fail. Deposit insurance (the FDIC) assures depositors their money is safe, removing the incentive to run.

Problem 39. Ana deposits $2,000 of cash she was holding into a checking account. The reserve ratio is 20% and banks lend all excess reserves. (a) What is the maximum total increase in checkable deposits? (b) What is the maximum increase in M1 (currency plus checkable deposits)? (c) Why do the two differ?

Show solution

(a) Maximum ΔD = $2,000 ÷ 0.20 = $10,000.
(b) Ana's currency left circulation, so currency fell by $2,000 while deposits rose by $10,000: ΔM1 = $10,000 − $2,000 = $8,000.
(c) The initial $2,000 was already counted in M1 as currency.
Only the part created by new loans ($8,000) is new money.
If instead the reserves came from the Fed (for example through a bond purchase), the whole $10,000 is new money.
