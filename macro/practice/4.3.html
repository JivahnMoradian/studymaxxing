4.3

The Money Market & Loanable Funds

Read the theory → Drill 4.3 →

A. Money Market Shifts

Problem 1. The Fed buys government bonds in an open market operation. Show the effect in the money market and say what happens to the nominal interest rate and to bond prices.

Show solution

The Fed's purchase adds reserves, so the money supply shifts right (MS1 to MS2). At the old rate people hold more money than they want, so they buy bonds, bond prices rise, and the nominal interest rate falls.

Problem 2. The price level rises (and real GDP is unchanged). What happens to money demand and the nominal interest rate, other things equal?

Show solution

Higher prices mean more dollars are needed for the same purchases, so money demand shifts right. With the money supply fixed, the nominal interest rate rises.

Problem 3. A recession reduces real GDP and incomes. Using the money market, find the effect on the nominal interest rate, assuming the Fed holds the money supply constant.

Show solution

Lower income means fewer transactions, so money demand shifts left. The money supply is fixed, so the nominal interest rate falls.

Problem 4. Mobile payments and widely accepted credit cards let people hold less cash. What happens to money demand and the interest rate?

Show solution

Households need less money for transactions, so money demand shifts left and the nominal interest rate falls (if the Fed leaves MS unchanged).

Problem 5. The Fed sells government bonds. Show the effect on the money market and say what happens to the interest rate and to bond prices.

Show solution

Banks pay for the bonds with reserves, so the money supply shifts left. People now hold less money than they want, so they sell bonds: bond prices fall and the nominal interest rate rises.

Problem 6. The Fed lowers the required reserve ratio. What happens to the money supply and the nominal interest rate?

Show solution

A lower required ratio frees excess reserves, so banks can lend more and the money supply increases (shifts right). The nominal interest rate falls.

Problem 7. For each event, say whether the nominal interest rate rises, falls, or is ambiguous (the money supply is controlled by the Fed in each case): (a) real GDP rises and the Fed sells bonds; (b) the price level falls and the Fed buys bonds; (c) real GDP rises and the Fed buys bonds; (d) the Fed lowers the discount rate and a recession lowers real GDP.

Show solution

(a) MD right and MS left: i rises (both push up). (b) MD left and MS right: i falls. (c) MD right (pushes i up) and MS right (pushes i down): ambiguous. (d) MS right and MD left: i falls.

Problem 8. As the nominal interest rate rises, what happens to the quantity of money demanded? Is this a shift or a movement along the curve? Explain using opportunity cost.

Show solution

The quantity of money demanded falls: a movement along the MD curve (the interest rate is the variable on the vertical axis, so changing it does not shift the curve). Holding money costs the interest you could have earned on a bond, so a higher i makes people hold less money and more bonds.

Problem 9. Explain why the money supply curve is vertical and the money demand curve slopes downward.

Show solution

The Fed sets the quantity of money; it does not change with the interest rate, so MS is vertical. MD slopes down because the nominal interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money: a lower rate makes money cheaper to hold, so people want more of it.

B. Money Market Equilibrium with Numbers

(Money in $ billions; i in percent.)

Problem 10. Money demand is Md = 700 − 50i and the money supply is 400. Find the equilibrium nominal interest rate.

Show solution

Set Md = MS: 700 − 50i = 400, so 50i = 300 and i = 6%.

Problem 11. In Problem 10, the Fed raises the money supply to 475. What is the new equilibrium interest rate?

Show solution

700 − 50i = 475, so 50i = 225 and i = 4.5%.
More money lowers the rate from 6% to 4.5%.

Problem 12. Money demand is Md = 1200 − 80i and the money supply is 800. (a) Find the equilibrium rate. (b) The Fed wants the rate to be 3%. What money supply is needed, and by how much must it change?

Show solution

(a) 1200 − 80i = 800 gives i = 400 ÷ 80 = 5%.
(b) At i = 3, money demand is 1200 − 80(3) = 960, so set MS = 960, an increase of 160.

Problem 13. Money demand is Md = 900 − 60i and MS = 480. Rising income shifts money demand to Md = 1020 − 60i. (a) Find the interest rate before and after. (b) What money supply keeps the rate at its original level?

Show solution

(a) Before: 900 − 60i = 480 gives i = 7%.
After: 1020 − 60i = 480 gives i = 9%.
(b) To hold i at 7%, need MS = 1020 − 60(7) = 600.0 (an increase of 120.0).

Problem 14. Money demand is Md = 800 − 50i and MS = 495. The price level rises 10%, which raises nominal money demand by 10% at every interest rate. Find the interest rate before and after.

Show solution

Before: 800 − 50i = 495, so i = 305 ÷ 50 = 6.1%.
After: 1.10 × (800 − 50i) = 880 − 55i.
Setting this equal to 495: 385 = 55i, so i = 7%.
A higher price level raises the interest rate unless the Fed adds money.

Problem 15. The table gives money demand at different nominal interest rates. The money supply is 500. (a) Find equilibrium i. (b) If the Fed raises MS to 700, what is the new rate?

Nominal interest rate 2% 4% 6% 8%
Money demanded 900 700 500 300
Show solution

(a) Money demanded equals 500 at i = 6%.
(b) Money demanded equals 700 at i = 4%.

C. Loanable Funds: Shifts

Problem 16. The government's budget deficit increases. Show the effect on the loanable funds market. What happens to the real interest rate and to private investment?

Show solution

The government borrows more, so demand for loanable funds shifts right. The real interest rate rises. The higher rate makes private projects less attractive, so private investment falls (crowding out).

Problem 17. Firms become optimistic about future profits and plan more investment. What happens to the real interest rate and the quantity of loanable funds?

Show solution

Demand for loanable funds shifts right: r rises and the quantity of funds lent and invested rises.

Problem 18. Households become more thrifty and save a larger share of income. Show the effect and describe what happens to r and investment.

Show solution

The supply of loanable funds shifts right. r falls and investment rises.

Problem 19. The government moves from a deficit to a budget surplus. What happens to the supply of loanable funds, the real interest rate, and investment?

Show solution

Public saving rises, so national saving rises and the supply of loanable funds shifts right. r falls and investment rises.

Problem 20. Congress passes an investment tax credit that makes new equipment cheaper. What happens in the loanable funds market?

Show solution

The tax credit raises the return on investment, so firms want to borrow more: demand shifts right, r rises, and the quantity of loanable funds and investment rise.

Problem 21. Foreign savers decide to buy more of this country's assets and lend more here. Show the effect on the real interest rate and on domestic investment.

Show solution

Foreign funds add to the supply of loanable funds: supply shifts right. r falls and domestic investment rises.

Problem 22. In the loanable funds market, the demand for funds shifts right at the same time as the supply shifts right. What happens to r and to the quantity of loanable funds?

Show solution

The quantity of loanable funds rises (both shifts increase it). The effect on r is ambiguous: higher demand pushes r up while higher supply pushes it down. It depends on which shift is larger.

D. Loanable Funds with Numbers

(Loanable funds in $ billions; r in percent. In the numeric graphs, the vertical axis is the real interest rate r (labelled P) and the horizontal axis is the quantity of loanable funds (labelled Q).)

Problem 23. Demand for loanable funds is Qd = 900 − 60r and supply is Qs = 100 + 40r. Find the equilibrium real interest rate and quantity.

Show solution

900 − 60r = 100 + 40r, so 800 = 100r and r* = 8%.
Q* = 900 − 60(8) = 420.

Problem 24. In Problem 23, private investment demand is the 900 − 60r above. The government now also borrows 100 (a deficit) regardless of the rate, so total demand is Qd = 1000 − 60r. Find the new equilibrium rate and how much private investment is crowded out.

Show solution

1000 − 60r = 100 + 40r, so 900 = 100r and r* = 9%, with total borrowing of 460.
Private investment demand at 9% is 900 − 60(9) = 360, down from 420, so 60 of private investment is crowded out.
The other 100 of the 460 total goes to the government.

Problem 25. Starting from Problem 23's market, households save more and supply becomes Qs = 200 + 40r (with demand still 900 − 60r). Find the new equilibrium.

Show solution

900 − 60r = 200 + 40r, so 700 = 100r and r* = 7% and Q* = 480.
The rate falls from 8% to 7% and investment rises from 420 to 480.

Problem 26. Demand is Qd = 600 − 25r and supply is Qs = 50 + 30r. Find r* and Q*. Then find the shortage or surplus if the real interest rate were stuck at 6%.

Show solution

600 − 25r = 50 + 30r gives 550 = 55r, so r* = 10% and Q* = 350.
At r = 6: Qd = 600 − 150 = 450, Qs = 50 + 180 = 230, so there is a shortage of 220, which pushes r up toward 10%.

Problem 27. Demand is Qd = 450 − 20r and supply is Qs = −30 + 40r. An investment boom raises demand to Qd = 570 − 20r. Find the equilibrium before and after, and describe the change.

Show solution

Before: 450 − 20r = −30 + 40r gives 480 = 60r, so r* = 8%, Q* = 290.
After: 570 − 20r = −30 + 40r gives 600 = 60r, so r* = 10%, Q* = 370.
The real interest rate rises by 2 points and investment (and saving) rise by 80.

Problem 28. Demand is Qd = 1000 − 50r and supply is Qs = 100 + 30r. A recession lowers expected profits so demand falls to Qd = 800 − 50r. Find the change in the equilibrium rate and quantity.

Show solution

Before: 900 = 80r, so r* = 11.25% and Q* = 437.5.
After: 700 = 80r, so r* = 8.75% and Q* = 362.5.
The real interest rate falls by 2.5 points and investment falls by 75.

E. National Saving, Private Saving, and Public Saving

(Closed economies.)

Problem 29. A closed economy has GDP of 9,500, consumption of 6,100, government purchases of 2,000, and taxes net of transfers of 1,900 (all in $ billions). Find private saving, public saving, national saving, and investment. Is there a budget deficit or surplus?

Show solution

Private saving = Y − T − C = 9,500 − 1,900 − 6,100 = 1,500.
Public saving = T − G = 1,900 − 2,000 = −100, a budget deficit of 100.
National saving = 1,500 + (−100) = 1,400, which also equals Y − C − G = 9,500 − 6,100 − 2,000.
In a closed economy investment = 1,400.

Problem 30. GDP is 4,200, consumption is 2,700, government purchases are 700, and net taxes are 800 ($ billions, closed economy). Find private, public, and national saving and investment.

Show solution

Private = 4,200 − 800 − 2,700 = 700.
Public = 800 − 700 = 100 (a surplus).
National = 800 = investment.

Problem 31. In a closed economy GDP is 5,000, government purchases are 900, and national saving is 650. How large is consumption, and how large is investment?

Show solution

S = Y − C − G, so C = Y − G − S = 5,000 − 900 − 650 = 3,450.
Investment = S = 650.

Problem 32. A closed economy has Y = 7,000 and C = 4,700. The government raises its purchases from 1,500 to 1,700, and neither Y nor C changes. What happens to national saving and investment?

Show solution

Before: S = 7,000 − 4,700 − 1,500 = 800.
After: S = 7,000 − 4,700 − 1,700 = 600.
Saving falls by 200, so investment falls by 200 to 600.
This is crowding out: more government spending leaves fewer funds for private investment.

Problem 33. In a closed economy, Y = 6,000 and G = 1,200. Before a tax cut T = 1,300 and C = 3,800. The tax cut lowers T to 1,150 and consumption rises to 3,890. Find private, public, and national saving before and after.

Show solution

Before: private = 6,000 − 1,300 − 3,800 = 900;
public = 1,300 − 1,200 = 100;
national = 1,000.
After: private = 6,000 − 1,150 − 3,890 = 960;
public = 1,150 − 1,200 = −50;
national = 910.
Private saving rose by 60 but public saving fell by 150 (a larger drop), so national saving fell by 90.

Problem 34. Tax revenue is 1,450 and government purchases are 1,380. What is public saving, and is the budget in deficit or surplus? If the surplus doubles, what happens to the supply of loanable funds?

Show solution

Public saving = 1,450 − 1,380 = 70, a budget surplus.
Doubling it to 140 raises national saving, so the supply of loanable funds shifts right and the real interest rate falls.

Problem 35. Which of these count as investment in the macroeconomic sense, and which are saving or neither? (a) A firm builds a new warehouse. (b) You buy shares of Microsoft stock from another investor. (c) A family buys a newly built house. (d) A bakery adds unsold loaves to its inventory. (e) You deposit $500 into a savings account.

Show solution

(a) Investment (new capital). (b) Neither (a purchase of an existing financial asset; it is saving by you, and the seller gets cash). (c) Investment (new residential construction). (d) Investment (inventory change). (e) Saving (a financial transaction, not a purchase of new capital).

F. Exam-Style: Putting the Markets Together

Problem 36. Decide whether each question is best analyzed in the money market (nominal i) or the loanable funds market (real r), and give the effect: (a) the Fed buys bonds; (b) households increase their saving; (c) the price level rises; (d) the government runs a larger deficit.

Show solution

(a) Money market: MS right, i falls. (b) Loanable funds: S right, r falls. (c) Money market: MD right, i rises. (d) Loanable funds: D right, r rises (crowding out).

Problem 37. The government increases its budget deficit to finance a stimulus package. (a) Which market and which curve shifts? (b) What happens to the real interest rate? (c) What happens to private investment, and what does this imply for future potential output?

Show solution

(a) The loanable funds market; demand shifts right. (b) r rises. (c) Private investment falls (crowding out), so the capital stock grows more slowly and future potential output (LRAS) is lower than it otherwise would be.

Problem 38. A recession lowers real GDP. The Fed wants to keep the nominal interest rate unchanged. (a) What happens to money demand? (b) What must the Fed do to the money supply, and which tool does that? (c) Draw both curves.

Show solution

(a) Money demand shifts left. (b) To keep i from falling, the Fed must reduce the money supply by selling bonds (or raising the reserve ratio). (c) MD shifts left, and MS shifts left by an equal horizontal amount so the new equilibrium is at the same i.

Problem 39. The Fed buys bonds, lowering the nominal interest rate. (a) What happens to the price of existing bonds? (b) Which people benefit? (c) Predict the effect on investment spending and explain the direction.

Show solution

(a) Existing bond prices rise (interest rates and bond prices move inversely). (b) Borrowers, such as firms and home buyers, since loans become cheaper. (c) Investment rises: a lower interest rate makes more projects profitable, which will increase aggregate demand (Unit 4.4).
