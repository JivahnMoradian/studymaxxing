4.2

Financial Assets & Time Value of Money

Read the theory → Drill 4.2 →

A. Future Value

Problem 1. Ines deposits $2,000 in an account paying 5.5% interest per year, compounded annually. How much will be in the account after 10 years?

Show solution

FV = $2,000 × (1 + 0.055)10 = $2,000 × 1.7081 = $3,416.29.

Problem 2. Kofi deposits $500 in an account paying 7.5% interest per year, compounded annually. How much will be in the account after 6 years?

Show solution

FV = $500 × (1 + 0.075)6 = $500 × 1.5433 = $771.65.

Problem 3. A trust fund deposits $12,000 in an account paying 3% interest per year, compounded annually. How much will be in the account after 25 years?

Show solution

FV = $12,000 × (1 + 0.03)25 = $12,000 × 2.0938 = $25,125.34.

Problem 4. Compare $1,000 invested for 30 years at 6% per year with simple interest (interest only on the original amount) and with annual compounding. How much more does compounding earn?

Show solution

Simple: $1,000 + 30 × (0.06 × $1,000) = $2,800.
Compound: $1,000 × 1.0630 = $5,743.49.
Compounding earns $2,943.49 more, because interest itself earns interest.

Problem 5. $2,500 grows to $2,704 in 2 years with annual compounding. What is the interest rate?

Show solution

$2,500 × (1 + r)2 = $2,704, so (1 + r)2 = 1.0816 and 1 + r = √1.0816 = 1.04.
The rate is 4%.

Problem 6. Challenge: $3,000 is invested at a stated rate of 8% per year, with interest paid and compounded twice a year (4% every six months). What is the balance after 3 years, and how does it compare with annual compounding?

Show solution

There are 6 half-year periods at 4%: $3,000 × 1.046 = $3,795.96.
Annual compounding gives $3,000 × 1.083 = $3,779.14.
The more frequent compounding earns $16.82 more.

Problem 7. Dara saves $5,000 at 6% interest for 10 years while prices rise 2% a year. (a) What is her nominal balance? (b) What is that balance worth in today's purchasing power?

Show solution

(a) $5,000 × 1.0610 = $8,954.24.
(b) Prices rise by a factor of 1.0210 = 1.2190, so the balance buys what $8,954.24 ÷ 1.2190 = $7,345.59 buys today.
The real gain is smaller than the nominal gain because inflation erodes purchasing power.

B. Present Value

Problem 8. A scholarship will pay $10,000 in 5 years. The interest rate is 6.5%. What is the present value?

Show solution

PV = $10,000 ÷ 1.0655 = $10,000 ÷ 1.3701 = $7,298.81.

Problem 9. You can take $9,000 today or $11,000 in 3 years. The interest rate is 4%. Which is worth more, and by how much in today's dollars?

Show solution

PV of the future payment = $11,000 ÷ 1.043 = $9,778.96.
That is more than $9,000, so waiting is better by $778.96 in present-value terms.

Problem 10. A prize pays $60,000 at the end of each of the next 3 years. The interest rate is 7%. What is the present value of the prize?

Show solution

PV = 60,000 ÷ 1.07 + 60,000 ÷ 1.072 + 60,000 ÷ 1.073 = $56,074.77 + $52,406.32 + $48,977.87 = $157,458.96.
It is less than the $180,000 headline amount.

Problem 11. How much must be deposited today at 8% interest to have $50,000 in 18 years?

Show solution

PV = $50,000 ÷ 1.0818 = $50,000 ÷ 3.9960 = $12,512.45.

Problem 12. What is the present value of $20,000 received in 10 years if the interest rate is (a) 3% and (b) 9%? What do your answers say about interest rates and present value?

Show solution

(a) $20,000 ÷ 1.0310 = $14,881.88.
(b) $20,000 ÷ 1.0910 = $8,448.22.
A higher interest rate lowers present value: the same future payment is worth less today.

Problem 13. A firm can spend $14,000 on equipment that will return $16,500 in 3 years (no other payments). Should it buy if the interest rate is (a) 3%? (b) 7%?

Show solution

(a) PV of the return = $16,500 ÷ 1.033 = $15,099.84 > $14,000: buy.
(b) $16,500 ÷ 1.073 = $13,468.91 < $14,000: do not buy.
A higher interest rate reduces the value of future returns, so fewer investment projects pass the test, which is why investment falls as rates rise.

C. The Rule of 70

Problem 14. Real GDP grows 2.8% a year. About how many years until it doubles?

Show solution

70 ÷ 2.8 = 25 years.

Problem 15. A country wants its income per person to double in 10 years. What annual growth rate does it need?

Show solution

Rate ≈ 70 ÷ years = 70 ÷ 10 = 7% per year.

Problem 16. Inflation runs steadily at 2.5% a year. How long until the price level doubles? What happens to the purchasing power of cash over that period?

Show solution

70 ÷ 2.5 = 28 years.
After 28 years the price level is twice as high, so a given amount of cash buys only half as much as before.

Problem 17. Country A's income per person grows 2% a year and Country B's grows 4%. Both start at 10,000. Approximately how much will each have after 70 years?

Show solution

A doubles every 70 ÷ 2 = 35 years, so it doubles twice in 70 years: 10,000 × 4 = 40,000.
B doubles every 70 ÷ 4 = 17.5 years, or four times in 70 years: 10,000 × 16 = 160,000.
(Exact: 1.0270 = 4.00 and 1.0470 = 15.57.) A small difference in growth rates makes a huge difference over generations.

Problem 18. How long will it take an investment growing 9% a year to quadruple using the rule of 70? Compare with the exact time to double.

Show solution

Doubling takes about 70 ÷ 9 ≈ 7.8 years, so quadrupling (two doublings) takes about 15.6 years. The exact doubling time is ln 2 ÷ ln 1.09 ≈ 8.04 years, so the rule is a close approximation.

Problem 19. A government's debt grows 6% a year while the economy's nominal GDP grows only 3.5%. About how long does each take to double, and what does this imply for the debt-to-GDP ratio?

Show solution

Debt doubles in 70 ÷ 6 ≈ 11.7 years;
GDP doubles in 70 ÷ 3.5 = 20 years.
Debt grows faster than GDP, so the debt-to-GDP ratio rises over time.

D. Bond Prices and Interest Rates

Problem 20. Existing bonds pay a fixed coupon. Say what happens to the price of an existing bond in each case: (a) the market interest rate rises from 4% to 7%; (b) the market rate falls from 6% to 4%; (c) the issuer is downgraded and buyers now require a higher yield; (d) a bond with a 30-year maturity versus one with 2 years, when rates change.

Show solution

(a) Price falls. (b) Price rises. (c) Price falls (yield and price move in opposite directions). (d) The 30-year bond's price moves more, because its fixed payments are discounted over a longer time.

Problem 21. A zero-coupon bond has a face value of $1,000 and matures in 3 years. What is its price if the market interest rate is 3.5%?

Show solution

Price = $1,000 ÷ (1 + 0.035)3 = $901.94.

Problem 22. A zero-coupon bond has a face value of $1,000 and matures in 3 years. What is its price if the market interest rate is 8%? (This is the same bond as the previous problem, with a higher rate.)

Show solution

Price = $1,000 ÷ (1 + 0.08)3 = $793.83.
When the rate rises from 3.5% to 8%, the price falls by $108.11.

Problem 23. A bond with a $1,000 face value pays a 6% coupon and matures in one year. Find its price if the market rate is (a) 4%; (b) 8%.

Show solution

Coupon = 0.06 × $1,000 = $60, and the final payment is $1,060.
(a) $1,060 ÷ 1.04 = $1,019.23 (above face: coupon is higher than market rate).
(b) $1,060 ÷ 1.08 = $981.48 (below face).

Problem 24. A two-year bond has a $1,000 face value and a 7% annual coupon. The market interest rate is 9%. Find the price. Is the bond selling at a premium or a discount?

Show solution

Coupon = $70.
Price = 70 ÷ 1.09 + 1,070 ÷ 1.092 = $64.22 + $900.60 = $964.82.
That is below face value, so it sells at a discount (coupon rate < market rate).

Problem 25. A bond with a 5% coupon and a $1,000 face value is selling at exactly $1,000. If the market interest rate rises, will the bond sell at a premium or a discount? What if the rate falls?

Show solution

Rate rises above 5%: the bond pays less than new bonds, so it sells at a discount (price < $1,000). Rate falls below 5%: it pays more than new bonds, so it sells at a premium (price > $1,000). When the market rate equals the coupon rate, price equals face value.

Problem 26. An investor buys a one-year zero-coupon bond for $925. It pays $1,000 at maturity. What is the yield?

Show solution

Yield = ($1,000 − $925) ÷ $925 = 75 ÷ 925 = 8.11%.

Problem 27. Hana buys a new two-year zero-coupon bond with a $5,000 face value when the market rate is 6%. Right after she buys it, rates jump to 9%. (a) What did she pay? (b) What is the bond worth after the jump? (c) What if she holds it to maturity?

Show solution

(a) $5,000 ÷ 1.062 = $4,449.98.
(b) $5,000 ÷ 1.092 = $4,208.40, a loss of $241.58 if she sells now.
(c) Held to maturity she still receives $5,000, so the loss is only on paper.
Selling early is what exposes her to interest-rate risk.

Problem 28. The Fed raises interest rates unexpectedly. Describe what happens to (a) the price of existing bonds, (b) a bank that holds a lot of long-term bonds, (c) a firm planning to issue new bonds.

Show solution

(a) Existing bond prices fall. (b) The bank's assets lose value; if it must sell them it takes a loss. (c) The firm must offer a higher coupon rate to attract buyers, so borrowing is more expensive.

E. Risk, Return, Liquidity, and Diversification

Problem 29. Rank these assets from lowest to highest expected risk and expected return: a savings deposit, a start-up company's stock, a 10-year US Treasury bond, a stock in a large stable company, a BBB-rated corporate bond.

Show solution

Lowest to highest: savings deposit
→ Treasury bond → BBB corporate bond → large-firm stock → start-up stock. Higher expected return compensates for higher risk, so the ranking is the same for both.

Problem 30. Classify each risk as firm-specific or market risk, and say whether diversification can remove it: (a) a CEO resigns amid scandal at one company; (b) a recession lowers profits at nearly every firm; (c) a factory fire at a single retailer; (d) the Fed raises interest rates sharply; (e) a rival launches a better product against one firm.

Show solution

(a) Firm-specific. (b) Market. (c) Firm-specific. (d) Market. (e) Firm-specific. Diversification removes firm-specific risk by holding many stocks, but cannot remove market risk, which hits all of them.

Problem 31. Stock X returns +30% in a boom and −10% in a bust; Stock Y returns −10% in a boom and +30% in a bust. Booms and busts are equally likely. (a) Find the expected return of each stock. (b) Find the return of a portfolio split 50-50 in each state. What does this show?

Show solution

(a) X: 0.5(30) + 0.5(−10) = 10%.
Y: 0.5(−10) + 0.5(30) = 10%.
(b) Boom: 0.5(30) + 0.5(−10) = 10%; bust: 0.5(−10) + 0.5(30) = 10%.
The portfolio earns 10% in both states, so its risk is zero.
Diversification works when assets' returns move in opposite directions; real stocks mostly move together with the market, so only part of the risk disappears.

Problem 32. Ahmet increases his portfolio from 2 stocks to 40 stocks. What happens to the firm-specific risk and the market risk of his portfolio?

Show solution

Firm-specific risk falls (close to zero), but market risk does not change: it affects all 40 stocks in the same way.

Problem 33. Classify each feature as describing a bond, a stock, or both: (a) represents ownership; (b) a promise of fixed payments; (c) paid first in bankruptcy; (d) price can fall if interest rates rise; (e) its return includes capital gains.

Show solution

(a) Stock. (b) Bond. (c) Bond (creditors come before owners). (d) Both (bond prices fall directly; stock prices fall because future dividends are discounted more). (e) Both can have capital gains, but it is the main source for stock.

Problem 34. Predict whether each event raises or lowers the price of a share of Alpha Corp: (a) analysts expect higher profits; (b) market interest rates rise; (c) Alpha cuts its expected future dividends; (d) rates fall and profits are expected to be unchanged.

Show solution

A stock's price is the present value of expected dividends. (a) Rises. (b) Falls (higher discount rate). (c) Falls. (d) Rises.

Problem 35. A municipal bond pays 3% interest, exempt from federal tax. A corporate bond of the same risk pays 4.5% taxable. An investor is in the 30% tax bracket. Which gives the higher after-tax return?

Show solution

Corporate after tax = 4.5% × (1 − 0.30) = 3.15%, which beats the municipal bond's 3%.
This is why municipal bonds, being tax-exempt, can offer lower yields.
A buyer in a higher bracket (above about 33%) would prefer the municipal bond (4.5 × 0.667 = 3.0).

Problem 36. Why do long-term bonds usually pay a higher interest rate than short-term bonds, and why do low-rated corporate bonds pay more than Treasury bonds?

Show solution

Long-term bonds carry more price risk and uncertainty (their value moves more when rates change and the issuer has more time to fail), and lenders tie up funds longer, so they demand compensation. Low-rated corporate bonds have a higher default risk, so investors demand a risk premium over safe Treasuries.

F. Exam-Style Problems

Problem 37. A saver has $10,000 and two options for 8 years. Option 1: an account paying 6% compounded annually. Option 2: an account paying 5% compounded annually plus a one-time $1,500 bonus at the end of year 8. Which is worth more at year 8, and by how much?

Show solution

Option 1: 10,000 × 1.068 = $15,938.48.
Option 2: 10,000 × 1.058 + 1,500 = $14,774.55 + 1,500 = $16,274.55.
Option 2 is better by $336.07.

Problem 38. A 25-year-old wants $1,000,000 at age 65 (40 years) and expects a 7% annual return. (a) How much must she deposit today (one lump sum)? (b) Use the rule of 70 to check that your answer is about right.

Show solution

(a) PV = $1,000,000 ÷ 1.0740 = $1,000,000 ÷ 14.974 = $66,780.
(b) At 7%, money doubles every 70 ÷ 7 = 10 years, so in 40 years it doubles 4 times (×16). $1,000,000 ÷ 16 = $62,500, close to the exact answer (the rule slightly overstates how fast money grows, so it gives a slightly low present value).

Problem 39. A one-year bond has a $1,000 face value and a 5% coupon (it pays $1,050 at maturity). (a) What is its price when the market rate is 5%? (b) What is its price when the rate rises to 10%? (c) Compute the percentage change in the price. (d) Explain, using present value, why the price fell.

Show solution

(a) 1,050 ÷ 1.05 = $1,000.00.
(b) 1,050 ÷ 1.10 = $954.55.
(c) (954.55 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 = -4.55%.
(d) The bond's fixed payment is discounted at a higher rate, so its present value falls.
Equivalently, buyers will pay less for a 5% bond when new bonds pay 10%.
