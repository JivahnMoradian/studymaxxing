3.2

Aggregate Supply

Read the theory → Drill 3.2 →

A. Classifying SRAS Shifters

Problem 1. The price of crude oil rises sharply and stays high. How does SRAS shift?

Show solution

Oil is an input to many industries, so costs rise: SRAS shifts left. Price level rises and real GDP falls (stagflation).

Problem 2. A new software system raises worker productivity across the economy. How does SRAS shift?

Show solution

Cost per unit of output falls: SRAS shifts right. (LRAS also shifts right, because capacity has grown; see Part B.)

Problem 3. Workers and firms expect inflation to rise next year, so new contracts include bigger wage increases. How does SRAS shift?

Show solution

Nominal wages (a cost) rise: SRAS shifts left.

Problem 4. The government cuts taxes on business payrolls (a tax on employing workers). How does SRAS shift?

Show solution

Costs of production fall: SRAS shifts right.

Problem 5. A drought destroys a large share of the harvest. How does SRAS shift?

Show solution

An adverse supply shock raises food and input costs: SRAS shifts left.

Problem 6. The government enacts stricter environmental regulations that raise compliance costs for firms. How does SRAS shift?

Show solution

Higher compliance costs: SRAS shifts left. (If the regulation does not reduce capacity, LRAS does not change.)

Problem 7. Unions negotiate lower wage growth than expected. How does SRAS shift?

Show solution

Nominal wages are lower than otherwise: SRAS shifts right.

Problem 8. The government gives manufacturers a subsidy per unit produced. How does SRAS shift?

Show solution

A subsidy reduces net cost per unit: SRAS shifts right.

Problem 9. Imported materials become cheaper because the domestic currency appreciates. How does SRAS shift?

Show solution

Imported inputs cost less: SRAS shifts right.

Problem 10. Consumers become more optimistic and spend more. Does SRAS shift?

Show solution

No. Consumer spending is a demand change (AD shifts right). SRAS does not shift. Moving up along SRAS, the price level and output both rise.

B. SRAS or LRAS, or Both?

Problem 11. A wave of immigration increases the number of workers. Which curves shift?

Show solution

More labor raises productive capacity, so LRAS shifts right. Because capacity is up, SRAS shifts right too.

Problem 12. Nominal wages across the economy rise 5% with no change in productivity. Which curves shift?

Show solution

SRAS shifts left only. Wages are a cost but do not change the economy's physical capacity, so LRAS does not shift.

Problem 13. A hurricane destroys factories and roads. Which curves shift?

Show solution

The capital stock falls, so potential output falls: LRAS shifts left and SRAS shifts left.

Problem 14. Businesses build more factories and buy more equipment. Which curves shift in the long run?

Show solution

Physical capital rises: LRAS and SRAS both shift right (economic growth).

Problem 15. The government spends on public schools and job training, and workers become more skilled. Which curves shift?

Show solution

Human capital rises: LRAS and SRAS shift right (over time).

Problem 16. The price of oil falls temporarily because of a one-time surge in supply. Which curves shift?

Show solution

SRAS shifts right; LRAS does not shift, because the economy's resources and technology are unchanged.

Problem 17. The government raises the minimum wage for a large group of workers. Which supply curve shifts?

Show solution

Higher labor costs: SRAS shifts left. LRAS is unchanged unless the natural rate of unemployment changes. (Some economists argue a higher minimum wage raises the natural rate, which would also shift LRAS left.)

Problem 18. For each event, state whether it shifts SRAS only, LRAS and SRAS, or neither: (a) a rise in consumer confidence; (b) a technology breakthrough; (c) a drop in expected inflation; (d) a fall in government spending.

Show solution

(a) Neither (AD shifts). (b) Both shift right. (c) SRAS only (right). (d) Neither (AD shifts left).

C. Supply Shocks: Effects on Price Level, Output, Unemployment

Problem 19. The economy is at long-run equilibrium. An oil embargo shifts SRAS left. What happens to the price level, real GDP, and unemployment?

Show solution

Price level rises, real GDP falls below potential, and unemployment rises above the natural rate. This is stagflation.

Problem 20. A positive supply shock shifts SRAS right. What happens to the price level, real GDP, and unemployment?

Show solution

Price level falls, real GDP rises above potential, and unemployment falls below the natural rate.

Problem 21. Why is stagflation a problem for policymakers?

Show solution

The goals conflict. Raising AD to fight unemployment pushes the price level up further; lowering AD to fight inflation deepens the recession. Policy can fix only one problem at a time.

Problem 22. Explain, in words, why the short-run effect of a drop in nominal wages is higher output but the long-run effect on output is zero.

Show solution

Lower wages cut costs, so SRAS shifts right and output rises. In the long run, the extra output pushes down the price level until the real wage is back at its original level: output returns to potential, so LRAS (which did not shift) governs the long-run outcome.

Problem 23. Distinguish a movement along SRAS from a shift of SRAS with one example of each.

Show solution

Movement: a higher price level with unchanged wages makes firms produce more (up along SRAS). Shift: an increase in oil prices raises costs at every price level (SRAS left).

Problem 24. After a negative supply shock, some economists urge the central bank to raise AD to restore output. What is the likely side effect?

Show solution

Raising AD pushes output back toward potential but pushes the price level even higher than the shock alone did, so inflation rises further.

Problem 25. True or false: "Stagflation can be caused by a decrease in aggregate demand."

Show solution

False. A fall in AD lowers both output and the price level. Stagflation (falling output with a rising price level) requires a leftward shift of SRAS.

D. Output Gaps and Real Wages (Calculations)

Problem 26. Potential output is 10,000 and actual output is 9,400. Compute the output gap in dollars and as a percent.

Show solution

Gap = 9,400 − 10,000 = −600.
Percent = −600 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = −6%.
Output is below potential.

Problem 27. Actual real GDP is 5,460 and potential output is 5,200. Compute the percent output gap. Is it a recessionary or inflationary gap?

Show solution

(5,460 − 5,200) ÷ 5,200 × 100 = +5%.
Actual output is above potential: inflationary gap.

Problem 28. Real GDP is 2,310 and potential output is 2,400. Find the percent output gap.

Show solution

(2,310 − 2,400) ÷ 2,400 × 100 = −3.75% (a recessionary gap).

Problem 29. Actual output is $15.6 trillion and the output gap is −2.5%. Find potential output.

Show solution

Y = 0.975 × Yf, so Yf = 15.6 ÷ 0.975 = $16.0 trillion.

Problem 30. Workers have a nominal wage of $22 per hour fixed for the year. The price index rises from 100 to 110. Find the real wage. What happens to firms' willingness to hire?

Show solution

Real wage = 22 ÷ 110 × 100 = $20, down from $22.
Labor is cheaper in real terms, so firms hire more and produce more.
This is why SRAS slopes up.

Problem 31. A contract fixes the nominal wage at $18. The price index rises from 100 to 120. Find the real wage. If prices had instead fallen to 90, what would it be?

Show solution

Rise to 120: 18 ÷ 120 × 100 = $15.
Fall to 90: 18 ÷ 90 × 100 = $20.
A falling price level raises the real wage, so firms hire fewer workers and output falls.

Problem 32. Nominal wages rise 6% and productivity rises 2%. Approximately how much does unit labor cost change, and what happens to SRAS?

Show solution

Unit labor cost ≈ 6% − 2% = +4% (exactly 1.06 ÷ 1.02 − 1 ≈ 3.9%).
Costs rise, so SRAS shifts left.

Problem 33. Nominal wages rise 3% and productivity rises 5%. What happens to unit labor cost and SRAS?

Show solution

Unit labor cost ≈ 3% − 5% = −2% (exactly 1.03 ÷ 1.05 − 1 ≈ −1.9%).
Costs fall, so SRAS shifts right.

Problem 34. Potential output is 5,000 and grows 3% per year. Find potential output after one year and after two years.

Show solution

Year 1: 5,000 × 1.03 = 5,150.
Year 2: 5,150 × 1.03 = 5,304.5, about 5,305.
LRAS shifts right each year.

Problem 35. LRAS moves from 4,800 to 5,040. What is the growth rate of potential output?

Show solution

(5,040 − 4,800) ÷ 4,800 × 100 = 5% growth.

E. Exam-Style Problems

Problem 36. An economy is in long-run equilibrium. A war in a major oil-producing region doubles oil prices. (a) Show the effect on a graph. (b) What happens to the price level, output, and unemployment? (c) Does LRAS shift?

Show solution

(a) SRAS shifts left. (b) Price level rises, output falls below Yf, unemployment rises above the natural rate. (c) No, unless the shock persists and damages capacity.

Problem 37. An economy's LRAS is 8,200 and the economy is at potential. The labor force grows 2.5% with no change in productivity. (a) What is the new LRAS (assume output grows with labor)? (b) Show the change on a graph.

Show solution

(a) 8,200 × 1.025 = 8,405.
(b) LRAS shifts right, and with higher capacity SRAS shifts right as well.
With AD held fixed, the price level falls (PL1 to PL2).

Problem 38. Classify each as a change in AD, SRAS, LRAS, or a combination: (a) lower interest rates; (b) a breakthrough in battery technology; (c) a ban on imports of key materials; (d) a large increase in the labor force.

Show solution

(a) AD right (I rises). (b) SRAS and LRAS right. (c) SRAS left (higher input costs). (d) SRAS and LRAS right.

Problem 39. Workers in an economy expect 6% inflation, but actual inflation turns out to be 2%. Their nominal wages were set for 6%. What happens to real wages and to output in the short run?

Show solution

Wages rose faster than prices, so real wages rise. Firms hire fewer workers and produce less: with wages set high, SRAS is further left than if expectations had been accurate, so output falls and unemployment rises in the short run.
