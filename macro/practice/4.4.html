Monetary Policy & the Fed

Read the theory → Drill 4.4 →

A. The Fed, Its Tools, and Direction of Use

Problem 1. Answer true or false and correct any false statements: (a) The FOMC has 12 voting members. (b) Governors serve 4-year terms. (c) The Fed's dual mandate is maximum employment and stable prices. (d) The Fed sets the discount rate as the rate banks charge each other.

Show solution

(a) True. (b) False: governors serve 14-year terms (so they are insulated from short-term politics). (c) True. (d) False: the discount rate is what the Fed charges banks for loans; the rate banks charge each other is the federal funds rate.

Problem 2. Which of the following are tools of monetary policy? (a) selling government bonds; (b) raising the income tax rate; (c) lowering the discount rate; (d) clearing checks between banks; (e) raising the reserve requirement; (f) FDIC insurance of deposits.

Show solution

Tools: (a), (c), (e). (b) is fiscal policy. (d) is a Fed service, not a policy tool. (f) is deposit insurance, not monetary policy.

Problem 3. The economy is in a deep recession. Name three actions the Fed could take, say whether each is expansionary or contractionary, and describe its effect on the money supply and the interest rate.

Show solution

All three are expansionary: (1) Buy bonds (OMO): reserves and MS up, i down. (2) Lower the discount rate: banks borrow more reserves, MS up, i down. (3) Lower the reserve requirement: excess reserves rise, the multiplier rises, MS up, i down.

Problem 4. Inflation is far above target. Name three Fed actions to fight it and describe their effect on the money supply and the interest rate.

Show solution

All three are contractionary: (1) Sell bonds: reserves and MS down, i up. (2) Raise the discount rate: banks borrow less, MS down, i up. (3) Raise the reserve requirement: excess reserves fall, MS down, i up.

Problem 6. The Fed buys $60 million of Treasury securities from Marlow Bank. Show the T-account changes for the Fed and for Marlow Bank.

Show solution

The Fed creates reserves to pay for the securities. Fed: securities +$60m (asset) and reserves +$60m (liability to the banks). Marlow Bank: securities −$60m, reserves +$60m (total assets unchanged). The bank now has $60m more excess reserves to lend.

B. The Transmission Mechanism

Problem 7. The Fed buys bonds. Write the chain of effects from the open market operation to real GDP, and show the money market and the effect on AD.

Show solution

Fed buys bonds → bank reserves ↑ → money supply ↑ → nominal interest rate ↓ → investment (and interest-sensitive consumption) ↑ → AD shifts right
→ real GDP ↑, price level ↑, unemployment ↓.

Problem 8. The Fed sells bonds to fight inflation. Write the chain of effects and show the money market and AD.

Show solution

Fed sells bonds → bank reserves ↓ → money supply ↓ → nominal interest rate ↑ → investment ↓ → AD shifts left
→ real GDP ↓, price level ↓ (or inflation slows), unemployment ↑.

Problem 9. The FOMC lowers its target for the federal funds rate. Describe what the Fed does with OMO, what happens in the money market, and what happens to investment and AD.

Show solution

To lower the rate, the Fed buys bonds, increasing reserves and the money supply. In the money market MS shifts right and the interest rate falls. Cheaper borrowing raises investment, so AD shifts right.

Problem 10. Why does a rate cut raise investment? Name two other types of spending that respond to lower interest rates.

Show solution

A lower interest rate lowers the cost of borrowing, so projects that were not profitable before now are, and firms invest more. Households also buy more durable goods such as cars and new homes (mortgage rates fall), and lower rates raise asset prices, adding a wealth effect.

Problem 11. After a large cut in the nominal interest rate, firms barely increase their investment. Give two reasons the policy may be weak.

Show solution

(1) Investment is not very sensitive to interest rates when firms are pessimistic about demand and profits. (2) Banks may hold excess reserves instead of lending, or tighten credit standards, so the extra reserves do not become loans and the money supply grows less than the multiplier suggests.

Problem 12. Preview of the open economy: a lower US interest rate makes US bonds less attractive to foreign investors. What happens to the demand for dollars, the value of the dollar, and net exports? Is this an extra boost to AD?

Show solution

Foreign demand for dollar assets falls, so the dollar depreciates. A cheaper dollar makes US goods cheaper abroad, so exports rise, imports fall, and net exports rise. This adds to AD (another transmission channel; details in Unit 6).

C. Choosing Monetary Policy for a Gap (AD-AS)

Problem 13. The economy is in a recession with a recessionary gap and unemployment above the natural rate. (a) What should the Fed do and with which tool? (b) Show the effect in the money market and in AD-AS. (c) What happens to real GDP, the price level, and unemployment?

Show solution

(a) Expansionary policy: buy bonds (or lower the discount rate or reserve ratio). (b) MS shifts right, i falls, investment rises, AD shifts right to LRAS. (c) Real GDP rises to potential, the price level rises, unemployment falls toward the natural rate.

Problem 14. Real GDP is above potential and unemployment is below the natural rate. (a) What is the gap and what is likely to happen to prices? (b) What should the Fed do? (c) Show the effect.

Show solution

(a) An inflationary gap: wages and prices will rise. (b) Contractionary policy: sell bonds (or raise the discount rate or reserve ratio). (c) MS shifts left, i rises, investment falls, AD shifts left until output equals potential, with a lower price level than otherwise.

Problem 15. The economy is in a recession, but the Fed raises the reserve requirement. Describe the effect on the money supply, the interest rate, AD, and the gap.

Show solution

The higher ratio reduces excess reserves and lending, so MS falls, i rises, investment falls, and AD shifts left. Real GDP falls further below potential, so the recessionary gap widens and unemployment rises (a policy mistake).

Problem 16. For each economy, say whether the Fed should use expansionary policy, contractionary policy, or no change.

Economy Real GDP vs potential Unemployment vs natural rate Inflation vs target
A below above below
B above below above
C equal equal on target
D below above on target
Show solution

A: expansionary (recessionary gap). B: contractionary (inflationary gap). C: no change (long-run equilibrium). D: expansionary (recessionary gap with stable prices, so policy can lower unemployment without inflation risk).

Problem 17. A rise in oil prices shifts SRAS left, raising the price level and lowering output and employment (stagflation). Show it in AD-AS and explain the Fed's dilemma.

Show solution

Output falls (recession) while the price level rises. Expansionary policy would shift AD right to restore output but would push prices up further. Contractionary policy would fight inflation but deepen the recession. Monetary policy cannot fix both problems.

Problem 18. The economy is at full employment (long-run equilibrium). The Fed mistakenly increases the money supply a lot. Describe the short-run and long-run results.

Show solution

Short run: i falls, investment rises, AD shifts right: output rises above potential and the price level rises (an inflationary gap). Long run: unemployment is below the natural rate, so wages rise, SRAS shifts left, output returns to potential, and the price level ends higher. The only lasting effect is higher prices (money neutrality).

Problem 19. In a recession, compare relying on the economy's self-correction with using expansionary monetary policy. What are the benefits and drawbacks of each?

Show solution

Self-correction: wages fall, SRAS shifts right and the price level falls, restoring full employment, but this can be slow and painful (high unemployment for a long time). Expansionary policy: shifts AD right and closes the gap faster, but the price level ends higher, and policy lags mean it could arrive too late or overshoot.

Problem 20. Fill in the direction of change for each variable when the Fed conducts expansionary monetary policy: money supply, nominal interest rate, investment, aggregate demand, real GDP, price level, unemployment, bond prices.

Show solution

Money supply ↑, nominal interest rate ↓, investment ↑, AD ↑ (shifts right), real GDP ↑, price level ↑, unemployment ↓, bond prices ↑.

D. Sizing Open Market Operations

(Assume banks are fully loaned up and there is no currency drain unless stated.)

Problem 21. The required reserve ratio is 10%. How large an open market operation would raise the money supply by $500 million? Should the Fed buy or sell?

Show solution

Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10.
OMO = ΔM ÷ multiplier = $500 million × 0.1 = $50 million.
The Fed should buy securities.

Problem 22. The required reserve ratio is 12.5%. How large an open market operation would raise the money supply by $1.2 billion? Should the Fed buy or sell?

Show solution

Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.125 = 8.
OMO = ΔM ÷ multiplier = $1.2 billion × 0.125 = $0.15 billion.
The Fed should buy securities.

Problem 23. The reserve ratio is 20% and the Fed wants to reduce the money supply by $300 million. What should it do?

Show solution

Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5.
OMO = 300 ÷ 5 = $60 million.
The Fed should sell $60 million of securities.

Problem 24. The reserve ratio is 14% and the Fed sells $35 million of Treasury securities to banks. What is the maximum change in the money supply?

Show solution

ΔM = −35 ÷ 0.14 = −$250 million (the money supply falls by up to $250 million).

Problem 25. The required reserve ratio is 6%, but banks also keep an extra 9% of deposits as excess reserves. The Fed buys $45 million of bonds. Compare the money supply increase predicted by the simple multiplier with the increase that actually occurs.

Show solution

Simple: 45 ÷ 0.06 = $750 million.
Actual: banks hold 6% + 9% = 15% of deposits as reserves, so ΔM = 45 ÷ 0.15 = $300 million.
Voluntary excess reserves reduce the expansion by $450 million.

Problem 26. Money demand is Md = 1000 − 50i and the money supply is 700 ($ million). The reserve ratio is 20%. The Fed wants a federal funds rate of 4%. (a) What is the current interest rate? (b) What money supply is needed? (c) What size OMO achieves it?

Show solution

(a) 1000 − 50i = 700 gives i = 6%.
(b) At i = 4: Md = 1000 − 50(4) = 800, so MS must rise to 800 (ΔM = 100).
(c) OMO = ΔM × rr = 100 × 0.20 = $20 million of bond purchases.

E. Changing the Reserve Requirement and the Discount Rate

Problem 27. Banks are fully loaned up with reserves of $300 million and a reserve ratio of 20%. The Fed lowers the ratio to 15%. (a) What are deposits before? (b) What is the maximum level of deposits after? (c) By how much can the money supply change?

Show solution

(a) Deposits = 300 ÷ 0.20 = $1500 million.
(b) Deposits can reach 300 ÷ 0.15 = $2000 million.
(c) ΔM = +$500 million.
The multiplier rises from 5 to 6.67.

Problem 28. Why is the reserve requirement rarely used to adjust monetary policy, while open market operations are used constantly?

Show solution

Changing the required ratio forces every bank to rearrange its balance sheet at once, and even a small change has a large effect through the multiplier, so it is a blunt tool. Open market operations can be done in any size, at any time, are easily reversed, and are controlled entirely by the Fed.

F. The Reserve Market: Ample vs. Scarce Reserves

Problem 29. Draw and label the market for reserves in the ample reserves range. What determines the federal funds rate here, and what is the Fed's main tool for changing it?

Show solution

Supply of reserves is vertical and crosses demand on the flat part at the bottom, so the federal funds rate equals the IORB. The Fed's main tool is to change the IORB, which shifts the flat part of demand.

Problem 30. Reserves are scarce. The Fed sells government bonds. What happens to the supply of reserves, the quantity of reserves and the federal funds rate? Show it on a graph.

Show solution

Selling bonds removes reserves, so supply of reserves shifts left. The quantity of reserves falls and, because supply crosses the downward-sloping part of demand, the federal funds rate rises (E1 to E2).

Problem 31. Reserves are ample. The Fed buys a large quantity of bonds from banks. What happens to the quantity of reserves and to the federal funds rate? Show it on a graph.

Show solution

Supply of reserves shifts right, so the quantity of reserves rises. The new supply curve still crosses the flat part of demand, so the federal funds rate is unchanged (still at the IORB). Open market operations do not move the rate in the ample range.

Problem 32. Reserves are ample and inflation is above target. Describe how the Fed can raise the federal funds rate, and what happens next in the economy.

Show solution

The Fed raises the IORB. The flat part of demand shifts up, so the federal funds rate rises from E1 to E2 while the quantity of reserves stays the same. Other interest rates rise, investment falls, AD shifts left, and the price level and output fall, closing the inflationary gap.

Problem 33. Reserves are ample and the economy is in a recession. Show and explain what happens if the Fed lowers the IORB.

Show solution

The flat part of demand shifts down, so the federal funds rate falls (E1 to E2) while the quantity of reserves is unchanged. Lower interest rates raise investment, and AD shifts right, raising output and the price level and closing the recessionary gap.

Problem 34. True or false, and correct any false statement: (a) In the ample range, an open market purchase lowers the federal funds rate. (b) In the scarce range, an open market sale raises the federal funds rate. (c) In the ample range, the Fed controls the federal funds rate mainly through the IORB. (d) The reserve market graph shows the money supply on the horizontal axis.

Show solution

(a) False: the quantity of reserves rises but the rate stays at the IORB. (b) True: supply shifts left along the sloping part of demand. (c) True. (d) False: the horizontal axis shows the quantity of reserves, and the vertical axis shows the federal funds rate (the money market graph is the one with the money supply).

Problem 35. The demand for reserves is flat at the discount rate of 6.5% for Q up to 1.5, then the federal funds rate equals 8 − Q between Q = 1.5 and Q = 5.5 (Q in $ trillions, rate in %), then it is flat at the IORB of 2.5%. (a) Supply of reserves is 3. Find the federal funds rate and say whether reserves are scarce or ample. (b) The Fed buys 1.5 of bonds. Find the new rate. (c) How much more must the Fed buy before reserves become ample, and what is the rate after that?

Show solution

(a) On the sloping part, rate = 8 − 3 = 5%.
Supply crosses the sloping part, so reserves are scarce.
(b) New Q = 3 + 1.5 = 4.5, so rate = 8 − 4.5 = 3.5%.
(c) Reserves become ample at Q = 5.5 (where the rate reaches the IORB of 2.5%), so the Fed must buy 1 more.
Beyond that, the rate stays at 2.5% no matter how many more bonds it buys.

G. Exam-Style Problems

Problem 36. An economy has real GDP of $9 trillion, potential output of $10 trillion, and unemployment of 8% (natural rate 5%). (a) Identify the gap. (b) Describe the Fed policy, its tool, and its effect in the money market. (c) Explain how AD changes and what happens to the unemployment rate, real GDP, and the price level. (d) Name one limitation of the policy.

Show solution

(a) A recessionary gap of $1 trillion. (b) Expansionary: buy bonds (OMO). MS shifts right and the nominal interest rate falls. (c) Lower i raises investment, so AD shifts right: real GDP rises toward $10 trillion, unemployment falls toward 5%, and the price level rises. (d) Lags (or: investment may respond weakly if firms are pessimistic).

Problem 37. The Fed wants to expand the money supply by $400 million, banks are fully loaned up, and rr = 16%. (a) Which tool and what size? (b) Which direction do bond prices and the interest rate move? (c) What happens to AD? (d) If banks actually hold excess reserves, will ΔM be larger or smaller than $400 million?

Show solution

(a) OMO: buy $400 × 0.16 = $64 million of bonds.
(b) Bond prices rise; the interest rate falls.
(c) Investment rises, so AD shifts right.
(d) Smaller: excess reserves reduce the effective multiplier below 1/rr.

Problem 38. Suppose the economy is at long-run equilibrium and a drop in consumer confidence shifts AD left. Explain how monetary policy could offset it, and explain why the Fed might not be able to restore the original equilibrium completely.

Show solution

The Fed can buy bonds, lower i, raise investment, and shift AD back right to its original position. Limits: recognition and policy lags mean AD may already have fallen a lot; if i is already near zero, there is little room to cut; and weak investment demand may blunt the effect.

Problem 39. In Year 1 the Fed buys bonds in a recession. In Year 2 the economy overheats and the Fed sells bonds. Describe for each year the changes in (a) the money supply, (b) the interest rate, (c) bond prices, and (d) AD.

Show solution

Year 1 (expansionary): MS ↑, i ↓, bond prices ↑, AD ↑ (right). Year 2 (contractionary): MS ↓, i ↑, bond prices ↓, AD ↓ (left). The Fed leans against the business cycle in both years.
