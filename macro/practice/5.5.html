Supply-Side Policy & Policy Debates

Read the theory → Drill 5.5 →

A. Demand-Side versus Supply-Side Policies

Problem 1. Classify each as demand-side or supply-side. (a) Congress raises defense spending. (b) The Fed lowers the target interest rate. (c) A tax credit for firms' R&D. (d) Federal funding for vocational training. (e) $600 rebate checks to households.

Show solution

(a) Demand-side (fiscal; raises AD). (b) Demand-side (monetary). (c) Supply-side (technology, raises LRAS). (d) Supply-side (human capital). (e) Demand-side (raises disposable income and consumption).

Problem 2. Classify each. (a) Repeal of regulations that raise costs for new firms. (b) The Fed raises its target rate to cool inflation. (c) Lower marginal income tax rates to encourage work. (d) Larger unemployment benefits. (e) Government builds a high-speed rail line.

Show solution

(a) Supply-side. (b) Demand-side. (c) Supply-side (labor incentives). (d) Demand-side (raises incomes of the unemployed and spending). (e) Supply-side (infrastructure raises productivity), though construction also raises AD in the short run.

Problem 3. Explain why a cut in the corporate income tax can be both demand-side and supply-side.

Show solution

Demand side (short run): firms keep more profit and invest more, which raises AD (and shareholders may spend more). Supply side (long run): more investment raises the capital stock and productivity, which shifts LRAS right. So the policy shifts both AD and AS, though the timing differs.

Problem 4. Which of these changes potential output in the long run? (a) A rise in the money supply. (b) A permanent expansion of public education. (c) A temporary stimulus check. (d) Greater protection of property rights.

Show solution

(b) and (d) raise potential output (human capital, better incentives and institutions). (a) and (c) shift AD only: they are not supply-side policies and do not change LRAS in the long run.

Problem 5. An economy suffers from stagflation (high inflation and high unemployment). Why might supply-side policy be preferred to demand-side policy?

Show solution

Demand-side policy faces a dilemma: expansion cuts unemployment but worsens inflation, while contraction cuts inflation but worsens unemployment. Supply-side policy shifts SRAS right, which lowers the price level and raises output together, easing both problems.

Problem 6. Compare the short-run and long-run effects of (a) $200 billion of transfer payments and (b) $200 billion of spending on infrastructure and training.

Show solution

(a) Raises AD in the short run (multiplier). It does not change LRAS, so in the long run only the price level is higher. (b) Also raises AD in the short run, and in the long run raises productivity and LRAS, so output grows and the price level rises less.

Problem 7. True or false: "Supply-side policies are used to smooth out recessions in the short run."

Show solution

False. Smoothing recessions (stabilization) uses demand-side policies, which can work quickly. Supply-side policies work through investment and incentives, so their effects are slow and are aimed at long-run growth.

B. Graphing the Effects

Problem 8. A national education program raises worker skills. Show the long-run effect on LRAS, real GDP and the price level.

Show solution

Potential output rises: LRAS shifts right. With SRAS shifting right as well, real GDP rises (Yf1 to Yf2) and the price level falls (PL1 to PL2). Unemployment stays at the natural rate or falls.

Problem 9. Deregulation lowers firms' production costs, with no change in potential output in the short run. Show the effect.

Show solution

SRAS shifts right. Real GDP rises, the price level falls, and unemployment falls. On the Phillips curve, the SRPC shifts down.

Problem 10. The government cuts the payroll tax paid by employers. Show the effect on SRAS, output and prices, and describe the Phillips-curve effect.

Show solution

Labor is cheaper, so SRAS shifts right. Output rises and the price level falls. Unemployment falls. The SRPC shifts down: lower inflation at each unemployment rate.

Problem 11. Higher taxes on business investment reduce capital accumulation over the long run. Show the effect on LRAS and the PPC.

Show solution

Slower capital accumulation means potential output is lower than it would have been. Compared with the starting point, LRAS shifts left (or grows more slowly) and the PPC shifts in relative to the baseline.

Problem 12. An investment tax credit encourages firms to buy more equipment. Show the effect on the PPC.

Show solution

More capital goods today raise future capacity. The PPC shifts outward over time (and LRAS shifts right).

Problem 13. An oil shock shifts SRAS left. Compare a demand-side response with a supply-side response.

Show solution

The shock causes stagflation: output falls and the price level rises.

The graph shows the initial impact of the oil shock: SRAS shifts left.

  • Demand-side: AD shifts right. Compared with the post-shock equilibrium, the new equilibrium has higher real GDP and a higher price level (the price level rises further).
  • Supply-side: policies that lower costs (or time) shift SRAS back right. Compared with the post-shock equilibrium, the new equilibrium has higher real GDP and a lower price level.

Without any policy, the economy self-corrects as wages fall.

Problem 14. A tax cut raises household disposable income and also increases work effort. Describe the short-run and long-run effects in AD-AS.

Show solution

Short run: consumption rises, so AD shifts right (output and the price level rise). Long run: if more work effort and savings raise potential output, LRAS shifts right, so output is higher and the price level lower than it would otherwise be.

This is the short-run graph: AD shifts right.

C. The Laffer Curve

Problem 15. Explain why tax revenue is zero at a 0% rate and also at a 100% rate.

Show solution

At 0% nothing is collected on any base. At 100% no one keeps any income, so work and reported income vanish, so the tax base (and revenue) falls to zero. Between the extremes revenue is positive, so the curve is hump-shaped.

Problem 16. Revenue is R = t(100 − t) with t in percent. Find revenue at t = 20, 50 and 80.

Show solution

t = 20: 20 × 80 = 1,600.
t = 50: 50 × 50 = 2,500.
t = 80: 80 × 20 = 1,600.
The same revenue can be raised at a low and a high rate, and the maximum is at t = 50.

Problem 17. With R = t(90 − t), is the revenue higher at t = 70 or at t = 60? What does this say about cutting the tax rate from 70 to 60?

Show solution

t = 70: 70 × 20 = 1,400.
t = 60: 60 × 30 = 1,800.
Cutting the rate from 70 to 60 raises revenue by 400, because 70 is in the prohibitive range (right of the peak at 45).

Problem 18. With R = t(120 − 2t), find revenue at t = 20, 30, 40 and 50, and the revenue-maximizing rate.

Show solution

t = 20: 20 × 80 = 1,600;
t = 30: 30 × 60 = 1,800;
t = 40: 40 × 40 = 1,600;
t = 50: 50 × 20 = 1,000.
The maximum is at t = 30 (revenue 1,800).

Problem 19. The table gives the tax base at several rates. Find revenue at each rate and the revenue-maximizing rate.

Tax rate Tax base
10% 5,000
20% 4,200
30% 3,200
40% 2,100
50% 1,000
Show solution

Revenue: 10% → 500; 20%
→ 840; 30%
→ 960; 40%
→ 840; 50%
→ 500. Revenue is maximized at 30%. Note that 20% and 40% raise the same revenue.

Problem 20. Using the table from Problem 19, the government cuts the rate from 40% to 20%. What happens to revenue and why?

Show solution

Revenue stays at 840 (40% × 2,100 = 20% × 4,200).
The 40% rate is to the right of the peak, so the cut is exactly offset by the larger tax base.
A cut from 30% to 20% would lose revenue (960 to 840).

Problem 21. A politician claims a tax cut "will pay for itself". Under what condition is this true?

Show solution

Only if the economy is on the right side of the Laffer curve (the prohibitive range), where the rate is above the revenue-maximizing rate. On the left side, a cut reduces revenue (even though the base may grow somewhat).

D. Rules and Discretion

Problem 22. Explain time inconsistency using a central bank's inflation policy.

Show solution

The bank announces low inflation. If the public believes it, the bank is tempted to surprise with expansion to cut unemployment. The public anticipates this temptation, so expected inflation stays high; the actual outcome is higher inflation and no lower unemployment. Commitment (rules, independence, an inflation target) makes the low-inflation promise credible.

Problem 23. Give one argument for and one against policy rules.

Show solution

For: rules make policy predictable and credible and prevent the time-inconsistency problem or political manipulation. Against: rules are inflexible: they cannot respond to unusual shocks (a financial crisis or a pandemic), so discretion may stabilize better.

Problem 24. Which of the following are rules? (a) A target of 2% inflation. (b) Congress passing a stimulus bill during a recession. (c) The Fed holding money growth at 3% per year. (d) A constitutional balanced-budget requirement. (e) The Fed deciding meeting by meeting.

Show solution

Rules: (a), (c), (d). Discretion: (b), (e).

Problem 25. Why do economists favor independent central banks?

Show solution

Independence insulates the central bank from pressure to pursue short-term, politically popular expansions before elections (the political business cycle). That makes low-inflation commitments more credible, which reduces the time-inconsistency problem and the cost of disinflation.

E. Rational Expectations and the Lucas Critique

Problem 26. The central bank credibly announces a rise in the money growth rate. Under rational expectations, what happens to real output?

Show solution

Workers and firms raise wages and prices in advance, so SRAS shifts left as AD shifts right. The price level rises but real output is little changed: anticipated policy is ineffective for output. Only unexpected changes affect real output.

Problem 27. What does the Lucas critique say about using an estimated Phillips curve to choose policy?

Show solution

A Phillips curve estimated under one policy regime may shift when the regime changes, because people change their expectations and behavior. Policymakers cannot assume the old trade-off stays stable under a new policy.

F. Exam-Style Multi-Step

Problem 28. The economy is in a recessionary gap. Policy A is an increase in government spending, and Policy B is a cut in business taxes that raises investment and capacity. Compare the short-run and long-run effects on output, the price level and potential output.

Show solution

Policy A: AD shifts right: output rises toward Yf and the price level rises. LRAS is unchanged. Policy B: AD shifts right at first (investment spending) and, over time, LRAS shifts right as the capital stock grows. In the long run potential output is higher and the price level is lower than under A. B's effect arrives slowly and may cost revenue.

Problem 29. A supply-side tax cut is passed when tax rates are on the left side of the Laffer curve. Describe the effect on government revenue, the deficit, and the loanable funds market.

Show solution

On the left side, the cut lowers government revenue (even if the base grows somewhat), so the deficit rises. Government borrowing increases the demand for loanable funds: the real interest rate rises and private investment is crowded out, which partly offsets the supply-side gain in capital.
