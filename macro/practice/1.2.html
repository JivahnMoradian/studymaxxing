Comparative Advantage & Trade

Read the theory → Drill 1.2 →

A. Output Method

(Problems 1–6: output per worker-day.)

Wheat (bushels) Cloth (yards)
Norland 24 8
Sudland 15 12

Problem 1. Who has the absolute advantage in wheat? In cloth?

Show solution

Wheat: 24 > 15 → Norland. Cloth: 12 > 8
→ Sudland. In this problem, each country has an absolute advantage in one good, though it is possible for one country to have an absolute advantage in both goods.

Problem 2. Find each country's opportunity cost of 1 bushel of wheat (in cloth).

Show solution

Output method (other ÷ this good).
Norland: 8/24 = 1/3 yard.
Sudland: 12/15 = 0.8 yard.

Problem 3. Find each country's opportunity cost of 1 yard of cloth (in wheat).

Show solution

Norland: 24/8 = 3 bushels.
Sudland: 15/12 = 1.25 bushels.

Problem 4. Who has the comparative advantage in each good?

Show solution

Wheat: Norland's cost (1/3) < Sudland's (0.8) → Norland. Cloth: Sudland's cost (1.25) < Norland's (3)
→ Sudland.

Problem 5. Give the range of terms of trade (price of 1 bushel of wheat, in cloth) that benefits both.

Show solution

Between the two opportunity costs of wheat: between 1/3 and 0.8 yard of cloth per bushel.

Problem 6. Is a price of 0.9 yard of cloth per bushel of wheat mutually beneficial?

Show solution

No. 0.9 is above both costs. Sudland could make wheat itself for 0.8 yard, so it would refuse to pay 0.9. Only Norland would gain.

B. Input Method

(Problems 7–10: hours needed to make one unit.)

Kayaks (hrs) Canoes (hrs)
Tara 6 4
Wen 10 5

Problem 7. Who has the absolute advantage in each good?

Show solution

Fewer hours wins. Tara needs fewer hours for kayaks (6 < 10) and canoes (4 < 5) → Tara has the absolute advantage in both.

Problem 8. Find each person's opportunity cost of 1 kayak (in canoes).

Show solution

Input method (this good ÷ other).
Tara: 6/4 = 1.5 canoes.
Wen: 10/5 = 2 canoes.

Problem 9. Who has the comparative advantage in each good?

Show solution

Kayaks: Tara's cost (1.5) <
Wen's (2) → Tara.
Canoes: opportunity cost of 1 canoe is Tara 4/6 = 0.667 kayak and Wen 5/10 = 0.5 kayak → Wen.
Tara has an absolute advantage in both, yet trade still helps.

Problem 10. Give the range of terms of trade for one canoe, in kayaks, that benefits both.

Show solution

Between 0.5 (Wen's cost) and 0.667 (Tara's cost): between 0.5 and 2/3 kayak per canoe.

C. Specialization and Gains from Trade

(Problems 11–14: Eastland has 240 labor-hours and Westland has 300. Labor-hours needed per unit:)

Tractors Drones
Eastland 4 2
Westland 10 3

Problem 11. Who has the comparative advantage in tractors? In drones?

Show solution

Opp. cost of a tractor: Eastland 4/2 = 2 drones;
Westland 10/3 ≈ 3.33 drones.
Eastland has the comparative advantage in tractors.
Opp. cost of a drone: Eastland 0.5 tractor;
Westland 0.3 tractor → Westland has it in drones.

Problem 12. If each country specializes fully, how much does each produce?

Show solution

Eastland: 240 ÷ 4 = 60 tractors.
Westland: 300 ÷ 3 = 100 drones.

Problem 13. Suppose Eastland exports 24 tractors and Westland exports 60 drones. What price (drones per tractor) is this, and is it mutually beneficial?

Show solution

Price = 60 ÷ 24 = 2.5 drones per tractor, which lies between 2 and 3.33, so yes.

Problem 14. After this trade, what does each country consume? Without trade, Eastland produced 20 tractors and 80 drones, and Westland produced 15 tractors and 50 drones. Did each gain?

Show solution

Eastland: 60 − 24 = 36 tractors and 60 drones.
Westland: 24 tractors and 100 − 60 = 40 drones.

Check each against its own PPC.
Eastland: 36(4) + 60(2) = 264 hours > 240, so the bundle is outside its PPC.
Westland: 24(10) + 40(3) = 360 > 300, also outside.
Both countries consume bundles that were unattainable in isolation, so both gain.

More Practice — C. Terms of Trade

Problem 25. Alpha's opportunity cost of 1 apple is 0.4 pear and Beta's is 0.8 pear. Is a price of 0.55 pear per apple mutually beneficial? Who gains more?

Show solution

Yes: 0.4 < 0.55 < 0.8.
Alpha (the exporter) gains 0.55 − 0.4 = 0.15 pear per apple;
Beta (the importer) saves 0.8 − 0.55 = 0.25 pear per apple.
Beta gains more.

Problem 26. Di's opportunity cost of 1 desk is 3 chairs and Cy's is 4 chairs. Is a price of 4.5 chairs per desk mutually beneficial?

Show solution

No. 4.5 is above both costs. Cy could make a desk himself for 4 chairs, so he would never pay 4.5. Only Di would gain.

Problem 27. Noel makes laptops at an opportunity cost of 2 phones; Mira at 3 phones. What range of prices (phones per laptop) benefits both, and would a price of 2.9 favor Noel or Mira?

Show solution

The range is between 2 and 3 phones per laptop. At 2.9, Noel (exporter) gains 0.9 phone per laptop and Mira (importer) saves only 0.1, so the price favors Noel.

Problem 28. If the terms of trade equal exactly one country's opportunity cost, what does that country gain?

Show solution

Nothing: it receives exactly what it gives up at home. The other country captures all the gains, so the price is at the edge of the beneficial range.

More Practice — D. Specialization and Gains from Trade (Second Scenario)

Problem 29. Freeland (240 hours) needs 6 hours per laptop and 2 per phone. Gondor (300 hours) needs 5 per laptop and 5 per phone. Who has the comparative advantage in each good?

Show solution

Opp. cost of 1 laptop: Freeland 6/2 = 3 phones;
Gondor 5/5 = 1 phone
→ Gondor has the comparative advantage in laptops.
Opp. cost of 1 phone: Freeland 1/3 laptop;
Gondor 1 laptop → Freeland has it in phones.

Problem 30. If each country specializes fully, how much does each produce?

Show solution

Freeland: 240 ÷ 2 = 120 phones.
Gondor: 300 ÷ 5 = 60 laptops.

Problem 31. Give the range of terms of trade (phones per laptop) that benefits both.

Show solution

Between the opportunity costs of a laptop: between 1 and 3 phones per laptop.

Problem 32. Suppose Gondor sells 20 laptops to Freeland at 2 phones per laptop. What does each country consume? (Without trade: Freeland made 20 laptops and 60 phones; Gondor made 30 laptops and 30 phones.)

Show solution

Gondor receives 40 phones: it consumes 60 − 20 = 40 laptops and 40 phones.
Freeland pays 40 phones: it consumes 20 laptops and 120 − 40 = 80 phones.

Problem 33. Did each country gain? Use the PPFs.

Show solution

Freeland: (20 laptops, 80 phones) needs 20(6) + 80(2) = 280 hours > 240, so it lies outside its PPF; before trade it had 20 laptops and 60 phones (more phones now, same laptops).
Gondor: (40, 40) needs 40(5) + 40(5) = 400 > 300, outside its PPF; before, (30, 30).
Both gain.
