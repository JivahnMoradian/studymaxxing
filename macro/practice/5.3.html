5.3

Deficits, Debt & Crowding Out

Read the theory → Drill 5.3 →

A. Deficit and Surplus Arithmetic

Problem 1. In a year the government collects $3,400 billion in taxes. It spends $2,600 billion on purchases, $700 billion on transfers and $220 billion in interest. Find the budget balance and the primary balance.

Show solution

Total spending = 2,600 + 700 + 220 = 3,520.
Budget balance = 3,400 − 3,520 = −120, a deficit of $120 billion.
The primary balance excludes interest: 3,400 − (2,600 + 700) = +100, a primary surplus of $100 billion.

Problem 2. Tax revenue is 1,450 and total spending is 1,380. What is the balance, and what happens to the debt?

Show solution

1,450 − 1,380 = +70: a surplus of 70.
The national debt falls by 70 (the surplus is used to repay debt).

Problem 3. The debt starts the year at 8,000. This year's deficit is 300 and next year's surplus is 120. Find the debt after each year.

Show solution

After year 1: 8,000 + 300 = 8,300.
After year 2: 8,300 − 120 = 8,180.

Problem 4. The debt is 12,000. The next three deficits are 450, 520 and 380. Find the debt after three years.

Show solution

12,000 + 450 + 520 + 380 = 13,350.

Problem 5. At full employment, tax revenue would be 2,900 and spending 3,000. In a recession, revenue is 2,700. Find the structural, total and cyclical deficits.

Show solution

Structural deficit = 3,000 − 2,900 = 100.
Actual deficit = 3,000 − 2,700 = 300.
Cyclical deficit = 300 − 100 = 200 (due to lost revenue from the recession).

Problem 6. Does the deficit rise, fall or stay the same, other things equal? (a) A recession reduces incomes. (b) Congress cuts income tax rates. (c) The economy expands strongly. (d) Congress raises spending on defense.

Show solution

(a) Rises (revenue down, transfers up). (b) Rises. (c) Falls (revenue up, transfers down). (d) Rises.

Problem 7. True or false: "If the government runs a surplus, the national debt is eliminated."

Show solution

False. A surplus only reduces the debt (by the size of the surplus). The debt is eliminated only if surpluses accumulate to cover the whole stock.

B. Debt-to-GDP and the Burden of Debt

Problem 8. The national debt is 9,000 and nominal GDP is 12,000. Find the debt-to-GDP ratio.

Show solution

9,000 ÷ 12,000 = 0.75, or 75%.

Problem 9. Debt rises from 15,000 to 15,900 while nominal GDP rises from 18,000 to 18,720. Does the debt ratio rise or fall?

Show solution

Before: 15,000 ÷ 18,000 = 83.3%.
After: 15,900 ÷ 18,720 = 84.9%.
The ratio rises, because the debt grew by 6% and GDP only 4%.

Problem 10. The debt is 6,500 and the average interest rate is 3.6%. Find the annual interest cost and its share of tax revenue of 2,600.

Show solution

Interest = 6,500 × 0.036 = 234.
Share of revenue = 234 ÷ 2,600 = 9%.

Problem 11. A deficit of 360 occurs in an economy with GDP of 9,000. Express it as a percent of GDP.

Show solution

360 ÷ 9,000 = 4% of GDP.

Problem 12. The debt is 3,000, GDP is 5,000 and nominal GDP grows 4%. What deficit would keep the debt ratio constant?

Show solution

For the ratio to stay at 60%, debt must also grow 4%: 3,000 × 0.04 = 120.
That is 120 ÷ 5,000 = 2.4% of current GDP.
A deficit smaller than this lowers the ratio; a larger deficit raises it.

Problem 13. The debt is 8,000 and the government must refinance it all at a higher rate: 2% rises to 4%. Find the change in annual interest costs, and say how it affects the budget.

Show solution

Before: 8,000 × 0.02 = 160.
After: 8,000 × 0.04 = 320.
The interest bill doubles (+160), which raises the deficit unless taxes rise or other spending is cut.

Problem 14. Debt starts at 10,000 and GDP at 14,000. Each year the deficit is 400 and nominal GDP grows 3%. Find the debt ratio after three years.

Show solution

Start: 71.4%. Year 1: debt 10,400, GDP 14,420, ratio 72.1%. Year 2: 10,800 and 14,852.6, 72.7%. Year 3: debt 11,200, GDP 15,298.2, ratio 73.2%. The ratio creeps up because the debt grows faster than nominal GDP.

C. Crowding Out in the Loanable Funds Market

Problem 15. The government borrows heavily to finance a deficit. Show the effect on the real interest rate and on private investment.

Show solution

The demand for loanable funds shifts right. The real interest rate rises (r1 to r2). Private investment, which is interest sensitive, falls: crowding out.

Problem 16. The government runs a budget surplus and uses it to retire debt. What happens in the loanable funds market?

Show solution

Government borrowing falls, so demand for loanable funds shifts left. The real interest rate falls and private investment rises (the reverse of crowding out).

Problem 17. Congress cuts taxes, financed by borrowing. Explain the effect on the real interest rate and investment.

Show solution

The tax cut raises the deficit, so government borrowing rises and demand for loanable funds shifts right. The real interest rate rises and private investment falls (crowding out). Part of the stimulus to AD is offset.

Problem 18. Show how crowding out affects the AD shift from expansionary fiscal policy.

Show solution

Higher government spending shifts AD right. Crowding out lowers investment, which offsets part of the increase in AD. The net AD shift is smaller than the multiplier alone would predict, and output and the price level rise by less.

In the graph, AD2 is where AD would move with no crowding out (the full multiplier effect), and AD3 is the smaller actual shift. Equilibrium moves from E1 to E3, not to E2.

Problem 19. What are the long-run effects of persistent crowding out on growth?

Show solution

Lower investment means a slower accumulation of physical capital, so the LRAS (and PPC) shifts right more slowly. Living standards grow more slowly than they would have without crowding out.

In the graph, LRAS2 is where LRAS would be after normal growth (no crowding out), and LRAS3 is the smaller actual shift: full-employment output rises from Y1 only to Y3, not to Y2.

Problem 20. For each event, say what happens to the real interest rate and to private investment. (a) Government borrows to fund a deficit. (b) Households save more. (c) Foreign savers lend more to domestic borrowers. (d) Firms become more optimistic and want to invest more.

Show solution

(a) r up, I down (demand right; crowding out). (b) Supply of loanable funds shifts right: r down, I up. (c) Supply shifts right: r down, I up. (d) Demand shifts right: r up and I up (investment rises, because the shift itself is an increase in investment demand, though the higher r damps the rise).

Problem 21. Private saving falls (households save less). Describe the effect on r and investment.

Show solution

Supply of loanable funds shifts left. The real rate rises and investment falls, similar to a deficit, since national saving falls.

D. Numerical Loanable Funds

(Problems 22–25: the demand for loanable funds by private borrowers is LFd = 400 − 40r and supply is LFs = 40 + 20r, with r in percent.)

Problem 22. Find the equilibrium real interest rate and quantity of loanable funds.

Show solution
400 - 40r = 40 + 20r ⟹  360 = 60r ⟹  r = 6%
LF = 40 + 20(6) = 160

Problem 23. The government now borrows 60 to finance a deficit. Find the new equilibrium rate and total funds lent.

Show solution

Total demand = (400 − 40r) + 60 = 460 − 40r.

460 - 40r = 40 + 20r ⟹  420 = 60r ⟹  r = 7%
LF = 40 + 20(7) = 180

Problem 24. Find private investment before and after the deficit, and the amount of crowding out.

Show solution

Before: private investment = 160.
After: 400 − 40(7) = 120.
Private investment falls by 40: that is the crowding out.
Of the 60 the government borrowed, 40 came from private investment and 20 came from the extra saving (supply rose from 160 to 180 as r rose).

Problem 25. Instead, the government runs a surplus of 30 (net lending of 30, reducing demand). Find the new r and private investment.

Show solution

Demand = 400 − 40r − 30 = 370 − 40r.

370 - 40r = 40 + 20r ⟹  330 = 60r ⟹  r = 5.5%

Private investment = 400 − 40(5.5) = 180, up 20 from 160 (the reverse of crowding out).
Total funds are 150, of which the government repays 30.

(Problems 26–27: private demand is LFd = 300 − 20r and supply is LFs = 50 + 30r.)

Problem 26. Find the initial equilibrium. Then the government borrows 45. Find the new rate.

Show solution

Initial: 300 − 20r = 50 + 30r, so 250 = 50r and r = 5%, LF = 200.
With borrowing of 45: 345 − 20r = 50 + 30r, so 295 = 50r and r = 5.9%, LF = 50 + 30(5.9) = 227.

Problem 27. In Problem 26, find the amount of private investment crowded out, and compare with the amount the government borrowed.

Show solution

Private investment = 300 − 20(5.9) = 182, down from 200.
Crowding out = 18, less than the 45 borrowed (the other 27 comes from higher saving).

Problem 28. Would the crowding out in Problem 24 be larger or smaller if private investment were more sensitive to the interest rate? Explain.

Show solution

Larger. If investment demand is very responsive to r, a given rise in the rate cuts private investment by more. If saving is also very responsive to r, more of the borrowing is funded by additional saving, so crowding out is smaller.

E. Debt, Financing and the Burden

Problem 29. Name two ways of financing a deficit and one consequence of each.

Show solution

(1) Borrowing from the public (selling bonds): raises the real interest rate and crowds out private investment. (2) Monetizing the debt (the Fed buys bonds with new money): increases the money supply and risks inflation.

Problem 30. Is the burden of debt greater when it is held by foreigners or by domestic residents? Explain.

Show solution

Debt owed to foreigners is a larger burden: interest and principal are paid to foreigners, a claim on domestic output that leaves the country. When held domestically, interest payments go to other residents (though taxes still redistribute from taxpayers to bondholders).

Problem 31. Why does interest on the national debt matter for the budget?

Show solution

Interest is a mandatory spending item. A larger debt, or a higher interest rate, means more of every tax dollar goes to interest, leaving less for other uses (education, defense, tax cuts) or requiring a larger deficit.

Problem 32. A recession raises the deficit. Is that a problem, and what policy rule would economists propose?

Show solution

A cyclical deficit is a normal, desirable result of automatic stabilizers, which cushion demand. Economists focus on the structural deficit and propose balancing the budget over the business cycle, not every year.

Problem 33. (College) What does Ricardian equivalence predict about a deficit-financed tax cut, and what happens to crowding out?

Show solution

Households expect future tax increases and save the tax cut: private saving rises by as much as public saving falls. National saving is unchanged, so the real interest rate does not change and there is no crowding out. In practice the equivalence is only partial.

F. Multi-Step, Exam-Style

Problem 34. The economy is in a recessionary gap, and the government increases spending, financing it by selling bonds. (a) Show the effect on the loanable funds market. (b) What happens to private investment? (c) What is the effect on AD? (d) What is the long-run concern?

Show solution

(a) Demand for loanable funds shifts right and the real interest rate rises. (b) Private investment falls (crowding out), though the economy is below potential so the effect may be small. (c) AD shifts right by less than the full multiplier effect because of crowding out, but still closes part of the gap. (d) Lower investment means slower capital accumulation, so long-run growth is lower, and the debt increases.

Problem 35. Compare two policies to close an inflationary gap: (a) cut government spending, (b) raise taxes. What are the effects of each on r and investment?

Show solution

Both policies reduce the deficit (or create a surplus), which lowers government borrowing: demand for loanable funds shifts left, r falls and investment rises (the reverse of crowding out). AD shifts left, but part of the contraction is offset by higher investment, so the net AD shift is smaller than the cut alone would imply. The inflationary gap narrows but does not fully close (output and the price level fall, though output stays a little above the LRAS level), so a larger policy change may be needed.
