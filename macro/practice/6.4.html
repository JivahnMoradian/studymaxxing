6.4

Policy in an Open Economy

Read the theory → Drill 6.4 →

Assume flexible exchange rates, perfect mobility of financial capital, and no change in other variables unless stated.

A. Monetary Policy Chains

Problem 1. The economy is in a recessionary gap and the Fed buys bonds. Trace the full chain of effects through the money market, the dollar market, NX and AD-AS.

Show solution

Money supply rises, so the nominal (and real) interest rate falls. Lower r raises investment. Lower US rates also cause capital outflow: the demand for dollars falls and the supply of dollars rises, so the dollar depreciates. The weaker dollar raises exports and lowers imports: NX rises. AD shifts right (I and NX both rise): real GDP rises, the price level rises, unemployment falls.

Problem 2. The economy is in an inflationary gap and the Fed sells bonds. Trace the chain.

Show solution

MS falls, so the interest rate rises; investment falls. Higher US rates attract capital: the demand for dollars rises, the supply falls, and the dollar appreciates. NX falls. AD shifts left, restoring output to Yf and lowering the price level.

Problem 3. The European Central Bank cuts interest rates sharply. What happens to the US dollar, US NX and US AD?

Show solution

European assets become less attractive: capital flows to the US, so the demand for dollars rises and the supply of dollars falls. The dollar appreciates, US NX falls, and US AD shifts left (real GDP and the price level fall).

Problem 4. Explain why expansionary monetary policy has a larger effect on AD in an open economy than in a closed one.

Show solution

In a closed economy the only channel is r ↓
→ I ↑. In an open economy lower r also causes capital outflow, a depreciation, and NX ↑. The two components of AD (I and NX) both rise, so the AD shift is larger.

Problem 5. For each event, state the direction of r, capital flows, the dollar and NX: (a) the Fed sells bonds; (b) the Fed lowers the discount rate; (c) the Fed lowers the reserve requirement; (d) the Bank of Japan eases policy sharply.

Show solution

(a) r ↑, inflow, appreciates, NX ↓. (b) Cheaper borrowing for banks raises MS: r ↓, outflow, depreciates, NX ↑. (c) MS ↑: r ↓, outflow, depreciates, NX ↑. (d) Japanese rates fall relative to US rates: capital flows to the US, the dollar appreciates (the yen depreciates), US NX ↓.

Problem 6. The Fed's expansionary policy raises investment by 25 and net exports by 15. The MPC is 0.8. By how much does AD shift?

Show solution

Multiplier = 1 / (1 − 0.8) = 5.
Initial change in spending = 25 + 15 = 40.
AD shifts right by 40 × 5 = 200.

Problem 7. Contractionary policy cuts investment by 12 and NX by 8. With MPC = 0.75, find the AD shift.

Show solution

Multiplier = 4.
Change in spending = −12 − 8 = −20.
AD shifts left by 20 × 4 = 80.

Problem 8. Suppose the Fed cuts interest rates and the economy is at full employment. Describe the short-run and long-run results.

Show solution

Short run: AD shifts right, so output exceeds Yf (an inflationary gap) and the price level rises. Long run: wages rise, SRAS shifts left, output returns to Yf at a higher price level. The dollar's depreciation and higher NX in the short run reverse as prices rise (money is neutral in the long run).

B. Fiscal Policy Chains

Problem 9. At full employment, the government increases spending financed by borrowing. Trace the effects through the loanable funds market, the dollar market and NX.

Show solution

The demand for loanable funds rises, so the real interest rate rises, and private investment is crowded out. Higher r attracts capital inflow: the demand for dollars rises, the supply falls, and the dollar appreciates; NX falls. AD still shifts right overall, but by less than the closed-economy multiplier suggests.

Problem 10. In a recession, the government cuts taxes. Describe the effects on r, the dollar, NX and AD.

Show solution

A larger deficit raises the demand for loanable funds: r rises; capital flows in; the dollar appreciates; NX falls. AD shifts right (consumption rises) but by less than in a closed economy; the recessionary gap narrows.

Problem 11. The economy is in an inflationary gap and the government cuts spending (a smaller deficit). Describe the effects.

Show solution

The demand for loanable funds falls: r falls; capital flows out; the dollar depreciates; NX rises. AD shifts left (G falls) but by less than in a closed economy, because higher NX and investment partly offset the cut.

Problem 12. The government raises G by 40 and MPC = 0.75. Investment falls by 10 and NX falls by 6 in response. Find the initial and the net shift in AD.

Show solution

Multiplier = 4.
Initial shift = 40 × 4 = 160.
Induced change in spending = −10 − 6 = −16, so −16 × 4 = −64.
Net shift = 160 − 64 = 96.

Problem 13. Explain the "twin deficits" idea using the loanable funds model.

Show solution

A budget deficit lowers public saving and national saving.
Holding I fixed, NCO = S − I falls, so the dollar appreciates and NX falls: the trade deficit widens along with the budget deficit.

Problem 14. A major trading partner launches a large fiscal stimulus, raising its income. What happens to US exports, NX and AD, holding the exchange rate fixed?

Show solution

Foreign income rises, so foreigners import more from the US: exports rise, US NX rises, and US AD shifts right. (If the foreign stimulus also raises foreign interest rates, the dollar would depreciate, which would further raise NX.)

C. Direction Tables and Policy Mixes

Problem 15. A country combines a large budget deficit (expansionary fiscal) with tight monetary policy (high interest rates). Describe the effects on r, the exchange rate and NX.

Show solution

Both policies push r up, through different channels: tight monetary policy cuts the money supply and raises the nominal interest rate in the money market, while the deficit adds to government borrowing and raises demand for loanable funds. Capital floods in, the currency appreciates sharply, and NX falls a lot (a large trade deficit). This resembles the US in the early 1980s.

Problem 16. Policy mix: expansionary monetary policy plus contractionary fiscal policy. Describe the effects on r, the exchange rate and NX.

Show solution

Monetary expansion lowers r; fiscal contraction also lowers r. Capital flows out, the currency depreciates, and NX rises. For AD, the two policies pull in opposite directions: monetary expansion raises AD (through investment and NX), while fiscal contraction lowers AD directly (lower G or higher T). The net AD shift is ambiguous, but the mix shifts the economy toward investment and NX and away from government spending and consumption.

Problem 17. True or false: "Under flexible exchange rates, a depreciation caused by expansionary monetary policy always makes the country better off."

Show solution

False (not always). The depreciation raises NX and AD, which helps in a recession, but it raises import prices and the price level, and trading partners lose competitiveness and may retaliate.

Problem 18. Fill in the direction of r, the dollar, NX for: (a) expansionary fiscal; (b) contractionary fiscal; (c) expansionary monetary; (d) contractionary monetary.

Show solution

(a) r ↑, dollar ↑ (appreciates), NX ↓. (b) r ↓, dollar ↓ (depreciates), NX ↑. (c) r ↓, dollar ↓, NX ↑. (d) r ↑, dollar ↑, NX ↓. (The chain in every case: r changes, so foreign demand for US assets changes, so the dollar moves the same way as r, so NX moves the opposite way.)

Problem 19. Fiscal and monetary stimulus are both used in a deep recession. Which has the reinforcing exchange-rate effect, and which has the offsetting one?

Show solution

Monetary stimulus has a reinforcing exchange-rate effect (depreciation raises NX). Fiscal stimulus has an offsetting effect (higher r appreciates the currency and lowers NX). The offset only dampens fiscal stimulus: in the standard AP analysis the net effect on AD is still positive, though in the extreme case of perfectly mobile capital the appreciation can offset it completely.

D. Trade Policy

Problem 20. The US imposes a tariff on imported steel. Show the effect in the dollar market and describe the effect on the exchange rate, imports, exports and net exports.

Show solution

Americans buy fewer foreign goods, so they supply fewer dollars: supply of dollars shifts left. The dollar appreciates. Imports of steel fall, but the stronger dollar makes US exports more expensive, so exports fall. Imports fall by more than exports, so net exports rise and AD shifts right in the short run; the appreciation partly offsets the tariff's effect. (For the long-run result, open the blue button in the lesson.)

Note: in this AP-style dollar market, imports are what supply dollars, so the tariff shifts supply left. The three-panel model in Chapter 6.3 shows the same tariff as a rightward shift of the NX (demand-for-dollars) curve. Either way the dollar appreciates.

Problem 21. Trading partners retaliate with tariffs on US exports. Show the effect on the dollar.

Show solution

Foreigners buy fewer US goods: demand for dollars shifts left; the dollar depreciates and US exports fall.

Problem 22. Both the US and its partners impose tariffs. What happens to the quantity of dollars traded and to the exchange rate?

Show solution

Both demand and supply of dollars shift left. The quantity of dollars traded falls for sure (trade shrinks); the effect on the exchange rate is ambiguous, depending on which shift is larger.

Problem 23. The US government introduces an export subsidy. Show and explain the effect on the dollar.

Show solution

Foreign demand for US goods rises: demand for dollars shifts right. The dollar appreciates, and imports rise, partly offsetting the export gain.

Problem 24. A free trade agreement removes tariffs on both sides. What happens to the demand and supply of dollars, the quantity traded and the exchange rate?

Show solution

Exports and imports both rise: demand and supply of dollars shift right. The quantity of dollars traded rises; the exchange rate effect is ambiguous.

Problem 25. Name two ways an import tariff helps domestic producers or the economy, and two ways it hurts domestic consumers or the economy.

Show solution

Helps: higher domestic price and output for protected producers; more jobs in the protected industry; government revenue. Hurts: higher prices for consumers; deadweight loss; retaliation against exporters; a stronger dollar that hurts other exporters.

Problem 26. An import quota is imposed. How is its effect on the exchange rate similar to a tariff, and how does it differ in who captures the gap between domestic and world prices?

Show solution

Like a tariff, a quota reduces imports, so the supply of dollars shifts left and the dollar appreciates. A tariff sends the price gap to the government as revenue; a quota sends it to the license holders (importers or foreign exporters) as rents.

E. Tariff and Quota Calculations

Problem 27. Domestic demand is Qd = 120 − 4P and domestic supply is Qs = −20 + 6P. The world price is 10. (a) Find imports under free trade. (b) A tariff of 2 is imposed: find the domestic price, Qd, Qs, imports, tariff revenue and deadweight loss.

Show solution

(a) Qd = 120 − 40 = 80, Qs = −20 + 60 = 40, imports = 40.
(b) P = 12;
Qd = 120 − 48 = 72;
Qs = −20 + 72 = 52;
imports = 20.
Revenue = 2 × 20 = 40.
DWL = ½(2)(52 − 40) + ½(2)(80 − 72) = 12 + 8 = 20.

Problem 28. Qd = 200 − 5P, Qs = 3P, world price 20. (a) Find free-trade imports. (b) What happens with a tariff of 5? Find revenue and DWL.

Show solution

(a) Qd = 100, Qs = 60, imports = 40.
(b) At P = 25, Qd = 200 − 125 = 75 and Qs = 75: imports are 0.
A tariff this large is prohibitive (25 is the no-trade price).
Revenue = 0.
DWL = ½(5)(75 − 60) + ½(5)(100 − 75) = 37.5 + 62.5 = 100.

Problem 29. Qd = 90 − 2P, Qs = −30 + 4P, world price 15, tariff 4. Find the changes in producer surplus and consumer surplus, tariff revenue, and the deadweight loss.

Show solution

Free trade: Qd = 60, Qs = 30 (imports 30).
With tariff: P = 19, Qd = 52, Qs = 46 (imports 6).
ΔPS = 4 × 30 + ½(4)(16) = 120 + 32 = +152.
ΔCS = −[4 × 52 + ½(4)(8)] = −(208 + 16) = −224.
Revenue = 4 × 6 = 24.
Net = 152 − 224 + 24 = −48, so DWL = 48 (check: ½(4)(16) + ½(4)(8) = 48).

Problem 30. Qd = 150 − 3P, Qs = −10 + 5P, world price 12. A 25% tariff is imposed. Find imports before and after, revenue and DWL.

Show solution

Tariff t = 0.25 × 12 = 3, so P = 15.
Before: Qd = 114, Qs = 50, imports 64.
After: Qd = 105, Qs = 65, imports 40.
Revenue = 3 × 40 = 120.
DWL = ½(3)(15) + ½(3)(9) = 22.5 + 13.5 = 36.

Problem 31. Using Problem 27's market, a quota limits imports to 20 units. Find the domestic price and compare with the tariff of 2. Who gets the revenue?

Show solution

Set Qd − Qs = 20: 140 − 10P = 20, so P = 12, the same as with the tariff of 2 (Qd = 72, Qs = 52).
The gap (12 − 10) × 20 = 40 goes to license holders rather than to the government.

Problem 32. With the same market (Problem 27), a stricter quota limits imports to 10. Find the domestic price, Qd, Qs and the license rents.

Show solution

140 − 10P = 10, so P = 13.
Qd = 120 − 52 = 68 and Qs = −20 + 78 = 58.
License rents = (13 − 10) × 10 = 30.

F. Exam-Style Problems

Problem 33. The economy is in a recession. (a) Describe the effects of expansionary monetary policy on the interest rate, the dollar and net exports. (b) Show the effects on AD-AS. (c) Explain why the effect on AD might be larger than the closed-economy effect.

Show solution

(a) MS rises, the interest rate falls, capital flows out, the dollar depreciates, NX rises. (b) AD shifts right: output and the price level rise; unemployment falls. (c) Both I and NX rise, so AD increases by more than investment alone would cause.

Problem 34. Public saving falls by 60 while private saving rises by 20 (partial offset). Investment falls by 15 because the real interest rate rises. MPC = 0.8. (a) Find the change in NCO and NX. (b) Find the AD shift from the change in NX alone. (c) State what happens to the dollar.

Show solution

(a) ΔS = −60 + 20 = −40.
ΔNCO = ΔS − ΔI = −40 − (−15) = −25, so ΔNX = −25.
(b) Multiplier = 5, so AD shifts left by 25 × 5 = 125.
(c) The supply of dollars (NCO) falls, so the dollar appreciates.

Problem 35. The US imposes a 20% tariff on all imports. (a) Show the effect on the dollar market. (b) Describe the effect on exports, imports and NX in the short run and the long run. (c) Evaluate the claim that the tariff will raise AD.

Show solution

(a) Americans demand fewer imports, so the supply of dollars shifts left and the dollar appreciates. (b) Short run: imports fall directly, and the appreciation makes exports more expensive, but exports fall by less than imports, so NX rises. Long run: the appreciation continues until exports fall as much as imports, so NX returns to S − I (see the blue button in the lesson). (c) The claim is true in the short run: higher NX shifts AD right, raising output and the price level. The effect is partly offset by the appreciation and by any retaliation, and firms that use imported inputs face higher costs (SRAS shifts left).
