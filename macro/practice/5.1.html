5.1

The Phillips Curve

Read the theory → Drill 5.1 →

A. Reading Inflation-Unemployment Pairs

(Problems 1–5: the natural rate of unemployment is 5%. An economy's SRPC passes through these points, given as (unemployment %, inflation %).)

Point Unemployment (%) Inflation (%)
A 3.5 7
B 4 5
C 5 3
D 6.5 2
E 8 1.5

Problem 1. Which point is the long-run equilibrium? What is expected inflation?

Show solution

Long-run equilibrium is where unemployment equals the natural rate (5%): point C. There actual inflation equals expected inflation, so expected inflation is 3%.

Problem 2. For points A and E, find cyclical unemployment and say which kind of gap the economy has.

Show solution

Cyclical unemployment = u − un.

  • A: 3.5 − 5 = −1.5 points. Unemployment is below the natural rate: an inflationary gap (output above potential).
  • E: 8 − 5 = +3 points. Unemployment is above the natural rate: a recessionary gap (output below potential).

Problem 3. Starting at B, a contractionary policy moves the economy to D. What happens to inflation and unemployment? Is this a movement along or a shift of the SRPC?

Show solution

Inflation falls from 5% to 2% (down 3 points) and unemployment rises from 4% to 6.5% (up 2.5 points). Because both points are on the same SRPC and the cause is a change in aggregate demand, this is a movement along the SRPC, down and to the right.

Problem 4. At point B, is real GDP above or below potential output? Was aggregate demand more likely rising or falling to get there from C?

Show solution

At B unemployment (4%) is below the natural rate (5%), so real GDP is above potential. Moving from C to B means lower unemployment and higher inflation, which is what an increase in aggregate demand produces.

Problem 5. Can the economy stay at point A year after year? Explain.

Show solution

No. At A inflation (7%) is well above the 3% people expected. Workers and firms revise expectations upward, wages and prices rise faster, and the SRPC shifts up. Unemployment returns to 5%, but at a higher inflation rate. The low unemployment at A is temporary.

B. Movement Along versus Shift of the Phillips Curves

Problem 6. Classify each event as a movement along the SRPC or a shift of the SRPC, and give the direction. (a) Congress cuts income taxes and consumption rises. (b) The Fed raises interest rates and investment falls. (c) A drought raises food and input costs. (d) Households and firms come to expect higher inflation. (e) World oil prices fall sharply. (f) A foreign recession cuts demand for exports.

Show solution

(a) AD rises: movement up and left along the SRPC. (b) AD falls: movement down and right. (c) Adverse supply shock: SRPC shifts up. (d) Higher expected inflation: SRPC shifts up. (e) Favorable supply shock: SRPC shifts down. (f) Net exports fall, so AD falls: movement down and right.

Problem 7. A student writes: "The government raised spending, so the SRPC shifted to the right." Find and fix the error.

Show solution

Higher government spending is a demand shock, and demand shocks cause a movement along the SRPC, not a shift. Correct statement: "Spending raised AD, so the economy moved up and to the left along the SRPC: unemployment fell and inflation rose."

Problem 8. For each change, say whether the LRPC shifts right, shifts left, or does not shift. (a) Unemployment benefits are extended and job search lengthens. (b) A new job-training program improves matching of workers to jobs. (c) The Fed cuts interest rates. (d) A technology shift leaves many workers with obsolete skills. (e) An online platform makes job search faster. (f) Expected inflation rises.

Show solution

(a) Natural rate up: right. (b) Natural rate down: left. (c) AD change only: no shift (movement along the SRPC). (d) More structural unemployment: right. (e) Less frictional unemployment: left. (f) Expectations shift the SRPC, not the LRPC: no shift.

Problem 9. The natural rate rises from 5% to 6.5% (for example, because of a skills mismatch). Describe what happens to the LRPC and the SRPC.

Show solution

The LRPC shifts right to 6.5%. The SRPC passes through the new long-run equilibrium (un, expected inflation), so it is usually drawn shifting right as well. For the same inflation, the economy now has a higher unemployment rate, which is a costly change.

Problem 10. An economy that begins in long-run equilibrium (u = 5, π = 3) records these years. For each change, say which kind of shock is most likely. Year 2: u = 4.2, π = 4.6. Year 3: u = 6.5, π = 6. Year 4: u = 8, π = 2.

Show solution
  • Year 1 to 2 (u down, π up): a demand increase, a movement up and left along the SRPC.
  • Year 2 to 3 (both u and π up): an adverse supply shock (or higher expected inflation): the SRPC shifts up. This is stagflation.
  • Year 3 to 4 (u up, π down): a demand decrease (e.g. contractionary policy), a movement down and right along the new SRPC.

C. From AD-AS to the Phillips Curve

(Each economy starts in long-run equilibrium unless stated. Give the short-run effect on real GDP, the price level, unemployment and inflation, and the effect on the Phillips curve.)

Problem 11. The government increases spending on infrastructure.

Show solution

AD shifts right. Real GDP rises above Yf, the price level rises (inflation), and unemployment falls below un. On the Phillips curve: movement up and left along the SRPC to point B.

Problem 12. Consumer confidence collapses and households cut spending.

Show solution

AD shifts left. Real GDP falls, the price level falls (or inflation slows), and unemployment rises above un. On the Phillips curve: movement down and right along the SRPC to point C.

Problem 13. An oil-exporting region cuts output, and energy prices jump.

Show solution

SRAS shifts left. Real GDP falls, the price level rises, and unemployment rises: stagflation. The SRPC shifts up (and to the right), so both inflation and unemployment are higher at point B.

Problem 14. A surge in labor productivity lowers firms' unit costs.

Show solution

SRAS shifts right. Real GDP rises, the price level falls, and unemployment falls. The SRPC shifts down: lower inflation and lower unemployment at the same time (point B).

Problem 15. The economy is in a recessionary gap (unemployment above un) and the government does nothing. What happens over time?

Show solution

Slack in the labor market pushes nominal wages down. SRAS shifts right until real GDP returns to Yf at a lower price level. In Phillips-curve terms, inflation runs below expectations, expectations fall, and the SRPC shifts down. The economy ends back at un with lower inflation (point C).

Problem 16. In the same recessionary gap, Congress cuts taxes by just enough to restore full employment. What happens, and how does it differ from Problem 15?

Show solution

AD shifts right until it intersects SRAS at Yf. Real GDP rises to potential, the price level rises, and unemployment falls to un. On the Phillips curve: a movement up and left along the SRPC back to long-run equilibrium. Unlike Problem 15, the SRPC does not shift and there is no deflationary adjustment, but the price level ends higher than under self-correction.

Problem 17. The economy is in an inflationary gap (unemployment below un) with no policy response. What happens in the long run?

Show solution

Tight labor markets push nominal wages up. SRAS shifts left until real GDP returns to Yf, at a higher price level. On the Phillips curve: inflation exceeded expectations, expectations rise, and the SRPC shifts up. The economy returns to un with higher inflation.

D. Long-Run Effects and Expectations

Problem 18. True or false: "By tolerating higher inflation, policymakers can permanently keep unemployment below the natural rate."

Show solution

False. Above-expected inflation only lowers unemployment temporarily. Once people expect the higher inflation, the SRPC shifts up and unemployment returns to un. Keeping unemployment below un requires ever-rising inflation. The LRPC is vertical: there is no long-run trade-off.

Problem 19. A government uses repeated stimulus to hold unemployment below the natural rate for several years. Describe the path of the SRPC and of inflation.

Show solution

Each year inflation exceeds what people expected, so expected inflation is revised up and the SRPC shifts up again and again. To keep u below un, policy must keep raising AD, so inflation accelerates. If policy stops, unemployment returns to un but inflation stays high.

Problem 20. A central bank announces a credible plan to cut inflation from 6% to 2%, and households and firms believe it. Explain the effect on the SRPC and why credibility matters.

Show solution

Expected inflation falls from 6% to 2%, so the SRPC shifts down. If the announcement is credible, wages and prices adjust downward quickly, so inflation falls without a deep, long recession. If the public does not believe the plan, expected inflation stays high and the only way down is a movement along the old SRPC to higher unemployment.

Problem 21. In the 1970s many economies had high unemployment and high inflation together. Use the Phillips curve to explain, and describe the policy dilemma.

Show solution

Oil-price shocks shifted SRAS left, and rising expected inflation also pushed the SRPC up. Both unemployment and inflation increased (stagflation). The dilemma: expansionary policy lowers unemployment but moves the economy up the new SRPC to even higher inflation; contractionary policy reduces inflation but raises unemployment further.

Problem 22. The economy starts at long-run equilibrium, and aggregate demand then rises permanently. Describe the short-run and long-run effects on real GDP, the price level, unemployment and inflation.

Show solution

Short run: real GDP rises above Yf, the price level rises, unemployment falls below un, inflation rises (movement along the SRPC). Long run: nominal wages rise, SRAS shifts left, real GDP returns to Yf and unemployment returns to un, but the price level is permanently higher and the SRPC has shifted up (higher inflation expected).

Problem 23. In the late 1990s the US had falling unemployment and low inflation together. Which shock explains this, and what happened to the SRPC?

Show solution

A favorable supply shock (rapid productivity growth from information technology, plus lower import prices) shifted SRAS right. The SRPC shifted down, allowing lower unemployment without higher inflation. Demand-driven expansions cannot do this, since they raise inflation as unemployment falls.

E. Numerical Phillips Curve (College Level)

(Problems 24–30 use the expectations-augmented Phillips curve π = πe − β(u − un), where πe is expected inflation and all rates are in percent.)

Problem 24. Expected inflation is 3%, β = 0.5 and the natural rate is 5%. Find inflation when u = 4%.

Show solution
π = 3 - 0.5(4 - 5) = 3 + 0.5 = 3.5%

Unemployment is 1 point below un, so inflation is 0.5 points above expectations.

Problem 25. Expected inflation is 4%, β = 0.8 and un = 6%. What unemployment rate is needed to bring inflation down to 2%?

Show solution
2 = 4 - 0.8(u - 6) ⟹  u - 6 = 4 - 20.8 = 2.5 ⟹  u = 8.5%

Unemployment must be 2.5 points above the natural rate.

Problem 26. Expectations are adaptive (πe equals last year's inflation). With β = 0.25 and un = 6%, policymakers hold u at 3%. If inflation was 1.5% last year, find inflation in each of the next three years.

Show solution

Each year inflation rises by β(un − u) = 0.25 × 3 = 0.75 points.

  • Year 1: 1.5 + 0.75 = 2.25%
  • Year 2: 3.0%
  • Year 3: 3.75%

Inflation keeps accelerating while u stays below un.

Problem 27. With adaptive expectations, β = 0.5 and un = 5%, inflation is currently 8%. The central bank wants 4% in two years, cutting inflation equally in each year. What unemployment rate must it hold in each year?

Show solution

Inflation must fall by 2 points per year: 2 = 0.5(u − 5), so u − 5 = 4 and u = 9% in each of the two years (4 points of cyclical unemployment).

Problem 28. Use Problem 27's plan and Okun's law (1 point of cyclical unemployment = 2% of output) to find the sacrifice ratio.

Show solution

Output loss each year = 4 points × 2 = 8% of GDP.
Over two years: 16% of one year's GDP.
Inflation falls by 4 points.

Sacrifice ratio = 164 = 4

Each point of disinflation costs about 4% of a year's GDP.

Problem 29. Expected inflation is 2%, β = 0.6 and un rises from 5% to 6.5%. Find inflation at u = 5% before and after the change.

Show solution

Before: π = 2 − 0.6(5 − 5) = 2%.
After: π = 2 − 0.6(5 − 6.5) = 2 + 0.9 = 2.9%.
With a higher natural rate, 5% unemployment is now an inflationary gap.

Problem 30. With expected inflation fixed at 4%, an economy has inflation of 5% at u = 4% and 3% at u = 6%. Find β and un.

Show solution

Subtract the two equations 5 = 4 − β(4 − un) and 3 = 4 − β(6 − un): 2 = 2β, so β = 1.
Then 5 = 4 − (4 − un), so un = 5%.
(Check: at u = 6, π = 4 − 1(1) = 3.)
