Unemployment

A. Computing Labor-Market Rates

Problem 1. An economy has 150 million employed and 10 million unemployed workers. Find the labor force and the unemployment rate.

Show solution

Labor force = 150 + 10 = 160 million.

Unemployment rate = 10160 × 100 = 6.25%

Problem 2. In Problem 1 the adult population is 240 million. Find the labor force participation rate and the employment-population ratio.

Show solution
Participation Rate = 160240 × 100 = 66.7%
Employment-population ratio = 150240 × 100 = 62.5%

Problem 3. The labor force is 25 million and the unemployment rate is 8%. How many people are employed?

Show solution

Unemployed = 0.08 × 25 = 2.0 million, so employed = 25 − 2.0 = 23 million.

Problem 4. The adult population is 250 and 90 adults are not in the labor force. Find the labor force and the participation rate.

Show solution

Labor force = 250 − 90 = 160.
LFPR = 160 ÷ 250 = 64%.

Problem 5. The adult population is 200 million, the participation rate is 64% and the unemployment rate is 5%. Find the number employed and the number unemployed.

Show solution

Labor force = 0.64 × 200 = 128 million.
Unemployed = 0.05 × 128 = 6.4 million.
Employed = 128 − 6.4 = 121.6 million.

Problem 6. Classify the adult population (in thousands) and compute the unemployment rate and participation rate.

Group Number
Full-time workers 92
Part-time workers 18
Looking for work, no job 9
Laid off, waiting to be recalled 3
Discouraged workers who stopped looking 4
Full-time students not working or looking 11
Retirees 25
Stay-at-home parents 14
Children under 16 40
Prison inmates 2
Show solution
  • Employed = 92 + 18 = 110 (part-time counts as employed).
  • Unemployed = 9 + 3 = 12 (a laid-off worker waiting for recall counts as unemployed).
  • Labor force = 122.
  • Not in the labor force = 4 + 11 + 25 + 14 = 54 (discouraged workers are not in the labor force).
  • Children and prisoners are not in the adult population.
  • Adult population = 122 + 54 = 176.
Unemployment Rate = 12122 = 9.8%
Participation Rate = 122176 = 69.3%

Problem 7. Year 1: 138 employed, 12 unemployed, adult population 220. Year 2: 141 employed, 15 unemployed, adult population 224. Compute the unemployment rate and participation rate in each year. Employment rose, so why did the unemployment rate rise?

Show solution
  • Year 1: LF = 150; UR = 12 ÷ 150 = 8.0%; LFPR = 150 ÷ 220 = 68.2%.
  • Year 2: LF = 156; UR = 15 ÷ 156 = 9.6%; LFPR = 156 ÷ 224 = 69.6%.

Employment rose by 3, but the labor force rose by 6, so more people were looking for work than found jobs. Unemployment rose because people entered the labor force (participation rose).

Problem 8. True or false: "The labor force participation rate is the share of the adult population that is employed."

Show solution

False. It is the share of the adult population that is employed or unemployed (in the labor force). The share that is employed is the employment-population ratio.

B. Entering and Leaving the Labor Force

(Problems 9–14 each start from: 150 employed, 10 unemployed, adult population 240. That gives UR = 6.25% and LFPR = 66.7%.)

Problem 9. Four unemployed workers find jobs. Find the new unemployment rate and participation rate.

Show solution

E = 154, U = 6, LF = 160.
UR = 6 ÷ 160 = 3.75%.
LFPR is unchanged at 66.7% (the labor force did not change).

Problem 10. Four unemployed workers become discouraged and stop looking. Find the new rates and comment.

Show solution

E = 150, U = 6, LF = 156.
UR = 6 ÷ 156 = 3.85%;
LFPR = 156 ÷ 240 = 65.0%.

The unemployment rate fell but nobody found a job. The drop is misleading: it came from a shrinking labor force.

Problem 11. Five employed workers are laid off and begin searching. Find the new rates.

Show solution

E = 145, U = 15, LF = 160.
UR = 15 ÷ 160 = 9.4%;
LFPR unchanged at 66.7%.

Problem 12. Eight people who were not in the labor force start looking for work, and none finds a job yet. Find the new rates.

Show solution

E = 150, U = 18, LF = 168.
UR = 18 ÷ 168 = 10.7%;
LFPR = 168 ÷ 240 = 70.0%.
Both rise.

Problem 13. Six people who were not in the labor force take jobs immediately. Find the new rates.

Show solution

E = 156, U = 10, LF = 166.
UR = 10 ÷ 166 = 6.0% (falls, since the denominator grows);
LFPR = 166 ÷ 240 = 69.2%.

Problem 14. Ten employed workers retire. Find the new rates.

Show solution

E = 140, U = 10, LF = 150.
UR = 10 ÷ 150 = 6.7% (up);
LFPR = 150 ÷ 240 = 62.5% (down).

C. Classify the Type of Unemployment

Problem 15. A recent college graduate is searching for a first job.

Show solution

Frictional: a normal job search, matching a worker with a firm.

Problem 16. A coal miner loses his job as power plants switch to natural gas and has no skills for the new jobs in the region.

Show solution

Structural: a mismatch between his skills and available jobs because of an industry shift.

Problem 17. A factory worker is laid off during a recession because demand for the firm's products has fallen.

Show solution

Cyclical: caused by a downturn in aggregate demand.

Problem 18. A worker quits her job to look for a better one and spends six weeks searching.

Show solution

Frictional (voluntary, short-term search).

Problem 19. A bank teller is replaced by software and needs retraining.

Show solution

Structural (technological change creates a skills mismatch).

Problem 20. A parent who left the workforce to raise a child is not looking for a job.

Show solution

None of these. The parent is not in the labor force, so is not counted as unemployed.

Problem 21. Construction workers lose their jobs when a housing slump cuts demand across the whole economy.

Show solution

Cyclical, because the cause is a drop in overall spending.

Problem 22. A union wage is set above the equilibrium wage, so more workers want jobs than firms will hire at that wage. What type is the resulting unemployment?

Show solution

Structural: a wage held above equilibrium creates a persistent surplus of labor.

D. Natural Rate and Cyclical Unemployment

Problem 23. The actual unemployment rate is 7.5% and the natural rate is 5.0%. Find cyclical unemployment and say where real GDP is relative to potential.

Show solution

Cyclical = 7.5 − 5.0 = 2.5 percentage points.
Actual is above natural, so real GDP is below potential (a recessionary gap).

Problem 24. Frictional unemployment is 2.5% and structural unemployment is 2.0%. What is the natural rate? If the economy is at full employment, what is the actual rate?

Show solution

Natural rate = 2.5 + 2.0 = 4.5%.
At full employment cyclical unemployment is zero, so the actual rate is also 4.5%.

Problem 25. The actual rate is 4.1% and the natural rate is 4.5%. Find cyclical unemployment and describe the economy.

Show solution

Cyclical = 4.1 − 4.5 = −0.4 points.
Actual unemployment is below the natural rate, so output is above potential (an inflationary gap): the economy is overheating.

Problem 26. Cyclical unemployment is 3.2 percentage points and the actual rate is 8.0%. Find the natural rate.

Show solution

Natural rate = 8.0 − 3.2 = 4.8%.

Problem 27. The table gives unemployment rates and the natural rate is 4.8%.

Year Actual rate
2019 4.0%
2020 8.2%
2021 6.4%
2022 5.4%
2023 4.9%

Find cyclical unemployment each year and describe the pattern.

Show solution

Cyclical = actual − 4.8: 2019: −0.8; 2020: +3.4; 2021: +1.6; 2022: +0.6; 2023: +0.1.

The economy starts above full employment, falls into a deep recession in 2020, then recovers toward the natural rate by 2023.

Problem 28. For each event, say whether the natural rate rises, falls or is unchanged: (a) a website that makes job matching faster; (b) a recession lasts three months; (c) a wave of automation leaves many workers with obsolete skills.

Show solution

(a) Falls (less frictional unemployment). (b) Unchanged: a recession raises cyclical unemployment, not the natural rate. (c) Rises (more structural unemployment).

E. Limitations of the Unemployment Rate

Problem 29. During a long recession, 3 million unemployed workers give up searching. What happens to the measured unemployment rate, and does it reflect an improvement?

Show solution

The measured rate falls, because those workers leave the labor force. It does not reflect an improvement: they are still jobless, but now discouraged workers outside the count. This is why the rate understates joblessness.

Problem 30. A worker with a PhD who wants a full-time job is working 15 hours a week at a coffee shop. How is she counted, and what does it show about the unemployment rate?

Show solution

She is counted as employed (any paid work counts). The unemployment rate therefore ignores underemployment and understates the amount of labor market slack.

Problem 31. Name one reason the measured unemployment rate might overstate joblessness.

Show solution

Some people reported as unemployed actually work in the underground economy (unreported jobs) or are not seriously searching. Either way, they are counted as unemployed without really being jobless.

Problem 32. Which of these workers is counted as unemployed: (a) a retiree who wants no job; (b) a discouraged worker who stopped searching six months ago; (c) a worker laid off last month who is searching actively; (d) a student working 10 hours per week?

Show solution

Only (c). (a) and (b) are not in the labor force, and (d) is employed.

F. Okun's Law (College-Level)

(Use Okun's law with a coefficient of 2: output gap (%) = −2 × (u − u*).)

Problem 33. Unemployment is 7% and the natural rate is 5%. By how much is output below potential?

Show solution

Cyclical unemployment = 2 points, so output is 2 × 2 = 4% below potential.

Problem 34. Potential output is 12,000 (billions). With the unemployment gap in Problem 33, how large is the output shortfall in dollars, and what is actual output?

Show solution

Shortfall = 4% × 12,000 = 480.
Actual output = 12,000 − 480 = 11,520.

Problem 35. Unemployment falls from 9% to 6% and the natural rate is 5%. By how many percentage points of potential output does the output gap close?

Show solution

Cyclical unemployment falls from 4 to 1 point, so the output shortfall goes from 8% to 2% below potential: the gap closes by 6 percentage points of potential output (a 3-point fall in unemployment × 2).

Problem 36. Unemployment is 4% and the natural rate is 5.5%. What does Okun's law say about output?

Show solution

Cyclical unemployment = −1.5, so output is 2 × 1.5 = 3% above potential: an inflationary gap.
