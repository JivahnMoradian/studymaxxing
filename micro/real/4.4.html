Oligopoly & Game Theory

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

An oligopoly is a market run by a handful of big players (gas, airlines, phone carriers, oil), where each one's best move depends on what the others do. Game theory is the tool for thinking that through, and its most famous lesson, the prisoner's dilemma, explains why rivals who would all gain by cooperating so often don't.

Key Terms

  • Oligopoly: A market dominated by a few large firms, with high barriers to entry. The telltale sign is interdependence: each firm has to guess and react to what the others will do.
  • Collusion and Cartels: When the few firms agree, openly or quietly, to act like a single monopoly, holding output down and prices up. A formal agreement is a cartel. Price-fixing cartels are illegal in the US and most rich countries; OPEC is a cartel of governments, which is a different legal animal.
  • Dominant Strategy: A choice that is your best move no matter what the other side does. Not every game has one.
  • Nash Equilibrium: An outcome where no one can do better by changing their move alone, given what everyone else is doing. It is where strategic situations tend to settle, even when a better deal for all exists.
  • Prisoner's Dilemma: A situation where each side has a private incentive to "defect" (cut price, overproduce, confess), so both defect and both end up worse off than if they had cooperated. It is the core reason cartels are unstable.

The Big Picture

The defining tension of oligopoly is that firms can both gain by cooperating, but each can gain even more by cheating while the others cooperate. Picture two gas stations on the same corner. If both keep prices high, both make good money. But either one can win customers by shaving its price, so each is tempted to cut; and once one cuts, the other must follow or lose everyone. They can slide into a price war that is great for drivers and bad for both stations. The same logic runs through airline fares, phone plans, and oil quotas, and it is why visible price wars and collapsed cartels are so common.

Think About It

1. Two rival firms would both make more money if they both kept prices high. Why do they so often fail to, even without any law stopping them?

Short Answer

Because "both high" is not stable. From there, either firm can cut its price and steal the other's customers, jumping to an even better payoff for itself. Each knows the other faces the same temptation, so both cut, and they land at the worse "both low" outcome. That is the prisoner's dilemma: the tempting move for each ruins the best outcome for both.

2. Why is a cartel like OPEC always in danger of falling apart, even when every member wants high prices?

Short Answer

Because once the group agrees to hold output down and prop up the price, each member can earn more by quietly pumping extra at that high price, hoping the others stick to their limits. But everyone has the same incentive, so cheating spreads, output creeps up, and the price the cartel was defending starts to sag.

Case Study: OPEC, Quotas, and the Cheating Problem

OPEC and its partners (together "OPEC+") are the clearest real-world cartel: a group of oil-producing governments that agree on production quotas to keep crude prices up. The arrangement is a running prisoner's dilemma. Every member would benefit from high prices, but each also has a standing temptation to pump a little more than its quota and pocket the extra revenue while the price stays high, counting on the others to hold the line.

That temptation shows up constantly in the news. Saudi Arabia, the group's biggest producer, has repeatedly cut its own output far below its quota to defend prices, while publicly scolding members who overproduce; its deputy minister once warned that trying to over-produce and hide it "always end[s] in failure." As recently as mid-2025, reports said a subset of OPEC+ countries were collectively pumping hundreds of thousands of barrels a day above target, with Kazakhstan a notable over-producer, and the group has pushed chronic cheaters to make "compensation" cuts, with limited success. At times Saudi Arabia has responded by opening its own taps to punish cheaters with lower prices and reclaim market share, exactly the "price war" branch of the game.

Why It Matters Outside the Oil Market: This is the prisoner's dilemma playing out among whole countries, with your gasoline and heating bills riding on the outcome. It shows why cooperation among rivals is fragile even when all of them want it to hold, and why the threat of someone defecting, and of retaliation, keeps resurfacing. Watching OPEC is watching game theory in real time.

Case Study: The Lysine Price-Fixing Cartel (ADM, 1990S)

When rivals collude in secret rather than in the open, it is usually illegal, and the story of lysine shows both the payoff and the risk. Lysine is an additive used in animal feed. In the early 1990s, executives at the US agribusiness giant Archer Daniels Midland (ADM) and several Asian competitors met secretly to fix its price and carve up sales, acting like a single monopoly over a commodity farmers everywhere depended on. The motto inside the conspiracy, later revealed on tape, was that the competitor was the friend and the customer was the enemy.

What they didn't know was that one ADM executive, Mark Whitacre, had become an FBI informant and spent roughly three years secretly recording their meetings. The case broke open in 1995, and in 1996 ADM pleaded guilty and paid a $100 million criminal fine, at the time the largest criminal antitrust fine in US history, covering the lysine scheme and a related one. Executives, including Whitacre himself, ultimately went to prison.

Why It Matters Outside the Feed Industry: This is the prisoner's dilemma that antitrust law is built to exploit. Cartels are unstable partly because members cheat on each other, and partly because the law offers big rewards (leniency and whistleblower deals) to whoever breaks ranks and talks first. The same incentive structure that makes a cartel fragile also makes it catchable.

Sources: Lysine price-fixing conspiracy (Wikipedia overview), Purdue case study on the lysine cartel.

The Takeaway

In an oligopoly, you can't understand any one firm's prices without asking what it expects its rivals to do. The prisoner's dilemma is the key: rivals routinely fail to sustain the cozy high-price outcome because each gains by defecting, which is why genuine price wars erupt and why cartels, legal or not, keep cracking. For consumers that instability is often good news: every breakdown in cooperation tends to mean lower prices. It is also why the law works so hard to make secret cooperation even harder to hold together.
