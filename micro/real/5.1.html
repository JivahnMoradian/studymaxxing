5.1

The Demand for Labor (MRP)

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

An employer decides what a worker is worth by the extra money that worker brings in. That number, the worker's marginal revenue product, is the ceiling on what anyone can be paid for long, and it explains most of why some jobs pay far more than others.

Key Terms

  • Derived Demand: Firms don't want workers for their own sake. They want the revenue the workers' output earns. So the demand for any kind of labor rises and falls with demand for the thing those workers make. When people stop buying a product, the jobs that make it go first.
  • Marginal Product (MP): The extra output one more worker adds. A second cook in a small kitchen adds a lot; the fifth, crammed into the same space, adds much less.
  • Marginal Revenue Product (MRP): The extra revenue one more worker brings in, which is their extra output multiplied by the price it sells for. This is the most a profit-minded employer will rationally pay that worker. Pay someone more than the revenue they generate and you lose money on them.
  • Diminishing Returns: As you pile more workers onto the same space and equipment, each one tends to add a little less than the last, so MRP falls as the workforce grows. That is why labor demand slopes downward: firms hire more only as the wage drops.
  • Price Taker vs. Market Power: A firm that sells at a fixed going price values a worker at output times that price. A firm that has to cut its price to sell more earns less on each extra unit, so its workers' MRP is lower and it hires fewer of them.

The Big Picture

Most pay gaps that look like they are about fairness or effort are really about MRP. A worker's wage is tied not to how hard the job is, or how skilled or decent the person is, but to how much revenue an hour of their work throws off. A kindergarten teacher and a derivatives trader can work equally hard; the trader's hour moves more money, so the market pays it more. This is uncomfortable, but it is also why understanding MRP is useful: it tells you that the way to raise your own pay is usually to raise the revenue your work generates, by getting more productive, moving to where your output is worth more, or acquiring a skill that is scarce relative to demand.

Think About It

1. A heart surgeon earns far more than a home health aide who may work just as hard and just as many hours. Why?

Short Answer

An hour of the surgeon's work generates a great deal of revenue (and there are very few people who can do it), so the surgeon's marginal revenue product is high. The aide's work is valuable to the family but generates little billable revenue per hour, so the market pays far less. MRP, not effort, sets the going rate.

2. A team owner is deciding whether to pay a star player $30 million a year. How should the owner think about it?

Short Answer

Compare the pay to the extra revenue the player brings in: added ticket sales, television money, jerseys and sponsorships. If the player's marginal revenue product is above $30 million, the deal makes money even at that eye-watering salary; if it is below, the owner overpays. Star pay is high because star output is sold to huge audiences.

3. A new machine does part of your job. Does that always cut your pay?

Short Answer

Not necessarily. If the machine replaces you, demand for your work falls. But if it makes you more productive, so you handle more customers or higher-value tasks, it can raise your marginal revenue product and your pay. The same technology can cut one kind of job and make another more valuable.

Case Study: What a Ballplayer Is Really Worth (the Reserve Clause, 1974)

For most of baseball's history a player was bound to his team by the reserve clause: he could not sell his services to the highest bidder, so the club, not the market, set his salary. In 1974 the economist Gerald Scully published a study in the American Economic Review that asked a simple question: how much revenue does a player actually generate, and how does that compare with what he is paid?

Scully did it in two steps that are pure MRP. First he estimated how many extra wins a player's performance produced (his marginal product). Then he estimated how much revenue each extra win brought the club in ticket sales and the like (turning wins into dollars). Multiplying the two gave each player's marginal revenue product. The finding was stark: under the reserve clause, players were paid only a fraction of the revenue they generated, with estimates for established stars running well below half, and in some cases a small share, of their MRP. The team captured the rest because players had nowhere else to sell their talent.

The sequel is the clincher. After an arbitrator struck down the reserve clause at the end of 1975 and players won free agency in 1976, salaries exploded almost immediately, because players could finally make teams bid against each other. The revenue they produced had not suddenly changed; what changed was that they could now capture it rather than hand it to the owner.

Why It Matters Outside Baseball: Two forces set your pay, and MRP is only the first. The value you produce is the ceiling. How much of that value you actually take home depends on your bargaining position: whether you can credibly take your work elsewhere. Workers tied to a single employer (by a contract, a visa, a non-compete, or a one-company town) tend to be paid below what they are worth, exactly as ballplayers were. (That gap between pay and MRP is the heart of the monopsony chapter, 5.4.)

Sources: What Is Right With Scully Estimates of a Player's Marginal Revenue Product (Kennesaw State), Berkeley senior honors thesis applying the Scully method, Overview of the Scully approach (The Hardball Times).

The Takeaway

A wage is not a measure of worth as a human being; it is roughly the revenue an hour of the work generates, pushed up or down by how easily the worker can walk away. If you want to understand why a job pays what it does, or how to earn more, ask what revenue the work produces and whether you can take that skill to a competing buyer.
