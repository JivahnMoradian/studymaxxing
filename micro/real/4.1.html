Monopoly

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

A monopoly is the only seller of something people can't easily get elsewhere, so it can hold output down and charge more than it could if it had rivals. Pure monopolies are rare, but near-monopolies shape prices you pay for medicines, utilities, and a lot of what happens online.

Key Terms

  • Monopoly: A single seller of a product with no close substitute. Because there is nowhere else to go, the seller sets the price rather than taking it from the market.
  • Barrier to Entry: Whatever stops new competitors from showing up and undercutting the price. The main ones are a patent or license, control of a scarce resource, a government franchise, or costs so large that one firm is cheapest.
  • Patent: A time-limited legal monopoly (20 years from filing in the US) that lets an inventor, often a drug company, be the only seller while it lasts. It is a deliberate trade: higher prices now in exchange for the invention existing at all.
  • Natural Monopoly: A market where one firm can serve everyone more cheaply than several could, because the upfront network (pipes, wires, track) costs a fortune and each extra customer costs almost nothing. Water, local electricity delivery, and gas pipelines are the classic cases.
  • Deadweight Loss: The trades that simply don't happen because the monopoly price is above what it costs to make another unit. Some people who would gladly pay more than the true cost still don't buy, and that lost value is gone for everyone.
  • Antitrust (Competition Law): The body of law used to block anticompetitive mergers, stop price-fixing, and rein in firms that abuse a dominant position.

The Big Picture

A monopolist doesn't charge "whatever it wants" with no limit. It still faces the fact that higher prices drive customers away. What it can do, unlike a seller surrounded by competitors, is deliberately produce less so the price stays high. That is the heart of the problem: the monopoly outcome is usually fewer units sold at a higher price than a competitive market would deliver, which is why monopoly power is something governments watch.

Think About It

1. A drug comes off patent and generic versions appear. What usually happens to the price, and why?

Short Answer

It usually falls sharply, often by well over half within a year or two. The patent was the barrier to entry; once it expires, rival manufacturers can legally make the same molecule, competition breaks the single-seller pricing power, and the price drops toward the cost of production.

2. Why does your town have one water company and one set of water pipes, instead of three competing ones?

Short Answer

Because water delivery is a natural monopoly: the pipes cost enormous sums to lay and then serve each house for almost nothing extra. A second or third competing network would just duplicate that cost and raise everyone's bills, so one firm is cheapest. The usual answer is to let one company run it but have a regulator approve its prices.

3. A company has a 90% market share. Is that illegal?

Short Answer

No. Simply being big or dominant is not against the law in the US. Antitrust law targets how a firm got or keeps that position. Winning share by building a better, cheaper product is fine; locking out rivals through exclusive deals or buying up competitors is what can draw a case.

Case Study: Daraprim Goes from $13.50 to $750 Overnight (2015)

Daraprim is an old drug, first approved in 1953, that treats a parasite infection dangerous to people with weak immune systems, such as some AIDS and cancer patients. Its patent had expired decades earlier, so in principle anyone could make it. In August 2015 a new company, Turing Pharmaceuticals, bought the US rights for about $55 million, and in September it raised the list price from about $13.50 a tablet to $750, a jump of more than 5,000%. A full course of treatment went from the low thousands into the tens of thousands of dollars.

How could an off-patent drug command that price? Not through a patent, but through other barriers to entry. The patient population was tiny, so no generic maker had bothered to compete, and Turing used a tightly controlled distribution system that made it hard for a would-be competitor to even obtain the samples it needed to win regulatory approval for a copy. For a while, Turing was effectively the only seller. After a public outcry, the company's founder said he would lower the price, but Turing's own account later stated the $750 list price never actually came down; instead it leaned on patient-assistance programs. A competing generic finally reached the market in 2020.

Why It Matters Outside the Pharmacy: A monopoly doesn't require a clever invention or a patent. It just requires that nobody else can easily sell the same thing. When one seller controls supply, even a cheap, decades-old product can be repriced at will, and the limit is public and political backlash, not competition.

Sources: Fortune on Turing and Daraprim, Turing Pharmaceuticals' own account, NYT report, as reproduced.

Case Study: De Beers and "a Diamond Is Forever"

For most of the 20th century, a single company, De Beers, came as close to a world monopoly as any firm ever has. By controlling South African mines and then buying up or channeling production from elsewhere, it is estimated to have sold roughly 80 to 90% of the world's rough diamonds through the 1980s. It kept prices high the textbook way, by holding diamonds back when demand sagged and releasing them when it recovered, and it spent heavily on advertising, including the famous "A Diamond Is Forever" slogan that helped turn diamond engagement rings into a near-obligation.

The grip loosened. New mines in Russia, Australia, and Canada produced diamonds De Beers didn't control, and its share fell toward 60% by the late 1990s. In 2000 the company formally abandoned its decades-long role as the market's stockpiler and price-manager, and today it supplies only around a third of the market.

Why It Matters Outside the Jewelry Store: Controlling a resource can build a monopoly without any patent or law, but it is hard to sustain. New supply the monopolist can't control is the natural enemy of that kind of power, which is one reason resource monopolies tend to erode over time.

Sources: Britannica on De Beers, GIA review of diamond industry forces.

How Natural Monopolies Get Handled

Where one firm genuinely is the cheapest way to serve a market, such as the wires and pipes that deliver electricity, gas, and water, governments don't try to force competition. In the US, most electricity customers, roughly 70% or more, are served by investor-owned utilities that operate as regulated monopolies: you cannot pick a different company for the physical delivery, but a state regulator approves the rates. The usual approach lets the firm earn a set "rate of return" on what it invests, enough to stay in business and keep the lights on, while capping how much it can charge. The constant tension is that regulators have to estimate the firm's real costs, and the firm always has reasons to argue those costs are high.

Sources: UC Berkeley working paper on utility regulation, The American Prospect on utility rates.

Case Study: The Google Antitrust Fight (2024–2026)

In August 2024 a federal judge, Amit Mehta, ruled that Google had illegally maintained a monopoly in online search, pointing in particular to the many billions of dollars Google pays each year to be the default search engine on phones and browsers. In September 2025 the same judge decided what to do about it. He declined to break the company up: Google will not be forced to sell its Chrome browser or Android system, and it can keep paying for default placement. But he barred certain exclusive contracts and ordered Google to share some search data with rivals. Both sides are unhappy: Google is appealing the finding that it broke the law, while the Justice Department and a group of states are appealing to get tougher remedies.

This case sits in genuinely contested territory, and reasonable people disagree. One view holds that dominant tech firms use their position to box out competitors and that strong remedies protect consumers and innovation. Another holds that these products are free and popular precisely because they are good, that heavy intervention can backfire, and that courts should be cautious about redesigning successful companies. The law itself tries to thread this needle: it does not punish being big, only acquiring or protecting dominance through conduct judged anticompetitive. Where exactly that line falls is what the appeals will decide.

Sources: Stanford Law on the Google remedies, ITIF on the remedies decision, Reuters/Yahoo on the appeals.

The Takeaway

Real pure monopolies are uncommon, but market power sits on a spectrum. The monopoly strategy of produce less and charge more shows up wherever a seller has no close rival. The policy responses sort into two buckets. Where one firm really is cheapest (utilities and other natural monopolies), regulate its prices. Everywhere else, use antitrust to keep competition alive, while arguing, as societies always will, about exactly where fair competition ends and abuse begins.
