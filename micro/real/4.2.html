Price Discrimination

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Price discrimination is charging different people different prices for the same thing, based on how much they're willing to pay rather than on what it costs to serve them. Once you know the trick, you see it everywhere: student and senior discounts, airline fares, coupons, "financial aid", and the price your phone shows you.

Key Terms

  • Price Discrimination: Selling the same (or nearly the same) good at different prices to different buyers, aimed at charging more to people who will pay more. It is legal and common, and not about cost differences.
  • Willingness to Pay: The most a given buyer would hand over before walking away. In price discrimination, the whole game is to charge each buyer the maximum price they would be willing to pay, instead of setting one price for all.
  • The Three Conditions: A seller can do this only if it has some pricing power (not a tiny player in a cutthroat market), can tell the high-value buyers from the low-value ones, and can stop cheap buyers from reselling to expensive ones.
  • Third-Degree (Group) Pricing: Different observable groups pay different prices, such as students, seniors, or off-peak travelers. The rule of thumb: the group that is more price-sensitive is offered the lower price.
  • Second-Degree (Self-Selection) Pricing: The seller can't tell who you are, so it offers a menu, bulk discounts or Basic/Standard/Premium tiers, and lets you sort yourself by what you pick.
  • First-Degree (Personalized) Pricing: The seller tries to charge each buyer their individual maximum. A haggling car dealer, a college financial-aid office, and some online "personalized" prices come closest.

The Big Picture

Aside from in perfect competition, a single price always leaves money on the table in two directions: some eager buyers would have paid more, and some reluctant ones were scared off. Price discrimination lets the seller charge the eager ones more while still making a sale to the reluctant ones with a discount. That is why a discount for one group is not charity; it is a way to grab extra business the full price would have lost, without lowering the price for everyone else. The flip side for you as a buyer is simple: whenever a lower price exists for someone, it is worth finding out whether you qualify.

Think About It

1. A cinema charges adults $15 and students $9 for the very same film. Why would it bother, instead of just setting one price?

Short Answer

Because students are more price-sensitive; many would skip a $15 ticket but happily pay $9. The cinema keeps charging full-price adults $15 while picking up student business it would otherwise lose. It needs the ID check at the door so the two groups can't swap tickets.

2. Why do coupons and clip-the-app discounts exist, when the store could just lower the price for everybody?

Short Answer

Coupons quietly separate shoppers by price sensitivity. People who care a lot about price will spend the few minutes to clip or scan; people who don't, won't. The store ends up charging the careful shoppers less and the time-pressed ones more, for the same product, without a visible two-tier price tag.

3. Two people on the same flight, in identical seats, paid wildly different fares. Did the airline make a mistake?

Short Answer

No. That is deliberate price discrimination. The one who booked months ahead and stayed over a weekend is probably a vacationer, flexible and price-sensitive. The one who booked two days out is probably traveling for work, with an inelastic "I have to be there" demand, and is charged far more for the same seat.

Case Study: Nobody Pays the College Sticker Price

A private US college might advertise a tuition "sticker price" that very few families actually pay. Colleges hand out their own grant aid, called "institutional aid", that shaves the real price down family by family, and the gap is enormous. According to the annual NACUBO survey of private nonprofit colleges, the average tuition "discount rate" for first-time, full-time freshmen reached roughly 54 to 56% in 2024–25. In plain terms, these schools collect, on average, only about 44 to 46 cents of every dollar of published tuition.

This is close to first-degree price discrimination done politely. The financial-aid form asks, in effect, what each family can afford, and the net price is then tailored to that answer. A wealthy family pays something near the sticker price; a lower-income family pays a fraction of it, for the identical education and diploma. Resale is impossible: you can't sell your admission to someone else, so the scheme holds together.

Why It Matters Outside Admissions: A high advertised price with big individualized "discounts" is a pricing strategy, not a sale. The same pattern appears in hospital bills, car dealerships, and enterprise software. When the list price is almost never what anyone pays, your job as a buyer is to find the discount you qualify for and to treat the sticker as an opening bid.

Sources: NACUBO tuition discounting hits a new high (Inside Higher Ed), NASFAA on the NACUBO study.

Case Study: The Same Airline Seat at Five Different Prices

Airlines are the masters of this. Two passengers in neighboring economy seats can pay very different amounts, and the airline designs it that way. The central split is business versus leisure travel. A vacationer books weeks ahead, is flexible about dates, and will shop around, so they are offered cheap advance fares. A business traveler often books days before departure, can't move the trip, and someone else is paying, so they face much higher fares. One analysis of corporate bookings found that buying zero to two days before departure carried a premium of more than 50% (around $250 a ticket) over booking two weeks or more ahead for the same flight.

For decades airlines enforced the split with rules like the "Saturday-night stay" requirement: cheap fares demanded you stay over a weekend, which vacationers happily did and business travelers hated. That particular trick has faded as budget carriers offer low walk-up fares, but the basic structure still sorts travelers by how price-sensitive they are: advance-purchase discounts, cheap non-refundable tickets versus flexible expensive ones, and stripped-down "basic economy".

Why It Matters Outside the Airport: The price you see is tied to the story your booking tells about you: when you buy, how flexible you are, and which device you're on. You can often pay the lower price simply by behaving like the price-sensitive group: book early, stay flexible, and compare.

Sources: Frommer's on how airline fares work.

The Takeaway

Price discrimination is not a scam; it is a rational response to the fact that buyers value things differently, and it often means a product reaches people who could never afford a single high price. But it does transfer money from the less price-sensitive to the sellers. The practical lesson for everyday life is to notice when a cheaper price exists for somebody and to ask whether a little effort (booking earlier, clipping the coupon, filling out the aid form, or flashing an ID) puts you in the group that pays less.
