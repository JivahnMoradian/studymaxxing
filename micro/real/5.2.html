5.2

Competitive Labor Markets & Shifts

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Wages are a price, set where the number of people willing to do a job meets employers' demand for it. When something shifts either the supply or demand for labor, whether it be booming demand for a product or a wave of new workers, pay and employment move in predictable directions.

Key Terms

  • Labor Demand: How many workers employers want at each wage. It slopes down (cheaper labor, more hiring) and it is derived from demand for the product: it rises when the thing those workers make becomes more valuable.
  • Labor Supply: How many people are willing to work at each wage. It slopes up: higher pay draws more people in, pulls them from other jobs, and tempts them to work more hours.
  • Equilibrium Wage: The wage at which the number of workers wanted equals the number willing to work. Above it there is a surplus of workers (unemployment in that market); below it, a shortage employers struggle to fill.
  • Wage Taker: A single small employer that is too minor to move the market wage, so it simply pays the going rate. Most firms are wage takers; the exception, a dominant local employer, is the monopsony case in 5.4.
  • What Shifts Demand: Anything that changes how much revenue a worker generates. Booming product demand, higher productivity, or a complementary tool getting cheaper all push labor demand up and wages up.
  • What Shifts Supply: Anything that changes how many people are available. Immigration or population growth, people moving in from other regions or occupations, or a drop in pay elsewhere all push supply up, which tends to lower the wage while raising employment.

Think About It

1. A region gets a sudden inflow of new workers. What happens to wages and to the number of people employed?

Short Answer

Labor supply shifts out, so the wage tends to fall and total employment rises. In practice the size of the wage effect is debated: new workers also spend money and create demand, which can offset the downward pressure, and the effect depends on whether they compete with or complement the workers already there.

2. Remote work lets a software engineer in a small town take a job at a big-city company. What happens to that engineer's pay?

Short Answer

It tends to rise. Remote work widens the set of employers bidding for the worker, so local supply and demand matter less and the worker is pulled toward the higher wage of the bigger market. The same force can push down pay in expensive cities as employers hire from cheaper places.

3. A factory installs robots that are a substitute for its assembly workers, and the robots get much cheaper. What happens to the demand for those workers?

Short Answer

It falls. When a cheaper machine can do the same job, firms replace workers with machines, so labor demand shifts down and both the wage and employment in that role tend to drop. (If the machine were a complement, something workers operate rather than something that replaces them, cheaper machines would raise the demand for those workers.)

Case Study: The North Dakota Oil Boom, 2004–2014

In the mid-2000s new drilling techniques unlocked the Bakken shale in western North Dakota, and the demand for workers in towns like Williston exploded. Because labor demand is derived from the value of what workers produce, and oil was suddenly very valuable to extract there, employers of every kind found themselves bidding for a tiny pool of available people.

The result was a textbook rightward shift in labor demand against a nearly fixed local supply. Between 2004 and 2011 the average annual wage in the core oil-producing counties rose more than 80% in inflation-adjusted terms, to about $56,000, far outpacing the state and the nation. And it was not just oil workers. The scramble for labor spilled into every sector: in those counties, real wages for hotel and food service workers rose about 63% over the same period, and manufacturing wages about 21%. The Minneapolis Fed estimated that this across-the-board bidding, rather than the shift of jobs into oil, accounted for roughly three quarters of the wage surge. On the ground it meant the Walmart in Williston reportedly advertised entry-level pay around $17 an hour, when the going rate for such work elsewhere was closer to $8 to $10.

Then came the other half of derived demand. When world oil prices crashed in 2014–2015, the number of working rigs dropped sharply, hiring froze, and the frantic bidding for workers cooled. The jobs had appeared because the product was valuable; when it stopped being valuable, the demand for labor contracted with it.

Why It Matters Outside North Dakota: Your wage is tethered to the value of what your employer sells, not just to your own effort. When demand for a region's or industry's product booms, pay can jump even for unrelated local jobs, because everyone is competing for the same workers. When that demand falls, the same mechanism runs in reverse. It is worth knowing whether your pay is riding a boom in the thing your employer sells.

Sources: Minneapolis Fed, "A rising oil tide lifts all wages" (2012), NPR on Williston wages (2012), The Fiscal Times on the North Dakota job market (2013).

The Takeaway

To predict where a wage is heading, ask two questions. Is the value of what these workers produce rising or falling (demand)? And is the pool of people able to do the work growing or shrinking (supply)? A rise in demand lifts both pay and jobs; a rise in supply lifts jobs but tends to hold pay down. Almost every wage story in the news, from nursing shortages to immigration debates to AI, is one of those two curves moving.
