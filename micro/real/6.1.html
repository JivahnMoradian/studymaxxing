Externalities

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

An externality is a cost or benefit that lands on someone who was not part of the deal: the smoke from a factory, the quiet from a neighbor's garden, the traffic one more car adds to a crowded road. When these spillovers are large, the free market makes too much of the bad thing and too little of the good thing, and that is where taxes, subsidies and permits come in.

Key Terms

  • Externality: A side effect of producing or consuming something that falls on a third party who is neither the buyer nor the seller. It can be negative (pollution, noise, congestion) or positive (a vaccine that also protects the people around you).
  • Negative Externality: The spillover is a cost. Because the person causing it does not pay for it, the market produces too much of it. Example: a power plant's air pollution.
  • Positive Externality: The spillover is a benefit others get for free. Because the person creating it is not paid for it, the market produces too little. Example: getting vaccinated, or restoring an old building that makes a street nicer.
  • Pigouvian Tax: A tax set equal to the damage an activity does to others, named after economist Arthur Pigou. The idea is not mainly to raise money but to make the polluter feel the full cost, so they cut back to the amount society actually wants. A carbon tax is the best-known example.
  • Cap-and-Trade: Instead of taxing each unit of pollution, the government sets a hard limit (the cap) on the total allowed, hands out or sells permits adding up to that cap, and lets firms buy and sell them. Firms that can cut cheaply do so and sell their spare permits; firms that find it expensive buy permits instead. The cap fixes the quantity of pollution; the market sets the price.

The Big Picture

Most of the things people argue about in environmental and transport policy are arguments about externalities, even when nobody uses the word. Should drivers pay to enter a crowded city center? Should carbon have a price? Should power plants be allowed to pollute as long as they buy a permit? In each case the starting point is the same: someone's private choice imposes a cost on strangers, and the market price does not reflect it. The disagreements are usually about how big the cost is and what to do about it, not about whether the spillover is real.

Think About It

1. Your neighbor plays loud music at 2 a.m. In what sense is this an "externality," and why does the market not fix it on its own?

Short Answer

The noise is a cost imposed on you, but your neighbor pays nothing for it, so they "overproduce" it relative to what is fair. The market does not fix it because there is no price on late-night noise. Fixes come from outside the market: a noise ordinance (regulation), a fine (a tax on the behavior), or simply you and your neighbor working it out directly.

2. A flu shot protects you, but it also makes it less likely you infect other people. Left alone, will the market provide too many flu shots or too few?

Short Answer

Too few. You decide based on the benefit to yourself, ignoring the benefit to everyone you do not infect. Because that extra benefit is not captured in the price, fewer people get vaccinated than would be best for society. This is why governments often make vaccines free or even pay people to get them.

3. Two ways to cut pollution: a tax per ton emitted, or a fixed cap on total emissions with tradable permits. What is the key difference in what each one controls?

Short Answer

A tax fixes the price of polluting and lets the total amount of pollution settle wherever firms' choices land it. A cap fixes the total amount of pollution and lets the price of a permit settle wherever the market lands it. If you care most about hitting an exact emissions target, a cap does that directly; if you care most about a predictable cost, a tax does that.

Case Study: London's Congestion Charge (2003 to Today)

On February 17, 2003, London started charging drivers a daily fee to enter the center of the city during working hours. The logic is pure externality economics: each extra car on a packed road slows down everyone else, and the driver does not pay for that delay. The first-year fee was 5 pounds a day. Transport for London, the city agency, reported that in the first year traffic entering the zone during charging hours fell by roughly 18 percent and congestion inside the zone dropped by around 30 percent, with no big jump in traffic on roads just outside the zone.

The fee has climbed over the years to keep it effective as prices and incomes rose. It reached 15 pounds a day in 2020, and on January 2, 2026 it rose to 18 pounds (21 pounds if you pay late), the first increase since 2020, with further yearly rises now tied to public-transport fares. By law, the net revenue has to be plowed back into London's transport network; TfL reported that by 2014 more than 1.2 billion pounds had been reinvested, including about 960 million pounds on the bus network.

The scheme is not a clean success story. Over the longer run, traffic speeds in central London have drifted back down to roughly where they were in 2003, even though the volume of traffic is lower, as the city's population grew and roadworks and delivery vans took up space. The charge slowed the problem rather than solving it.

Why It Matters Outside London: This is what "putting a price on an externality" looks like in daily life. A toll, a congestion charge, or a surge-pricing scheme is charging you for the delay or crowding you add to everyone else. Whether you think it is fair often comes down to who the money ends up helping, which is why the rule about reinvesting the revenue matters as much as the charge itself.

Sources: London congestion charge (Wikipedia), TfL first-year report, Arup, two decades on, TfL proposal to raise to 18 pounds.

Case Study: Trading Pollution Permits to Fight Acid Rain

In the 1980s, sulfur dioxide from coal-burning power plants was falling as acid rain across the eastern United States and Canada, damaging lakes and forests. The 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments tried a then-novel fix: a nationwide cap on power-plant sulfur dioxide, split into tradable permits. A plant that cut its emissions cheaply could sell its leftover permits to one that found cutting expensive. The program began phasing in from 1995.

The results were striking on cost and quantity. Power-sector sulfur dioxide emissions fell from about 15.7 million tons in 1990 to under 1 million tons by 2019, a drop of roughly 94 percent. The EPA says compliance cost far less than the 1990 projections, and a 2005 study estimated annual health benefits (mostly from cleaner air) worth well over 100 billion dollars against costs of around 3 billion dollars a year.

There is a real caveat worth stating plainly. Because permits could be traded, emissions did not fall evenly everywhere. A 2016 analysis argued that the trading pattern actually shifted some pollution toward more densely populated areas, so the health gains, while large, were smaller than they could have been under a less flexible design. Cap-and-trade minimizes the total cost of hitting a target; it does not guarantee that the cleanup happens in the places where it would help the most people.

Why It Matters Outside Power Plants: This is the template behind carbon markets in Europe, California and elsewhere. The lesson cuts both ways: letting firms trade is a cheap way to hit an overall pollution target, but "cheapest overall" is not the same as "fairest to every neighborhood," and where the pollution ends up still matters.

Sources: EPA Acid Rain Program results, EPA power plant emission trends, NBER digest on SO2 trading and health.

A Note on Carbon Taxes (a Contested Area)

Carbon pricing applies the same idea to climate change: burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide whose costs fall on people worldwide, so a carbon tax or cap tries to put a price on that damage. Sweden has had a carbon tax since 1991. Supporters argue a price is the cheapest way to cut emissions because it lets each person and firm find their own way to use less, and that the revenue can be returned to households. Critics worry about the burden on lower-income families and on energy-intensive industries, and about businesses relocating to places with no carbon price. Economists broadly agree the externality is real; the live debates are over the right price per ton (genuinely hard to pin down), how to protect those hit hardest, and whether a tax, a cap, or direct regulation works best.

Sources: Sweden's carbon tax (Government of Sweden), Tax Foundation, 30 years of carbon taxes in Sweden.

The Takeaway

When a choice quietly dumps costs on strangers, markets do too much of it; when it quietly helps strangers, markets do too little. The usual repairs are to put a price on the spillover (a tax or a tradable permit), to pay for the good version (a subsidy, like free vaccines), or to set rules. None of these is automatic or painless, and reasonable people disagree about how far to go, but spotting the externality is the first step to understanding almost any pollution, traffic or public-health debate.
