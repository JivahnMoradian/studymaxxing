Costs of Production

Real-World Relevance: Medium
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Every business, and every household, splits its costs into the ones that do not move when you do a little more or a little less (fixed) and the ones that do (variable). Knowing which is which is what tells you when to push harder, when to cut a price, and when to walk away.

Key Terms

  • Fixed Cost: A cost you owe no matter how much you produce or sell, at least for now: rent, insurance, a loan payment, the lease on a plane. It is there even if output is zero.
  • Variable Cost: A cost that rises and falls with how much you do: ingredients, fuel, hourly wages, packaging. No output, no variable cost.
  • Marginal Cost: The cost of doing exactly one more unit, one more meal, one more passenger, one more download. This is the number that should drive "should I make one more?" decisions, and it is often much smaller than people expect.
  • Sunk Cost: Money already spent that you cannot get back, whatever you choose next. It is the trap term: a cost being sunk means it should be ignored in today's decision, even though it feels like it should count.
  • Fixed Cost vs. Sunk Cost: Related but not the same. A fixed cost is just one that does not vary with output; some fixed costs can still be escaped (you can cancel a monthly subscription). A cost is sunk only when it is already gone and unrecoverable. Rent for next month is fixed but not yet sunk; last month's rent is sunk.

Think About It

1. You paid $60 for a concert ticket. On the night, you feel ill and would honestly rather stay home, and the ticket cannot be resold. Should the $60 make you go?

Short Answer

No. The $60 is a sunk cost: it is gone whether you go or not, so it should not tip the decision. The only question left is whether a night out sick beats a night resting. Dragging yourself out "so the money is not wasted" is the sunk-cost fallacy, and the money is already wasted either way.

2. A nearly empty flight is about to leave with ten spare seats. The airline offers them cheap at the gate. How can selling a $600 seat for $90 make sense?

Short Answer

Because almost all the cost of that flight, the plane, the crew, the fuel to fly it, is already committed whether the seat is full or empty. The extra (marginal) cost of one more passenger is tiny, mostly a bit of fuel and a drink. Any price above that small marginal cost adds to profit, so $90 beats an empty seat. The same logic drives last-minute hotel rooms and standby tickets.

3. Two coffee shops both have a slow month. One rents its space; the other owns the building outright with no mortgage. Which one is under more pressure to stay open day to day?

Short Answer

Neither faces more pressure from the building in the short run, because rent and a paid-off building are both fixed costs that do not change with how many coffees are sold. The day-to-day decision turns on variable costs: as long as a day's sales cover that day's beans, milk and staff, opening beats closing. Rent matters for the longer-run question of whether to stay in business at all.

Case Study: Why an Airline Seat Costs so Little to Fill

In 2019, US passenger airlines spent their money roughly like this: labor was the single biggest operating cost at about 32%, and fuel next at about 18%. Crucially, roughly two-thirds of an airline's operating costs are fixed once a flight is scheduled, the aircraft, the gates, most of the crew and ground staff. That is why the cost of carrying one extra passenger on a flight that is already leaving is very small, little more than a bit of extra fuel and a drink.

That cost structure shapes how airlines behave. Because so much is fixed, an airline has to fill a large share of its seats just to break even; the rule of thumb is around 65% of seats occupied on average, and modern carriers run fuller than that. One illustration puts a transcontinental flight at roughly $300 per seat just to cover costs. Below that break-even load the flight loses money, but once it is flying, a half-empty cabin is pure waste, so carriers discount hard, overbook, and sell standby seats cheap: a money-losing fare beats no fare at all on a seat that is about to fly empty.

The same math produced one of the stranger episodes of the pandemic. European "use it or lose it" rules let airlines keep valuable takeoff and landing slots only if they actually fly them. When demand collapsed in 2020, regulators temporarily waived the rule, but as it tightened again Lufthansa's chief said the airline expected to run around 18,000 barely booked flights over the winter of 2021-22 simply to hold onto its slots. (Airlines and regulators argued over how empty those flights really were.) The slot, a fixed asset worth protecting, was valuable enough that flying near-empty planes looked cheaper than losing it.

Sources: Airlines for America cost data via Geography of Transport Systems, Lufthansa "ghost flights", Reuters/CNN coverage summarized, Fortune on 2020 slot waiver.

The Takeaway

Sort your costs before you decide. For "should I do one more?" look only at the marginal cost and ignore what is already spent, which is why airlines, hotels and software firms sell spare capacity cheap. For "should I keep doing this at all?" the fixed costs come back into the picture. And whenever you catch yourself spending more because you have "already put so much in," stop: that money is sunk, and the only thing that matters is what happens next.
