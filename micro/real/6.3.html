Asymmetric Information

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Markets work smoothly when both sides know what they are dealing with. When one side knows much more than the other, whether it is the used-car seller who knows the car is a dud or the driver who knows they are reckless, the better-informed side can take advantage, and sometimes the market gets so distorted that good deals stop happening at all.

Key Terms

  • Asymmetric Information: One party to a deal knows something important that the other does not. The seller usually knows more about the product; the buyer of insurance usually knows more about their own risks.
  • Adverse Selection: The trouble that comes from hidden information before a deal. The people most eager to make a deal are often the ones you least want: the sickest buy the most insurance, the worst cars are the ones owners most want to sell. The informed side "selects" into the market in a way that works against the other side.
  • Moral Hazard: The trouble that comes from hidden behavior after a deal. Being protected changes how carefully people act. An insured driver may park more carelessly; a company bailed out once may take bigger risks next time. The behavior is hidden from whoever bears the cost.
  • Signaling: When the informed side proves its quality in a way that is hard to fake. A used-car dealer offering a warranty is signaling the car is sound, because only someone confident in the car would offer to cover repairs.
  • Screening: When the uninformed side sets up choices that make the other side reveal itself. An insurer offering a cheap high-deductible plan alongside a pricey low-deductible one lets low-risk and high-risk customers sort themselves out by which they pick.

The Big Picture

Asymmetric information is the hidden theme behind a surprising amount of everyday friction: why used cars are worth so much less the moment you drive them off the lot, why insurers ask so many questions, why employers care about diplomas, why a contractor's reviews matter so much. In each case, one side is trying to figure out what the other side already knows. Warranties, inspections, credit scores, professional licenses, and online ratings exist mainly to close that information gap so that trade can happen at all.

Think About It

1. Two people apply for the same all-you-can-eat buffet membership: a light eater and someone with an enormous appetite. Which is more likely to sign up, and what is that an example of?

Short Answer

The big eater, who gets more for the fixed price. That is adverse selection: the customers most drawn to a flat price are the ones who cost the business the most. It is the same reason the sickest people are the keenest to buy generous health insurance.

2. After buying comprehensive travel insurance that covers any injury, a tourist decides to try a risky cliff hike they would otherwise have skipped. What is this, and who ends up paying for the extra risk?

Short Answer

Moral hazard: being insured changed the tourist's behavior toward more risk, which the insurer cannot easily observe. The cost of that extra risk is spread across all the insurer's customers through higher premiums.

3. You are selling a genuinely excellent used car, but buyers assume it might be a dud and offer you a low, "average" price. What can you do to get the price your car deserves?

Short Answer

Signal the quality in a way a seller of a bad car could not afford to copy: offer a warranty, pay for an independent inspection, or share a full service history. By credibly sharing what you know, you close the information gap and can command a higher price.

Case Study: The Market for "Lemons" (Akerlof, 1970)

In 1970 a young economist named George Akerlof published a short paper using the used-car market to make a startling point. Suppose half the used cars are good and half are "lemons" (American slang for a defective car), and sellers know which is which but buyers cannot tell them apart. Buyers, unwilling to risk overpaying, will only offer a price somewhere in the middle. At that middling price, owners of genuinely good cars feel shortchanged and keep their cars, while owners of lemons are happy to sell. So the share of lemons on the market rises, buyers notice and offer even less, which drives still more good cars away. In the extreme, the good cars vanish and the market can collapse entirely, even though good cars and willing buyers both exist.

The idea was so unfamiliar that the paper was rejected by several leading journals, one reportedly dismissing the topic as trivial and another arguing that if it were true, almost no goods could be traded. It was eventually published and became one of the most cited papers in economics. In 2001 Akerlof shared the Nobel Prize in Economics with Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz for the study of markets with asymmetric information.

Why It Matters Outside Car Lots: The "lemons" logic appears wherever quality is hidden and hard to verify: freelance hiring, online marketplaces, even the job market (where it helps explain why employers lean on credentials). It also explains why we have so many trust-building devices, from carfax reports and eBay ratings to professional licenses. They exist to stop good products from being chased out by bad ones. Note that "lemon laws," the consumer statutes that let buyers return defective cars, borrow their nickname from this very idea.

Sources: The Market for Lemons (Wikipedia), Akerlof's personal essay on writing the paper.

Case Study: Insurance, Moral Hazard and the RAND Experiment

Insurance is where both problems show up most clearly. Adverse selection is why insurers screen applicants: the people most eager to buy a policy tend to be the ones most likely to use it. Moral hazard is why nearly every policy has a deductible or co-pay: once you are covered, you have less reason to be careful or frugal, and the insurer cannot watch your every move.

The best evidence on health-care moral hazard comes from the RAND Health Insurance Experiment. Run from 1974 to 1982, this was one of the largest controlled social experiments ever conducted. Thousands of families were randomly assigned to insurance plans ranging from fully free care to plans with substantial out-of-pocket costs. The finding, still central to the field, was that people who faced lower out-of-pocket costs used noticeably more medical care. Importantly, they used more of both the care that helped and the care that did not, and for most participants health outcomes were about the same either way, though there were exceptions among the poorest and sickest patients. (Researchers still debate the exact size of the effect, partly because some families dropped out of the study, but the direction, lower cost means more use, is widely accepted.)

Why It Matters Outside Health Care: This is why your car insurance has a deductible, why your phone's "accidental damage" plan still makes you pay something, and why "free" is rarely offered for things people can overuse. Cost-sharing is a deliberate design to counter moral hazard: it keeps you with a little skin in the game so that being insured does not completely remove your reason to be careful.

Sources: RAND Health Insurance Experiment overview (NBER working paper), Summary of RAND findings (PubMed Central).

A Contested Corner: Health Insurance Design

How to handle adverse selection in health insurance is genuinely contested, because the obvious fixes all involve trade-offs. If insurers are free to charge sick people more or turn them away, they avoid being swamped by high-cost customers, but the people who most need coverage may be unable to afford it. If insurers are required to cover everyone at similar prices, coverage is broader, but healthy people may wait until they are sick to sign up, pushing premiums up for everyone. This dynamic is sometimes called a "death spiral". Different countries strike this balance in different ways: mandates to buy in, government-provided coverage, subsidies, or tightly regulated private markets. Economists largely agree on the underlying problem; the disagreements are about which remedy is worth its costs.

Sources: Einav and Finkelstein, Selection in Insurance Markets (NBER working paper), AEA listing, Journal of Economic Perspectives 25(1).

The Takeaway

When one side of a deal knows more than the other, two things can go wrong: the wrong people are drawn in before the deal (adverse selection) or people behave worse after it (moral hazard). The market's own answers (warranties and inspections, deductibles and co-pays, and looking at applicant credentials) are all attempts to share or offset hidden information. When those are not enough, as often happens in insurance, governments step in, and reasonable people disagree about how.
