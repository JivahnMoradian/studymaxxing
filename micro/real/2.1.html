Demand, Supply & Equilibrium

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

A price is the meeting point between what buyers want and what sellers will provide. When something changes on either side, the price moves, and the moving price is what quietly pushes the market back into balance.

Key Terms

  • Demand: How much of something buyers want to buy at each price. As a rule, the cheaper it is, the more people buy; the pricier it is, the less.
  • Supply: How much of something sellers want to sell at each price. Usually the higher the price, the more sellers are willing to produce and offer.
  • Equilibrium Price: The price at which the amount buyers want exactly matches the amount sellers offer, so the market "clears" with no leftover and no waiting list.
  • Shortage vs. Surplus: A shortage is when the price is too low, so buyers want more than is available (empty shelves, waiting lists). A Surplus is when the price is too high, so sellers are stuck with unsold goods. Shortage means not enough to go around; surplus means too much.
  • A Change in Price vs. a Change in Demand: If a coffee gets pricier and you buy less, that is you sliding along the same demand. If something else changes (your income, a health scare, the price of tea) and you would now buy a different amount at every price, that is demand itself moving. The distinction sounds fussy but it is the heart of reading any price story in the news.

The Big Picture: Prices Are Signals, Not Commands

Nobody sits in an office deciding how many avocados America needs this week. Yet avocados mostly show up, in roughly the right amount, at a price that clears. That happens because a price does two jobs at once: a high price tells buyers to ease off and tells sellers to produce more, while a low price does the reverse. When a hurricane wipes out a crop or a product suddenly goes viral, the price jumps or drops, and that single number ripples out to millions of strangers who never coordinate with each other. Economists since Adam Smith have called this the "invisible hand." It is not magic and it is not always fair, but it is why most things are usually in stock.

Think About It

1. A concert sells out in minutes at $100 a ticket, and the same seats instantly appear on resale sites for $600. What does that tell you?

Short Answer

The original $100 price was set below the equilibrium price, so far more people wanted tickets than there were seats, which is a shortage. The $600 resale price is roughly where demand and the fixed supply of seats actually meet. The gap is exactly why scalping exists.

2. A snowstorm is forecast and grocery shelves empty out even though the store did not change its prices. Why the empty shelves?

Short Answer

Expecting to be stuck at home, people suddenly want to buy much more right now, so demand jumps. Because the store held its price steady instead of raising it, the amount people want outruns what is on the shelf, and you get a shortage rather than a higher price.

3. Your town gets a second coffee shop. What happens to the price of a latte, all else equal?

Short Answer

More sellers means more supply, which tends to push the price down and the quantity sold up. This is why towns with one gas station often have higher prices than towns with five.

Case Study: Uber's Surge Pricing

When a rainstorm hits downtown at rush hour, lots of people open the Uber app at once while few drivers are out. Rather than leave riders staring at a "no cars available" screen, Uber raises the price automatically. Uber describes surge pricing as a tool that kicks in when there are more riders in an area than available drivers, nudging both sides of the market: the higher fare signals drivers to head toward the busy zone, and some riders decide to wait, walk, or take the bus instead. As the two sides rebalance, the surge fades.

This is a textbook market rebalancing itself through price, done by software in real time. It is also genuinely unpopular, and the economics does not settle the argument. Independent researchers (Chen, Mislove and Wilson, 2015) found surge pricing really does respond to supply and demand, but not always the way Uber intends, and they called the underlying algorithm a "black box" that outsiders cannot verify.

Why It Matters Outside Ride-Hailing: Surge pricing is just a visible, fast version of what every market does slowly. Hotel rooms cost more on big event weekends, flights cost more at the holidays, and electricity in some regions costs more at 6 p.m. than at 3 a.m. The price rises when many people want the same limited thing at the same time, and that higher price is doing a job: steering the scarce supply toward whoever values it most and coaxing out more of it.

Sources: Uber on how surge pricing works, Quartz on Uber's economists, Christian Science Monitor on the surge-pricing study.

Case Study: The Used-Car Price Spike, 2021-2022

Normally a used car loses value the moment you drive it off the lot. For about a year, that reversed, and some people sold a used car for close to what they paid. The cause was a classic supply shock colliding with strong demand. A global shortage of semiconductor chips choked new-car production in 2021, so buyers who could not get a new car turned to used ones, while the usual flow of trade-ins and off-lease vehicles dried up. Supply fell and demand rose at the same time, and prices climbed hard.

The official consumer price index for used cars and trucks rose roughly 43% between February 2020 and mid-2021, and its year-over-year increase peaked around 35% in early 2022 before reversing as supply recovered. On the wholesale side, Cox Automotive's Manheim index hit records in 2021, up more than 50% from its pandemic low. By late 2022 and into 2023, as chip supplies and production normalized, used-car prices fell back.

Why It Matters Outside Car Lots: This is what a "supply shock" looks like in your own life. When one input (here, chips) becomes scarce, the shortage spreads to every product that needs it, prices jump, and the jump is temporary only if supply can recover. The same pattern drove lumber, bicycle, and appliance prices in the same period. If you are buying during a shock, you are paying the scarcity; if you can wait, prices usually ease once supply catches up.

Sources: BLS "consumer prices up" release, FRED used-cars CPI series, Cox Automotive / Manheim on the chip shortage.

Case Study: Egg Prices and Bird Flu, 2022-2025

Eggs are a good demand-and-supply story because demand for them barely changes (people want their usual eggs almost regardless of price) while supply can collapse suddenly. Starting in 2022, a severe outbreak of avian influenza forced farmers to cull tens of millions of egg-laying hens. Fewer hens meant far fewer eggs, so supply dropped sharply while demand held roughly steady, and the price soared.

By January 2025 the wholesale price for Midwest large eggs reached about $6.07 a dozen, beating the previous record of about $5.46 set in December 2022, according to price-reporting service Expana, which tied the spike mainly to bird flu. At the retail level, the average U.S. price of a dozen Grade A eggs hit about $5.90 in February 2025, per the Consumer Price Index, roughly double the typical pre-outbreak level. As outbreaks eased later in 2025, wholesale prices began falling back.

Why It Matters Outside the Grocery Aisle: When demand for something is steady but hard to cut back on, even a modest drop in supply can send the price flying, because buyers keep trying to buy their usual amount. That is why disruptions to staples (eggs, onions in India, coffee after a frost) produce dramatic price spikes and angry headlines, while the same-size disruption to a thing people can easily skip barely moves its price.

Sources: Axios on 2025 egg prices and bird flu, Associated Press / IBJ on USDA record egg prices.

The Takeaway

Almost every price in your life is a demand-and-supply story. When a price jumps or an item vanishes from the shelf, ask two questions: did something change on the buyers' side (income, tastes, a scare, a fad) or the sellers' side (costs, a disaster, new competition)? And is the price being allowed to move, or is something holding it fixed and creating a shortage or a glut instead? Those two questions explain surge fares, sold-out concerts, gas-price swings, and the eggs in your fridge.
