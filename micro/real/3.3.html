Profit Maximization

Real-World Relevance: Medium
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

A business can show a healthy profit on paper and still be a money-losing idea, because the honest profit question subtracts what you gave up to do it, not just the checks you wrote.

Key Terms

  • Explicit Costs: The out-of-pocket payments a business actually makes: wages, rent, materials, utilities. These are what an accountant records.
  • Implicit Costs: The value of things the owner already owns and pours into the business instead of using elsewhere, above all their own time and savings. No check is written, but the opportunity is real and lost.
  • Accounting Profit vs. Economic Profit: Accounting profit is revenue minus the explicit (out-of-pocket) costs, the number on a tax return. Economic profit subtracts the implicit costs too, so it is always the smaller of the two and is the one that tells you whether the venture truly beats your next-best alternative.
  • Normal Profit: The break-even line for economic profit. A business earning zero economic profit is not failing; it is covering every cost including the owner's opportunity cost, so the owner is doing exactly as well as they would have elsewhere.
  • Marginal Revenue vs. Marginal Cost: Marginal revenue is the money one more sale brings in; marginal cost is what that sale costs to make. The profit-maximizing rule is to keep going as long as the next sale brings in at least what it costs, and stop when they are equal.

Think About It

1. You quit a $70,000-a-year job to run your own shop. After a year the shop's revenue beats its bills by $55,000. Did you come out ahead?

Short Answer

On paper yes, a $55,000 accounting profit. Economically, no: you gave up a $70,000 salary to earn it, so your economic profit is about $55,000 minus $70,000, a $15,000 loss. The shop would need to clear more than your old salary just to break even in the sense that matters. This is the single most useful idea in the chapter.

2. An economist says a profitable family restaurant is earning "zero economic profit." Is that an insult?

Short Answer

No. Zero economic profit, called a normal profit, means the business covers all its bills and pays the owners as well as their next-best option would, the salary they could earn elsewhere and a fair return on the money they tied up. It is a perfectly healthy, sustainable business; it just is not beating the alternatives, which is a high bar.

3. A café owner is deciding whether to stay open an extra hour each evening. What two numbers should she compare?

Short Answer

The marginal revenue of that hour (the extra sales it brings in) against its marginal cost (the extra wages, electricity and ingredients). If the hour brings in more than it costs, keep it; if it costs more than it earns, drop it. Whether the café is profitable overall does not change this hour-by-hour call.

Case Study: Does Working for Yourself Pay?

Running your own business is the clearest place the gap between accounting profit and economic profit shows up, because the owner's own time is the biggest hidden cost. The economist Barton Hamilton studied this directly. Using US data, he found that people who stayed self-employed for ten years earned, at the median, roughly 35% less than they would have made as employees over the same period, even after accounting for differences in skill. On paper many of those businesses were "profitable"; measured against the salary the owners gave up, a large share were not.

Why do people do it anyway? Hamilton's reading was that the non-money rewards, being your own boss, controlling your own schedule, building something, are worth a lot to many people, enough to accept lower pay. In the language of this chapter, owners are willingly running at a negative economic profit because they value the job itself. The finding is debated: later researchers using differently measured earnings have found cases where entrepreneurship pays a premium, and the answer depends heavily on the type of business (solo operators tend to fare worse than those who build firms with employees). Estimates vary, but the core lesson is robust.

Why It Matters Outside Startups: Any time you put your own unpaid time or your own savings into something, a side business, a rental property, fixing up a house to flip, there is an implicit cost equal to what that time and money could have earned elsewhere. Counting only the cash you spend flatters every such project. The real test is whether it beats what you gave up.

Sources: Hamilton (2000), "Does Entrepreneurship Pay?" (listing), Review of returns to self-employment, Springer.

The Takeaway

Before you call something profitable, ask what you gave up to do it: the salary you did not earn, the interest your savings did not make, the time you did not spend elsewhere. Subtract that, and you get economic profit, the number that actually tells you whether to keep going. Zero economic profit is fine; it means you are doing as well as your best alternative. A positive accounting profit that hides a negative economic one is the quiet way good-looking ventures lose money.
