Income Inequality

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Income inequality is about how the economic pie is sliced, not how big it is. A market can be perfectly efficient and still leave a huge gap between the top and the bottom, and how much a society should do about that gap is one of the oldest arguments in politics.

Key Terms

  • Income Inequality: The degree to which income is spread unevenly across people or households. It is a separate question from how rich a country is on average; two countries with the same average income can have very different gaps between rich and poor.
  • Lorenz Curve: A graph that lines everyone up from poorest to richest and plots what share of total income the bottom X percent earn. If the bottom 40 percent earned exactly 40 percent of income, the line would be a straight diagonal (perfect equality). The more the curve sags below that diagonal, the more unequal the distribution.
  • Gini Coefficient: The Lorenz curve boiled down to a single number between 0 and 1. Zero means everyone has identical income; 1 means one person has everything. Higher means more unequal. Most countries land somewhere between about 0.25 and 0.65.
  • Progressive Tax: One where higher earners pay a larger share of their income (a graduated income tax). It narrows the gap. The opposite, a regressive tax, takes a bigger share from lower earners (a sales tax does this, because the poor spend more of what they earn).
  • Transfers: Payments from government to households, such as pensions, unemployment benefits, food assistance and child benefits. Together with progressive taxes, transfers are the main tool governments use to reduce inequality.
  • Market Income vs. Disposable Income: Market income is what you earn before the government touches it (wages, interest, business profit). Disposable income is what you actually have to spend after taxes are taken out and transfers are added in. Inequality is almost always lower measured on disposable income, and the gap between the two measures is exactly how much redistribution a country does.

The Big Picture: Taxes and Transfers Close Part of the Gap

Across rich countries, the market on its own produces a lot of inequality, and taxes and transfers then shrink it. Averaged across the OECD group of mostly wealthy nations in 2021, the Gini coefficient was about 0.46 before taxes and transfers and about 0.32 after. In other words, the tax-and-benefit system closed roughly a third of the gap that the market produced.

How much each country closes the gap varies a lot. Countries differ far more in disposable-income inequality than in market-income inequality, which tells you the differences come mostly from policy, not from the raw labor market. The United States, for example, has market-income inequality close to the rich-country average but ends up with the highest disposable-income inequality among high-income OECD members, because it redistributes less than most. At the other end, countries like the Slovak Republic and the Nordic nations end up among the most equal. The widest gaps in the world are elsewhere: the World Bank has repeatedly ranked South Africa as the most unequal country it measures, with a Gini around 0.63.

Sources: OECD Government at a Glance 2025, poverty and inequality, PIIE on US redistribution vs OECD, World Bank on South Africa.

Think About It

1. A Gini coefficient of 0 and a Gini of 1: what does each describe? Which of the two could actually exist in a real country?

Short Answer

Zero means everyone has exactly the same income (perfect equality); 1 means a single person has all the income and everyone else has nothing (perfect inequality). Neither extreme exists in reality; real countries cluster roughly between 0.25 and 0.65. The number is only useful for comparing places and tracking change over time.

2. A country's Gini measured on market income is 0.47, but on disposable income it is 0.30. What does the difference tell you?

Short Answer

That the country's taxes and transfers do a lot of redistribution, closing much of the gap the market produced. A country where the two numbers are close does little redistribution; a wide gap between them means a large tax-and-benefit system.

3. A sales tax applies the same 8 percent rate to everyone. Why do economists call it regressive even though the rate is identical for rich and poor?

Short Answer

Because lower-income households spend a larger share of their income (and save less), the tax eats up a bigger percentage of what they earn, even at the same rate. "Progressive" and "regressive" are about the share of income paid, not the headline rate.

Case Study: The Same Numbers, Two Honest Stories

Inequality statistics are unusually easy to argue about because the same figure genuinely supports more than one reading. Take a widely cited fact: in the United States, the share of all income going to the top 1 percent roughly doubled over the past few decades, and wealth is even more concentrated than income. Everyone looking at the data sees the same rise. What they disagree about is what it means and what to do.

One view emphasizes fairness and opportunity: when a large share of gains flows to the very top, people in the middle and bottom can feel the economy is not working for them, and concentrated wealth can translate into concentrated political power. On this reading, more progressive taxes and stronger transfers are a reasonable response. A competing view emphasizes efficiency and incentives: some inequality is the natural reward for skill, effort, risk-taking and innovation, and high taxes on top earners or large transfers can blunt the incentive to work, save and invest, and may push talent or capital elsewhere. On this reading, the cure can cost more than the disease.

The economist Arthur Okun captured the tension with the image of a "leaky bucket": moving income from rich to poor is like carrying water in a bucket with holes, because some is lost along the way to administration and to weakened incentives. The honest debate is about how leaky the bucket really is, which is an empirical question economists still study, and about how much leakage is worth accepting for a more equal result, which is a values question that economics alone cannot settle.

Why It Matters Outside the Statistics: When you see an inequality number used in an argument, the number itself is usually not in dispute. What is in dispute is the comparison (before or after taxes? income or wealth? which years?) and the values you bring to it. Knowing that lets you separate the factual claim from the political one.

Sources: CBO, The Distribution of Household Income, 2023, CBPP summary of CBO data on the top 1 percent's income share.

The Takeaway

The Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient are just tools for measuring how unevenly income is shared, and they show two consistent facts: markets alone produce substantial inequality, and taxes and transfers reduce it, by very different amounts in different countries. Beyond the measurement, almost everything is contested. How much inequality is too much, and how hard a government should work to shrink it, are questions about fairness and incentives where reasonable people, and economists, genuinely disagree. This page lays out the tools and the trade-off; it does not tell you where to land.
