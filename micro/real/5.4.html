5.4

Monopsony

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

When one employer (or a few) dominate the hiring for a kind of work, they can pay less than workers are worth, because the workers have nowhere else to go. This is why a dominant local employer can hold wages down, and why a well-set minimum wage can sometimes raise both pay and jobs.

Key Terms

  • Monopsony: A market with one dominant buyer. A monopoly is a single seller with power over its selling price; a monopsony is a single buyer, here a buyer of labor, with power over the wage it pays. The classic picture is the one big employer in a town, but it also arises for specialized workers who have few places to take their skills.
  • Marginal Factor Cost: The true cost of hiring one more worker. If attracting an extra worker means raising the wage, and the employer has to pay that higher wage to everyone already on staff, the real cost of that one hire is more than their wage. So a dominant employer holds back on hiring to keep the wage down.
  • The Wage Gap: A monopsony employer ends up paying less than a worker's marginal revenue product, the revenue that worker generates (see 5.1). In a competitive market, pay is bid up to that value; under monopsony, the employer keeps the difference.
  • Labor Market Concentration: A measure of how few employers do the hiring in a place and occupation. The more concentrated the market, and the harder it is for workers to move, the more wage-setting power employers have.
  • The Minimum-Wage Twist: Because a monopsony holds wages and hiring below the competitive level, a minimum wage set in the right range can push the wage up and lead the employer to hire more, the opposite of the "price floor cuts jobs" story. This is a big reason the minimum-wage debate is unsettled.

Think About It

1. You live in a town where one hospital, one meatpacking plant, or one university is by far the largest employer of your kind of work. How does that affect your pay?

Short Answer

It tends to lower it. With few rival bidders for your skills, and the cost and disruption of moving away, you cannot easily credibly threaten to leave, so the employer can pay below what your work is worth. The fewer your outside options, the larger that gap.

2. Why do non-compete clauses and "no-poach" agreements between employers matter for wages?

Short Answer

They shrink workers' outside options, which is exactly what gives an employer wage-setting power. A non-compete stops you taking your skills to a rival; a no-poach deal means rivals agree not to bid for each other's staff. Both make a labor market behave more like a monopsony, holding pay down.

3. Standard supply and demand says a minimum wage above the market rate should cut jobs. Why do economists argue about whether that is true?

Short Answer

Because many real labor markets are not perfectly competitive. Where employers have monopsony power, they already hold wages and hiring below the competitive level, so a minimum wage set in the right range can raise pay without cutting jobs, and may even raise them. How common and how strong this effect is in practice is genuinely disputed.

Case Study: Are Nurses a Monopsony?

Nursing is the textbook example of monopsony. In many towns one or two hospital systems do nearly all the hiring of registered nurses, nurses often cannot easily relocate, and the skills are specialized, exactly the conditions that give an employer power over wages. An influential 1989 analysis treated nursing as the standard case of monopsony in the labor market, and the idea has been studied ever since.

But the evidence is genuinely mixed, which is itself worth knowing. A 2005 study of 240 US labor markets by Barry Hirsch and Edward Schumacher found only weak and inconsistent signs of monopsony and concluded that nursing should not be treated as a clear-cut example. More recent work points the other way in some settings: a 2023 study found that where hospital systems were more consolidated, nurse wage growth was slower, but mainly in smaller metro areas, and a separate analysis estimated that registered nurses in highly concentrated markets earn meaningfully less, on the order of a few thousand dollars a year, than those in markets with more employers. Taken together, the research suggests employer power can depress nurse pay in some places, especially smaller markets, but it is not a universal law.

Why It Matters Outside Nursing: The same logic applies wherever your options are thin. Specialized skills, a thin local job market, visa ties, or contract clauses that bind you to one employer all hand bargaining power to the buyer of your labor. If you want to be paid closer to what your work is worth, widening your set of credible options (through mobility, portable skills, or remote work) is often worth more than asking nicely.

Sources: Sullivan, NBER Working Paper 3031 (1989) on monopsony power over nurse wages, Hirsch and Schumacher, "Classic or New Monopsony? Searching for Evidence in Nursing Labor Markets", Allegretto and Graham-Squire, hospital consolidation and nurse wage growth (2023), UCLA Anderson Review summary of concentration and nurse pay.

Case Study: The Minimum Wage, Still an Open Question

In 1992 New Jersey raised its minimum wage from $4.25 to $5.05 an hour. The economists David Card and Alan Krueger surveyed fast-food restaurants in New Jersey and just across the border in Pennsylvania, which had not raised its minimum, and compared how employment changed. Their 1994 study found no drop in employment in New Jersey relative to Pennsylvania; if anything, employment held up or rose slightly. This was striking because the simple supply-and-demand model predicts that a wage floor above the market rate should cut jobs, and the monopsony model is one explanation for why it might not.

The study did not settle the question; it opened a long argument that is still live. David Neumark and William Wascher reanalyzed the episode using payroll records rather than survey responses and reported a modest employment decline in New Jersey, roughly in the range the standard model predicts. Later replications and newer natural experiments have landed on both sides. The honest summary is that the employment effect of a minimum wage depends on how high it is set relative to local wages and how much employer power exists in that market, and reasonable economists still disagree. In a 2016 survey of leading economists on a proposed $15 federal minimum wage, only about a quarter thought it would substantially lower employment, while nearly 40% were unsure, a rare public display of professional uncertainty.

Why It Matters Outside the Minimum-Wage Fight: Be suspicious of anyone who tells you the answer is simple in either direction. Whether a wage floor costs jobs is an empirical question whose answer changes with the number, the place, and the degree of employer power, which is exactly why it is one of the most studied and most contested questions in economics. Supporters emphasize higher pay for low-wage workers and the monopsony case; critics emphasize possible job losses and harm to small businesses. Both point to real effects.

Sources: Card and Krueger, "Minimum Wages and Employment: A Case Study of the Fast-Food Industry" (1994), Neumark and Wascher reanalysis (working paper), IGM Economic Experts Panel on a $15 minimum wage, Chicago Booth Review summary of the economists' poll.

The Takeaway

Competitive markets bid wages up toward the value a worker produces. Monopsony is what happens when that bidding is weak: one or a few employers, few outside options for workers, and pay that sits below the value of the work. It explains a lot, from why company towns tend to underpay workers to why non-competes matter, and it is the reason the minimum-wage debate is not as one-sided as a first economics lesson suggests.
