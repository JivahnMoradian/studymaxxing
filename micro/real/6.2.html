6.2

Public Goods & Common Resources

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Some things are hard to sell by the unit because you cannot easily charge the people who use them, or because one person's use does not use them up. These two quirks explain why the government builds roads and armies, why open-source software is free, and why the world keeps running out of fish.

Key Terms

  • Rival: My using it leaves less for you. A sandwich is rival; if I eat it, you cannot. A radio broadcast is not rival; my listening does not reduce your reception.
  • Excludable: It is practical to stop people who do not pay from using it. A cinema seat is excludable (you need a ticket); the open ocean's fish are not.
  • Public Good: Non-rival and non-excludable at once. National defense is the classic case: it protects everyone in the country whether or not they chip in, and one person being defended does not reduce anyone else's protection.
  • Common-Pool Resource: Rival but non-excludable, the dangerous combination. Ocean fish, groundwater and public grazing land are used up as people take them, yet no one can easily be kept out. Everyone has a reason to grab their share before someone else does.
  • Free-Rider Problem: When you cannot be excluded, you have little reason to pay. If enough people reason this way, no private company can make money providing the good, so it does not get made. This is why public goods usually have to be paid for through taxes.
  • Tragedy of the Commons: When a shared resource has no gatekeeper, each user takes as much as they can because the cost of overuse is spread across everyone, while the benefit of grabbing more is all theirs. The predictable result is that the resource gets exhausted.

The Big Picture

The textbook example of a public good used to be the lighthouse: ships benefit from the light whether or not they pay, and one ship seeing it does not dim it for another, so in theory no private owner would build one. (In practice, as the economist Ronald Coase pointed out, early English lighthouses were funded by charging ships port fees, a reminder that clever arrangements can sometimes get around the problem.) The deeper point is that markets are brilliant at handling ordinary private goods and clumsy with these two awkward categories. Knowing which category something falls into tells you, fairly reliably, whether you should expect the market to handle it or expect a fight over who pays and who gets to use it.

Think About It

1. A fireworks display lights up the whole town. The organizer sells tickets to a park, but anyone can watch from their own backyard. What kind of good is the show, and what is the organizer's problem?

Short Answer

The show is close to a public good: non-rival (your watching does not block mine) and hard to exclude (backyard viewers pay nothing). The organizer faces the free-rider problem, so ticket sales alone may not cover the cost, which is why fireworks are usually paid for by a town or a sponsor rather than sold.

2. A town shares one aquifer for its wells. Each farmer can pump as much as they like for free. What is likely to happen to the water table, and why?

Short Answer

It will probably fall too fast. The water is rival (what one farmer pumps is gone) but non-excludable (no one is stopped), so each farmer races to pump before the others do and no one accounts for the shared cost of draining it. That is the tragedy of the commons, and it is why groundwater is so often regulated with permits or limits.

3. Why is it that private companies happily sell you a loaf of bread but rarely sell you clean air or an unclogged highway?

Short Answer

Bread is a private good: rival and excludable, so a baker can charge you and the deal works. Clean air and open roads are non-excludable, so a company cannot easily charge the people who benefit and cannot make money providing them. That gap is exactly where governments and shared rules tend to step in.

Case Study: The Grand Banks Cod Collapse (1992)

For roughly five centuries, the cold waters off Newfoundland held one of the richest fisheries on Earth. Northern cod were so abundant that early explorers wrote of scooping them up in baskets. The fish were a classic common-pool resource: rival (a cod caught by one boat is gone) and non-excludable (the sea was open to all comers). As bigger trawlers and better technology arrived through the twentieth century, catches climbed, then the stock began to crash.

By 1992 the spawning population had fallen to an estimated 1 percent of its historic peak. On July 2, 1992, the Canadian government shut the fishery down entirely. The moratorium was meant to last two years; the cod did not come back on schedule, and it stretched on for decades. The economic blow was enormous: estimates put the jobs lost at around 30,000 in Newfoundland and Labrador, and roughly 35,000 to 40,000 fishers and processing workers across the region. Whole coastal towns emptied out as people left to find work, and the province's population fell about 10 percent over the following decade.

The stock has only partly recovered. After signs of rebuilding, Canada formally reopened a commercial northern cod fishery in 2024, more than thirty years after the ban, with modest quotas that some scientists still consider risky. The exact causes remain debated: the government has emphasized natural factors such as cold water and changing predators, while many researchers put more weight on decades of overfishing.

Why It Matters Outside Newfoundland: This is the tragedy of the commons at full scale, with real towns and real lives. No single boat set out to destroy the fishery; each simply took what it could, as anyone would when the alternative is watching someone else take it. The same logic threatens other fisheries, groundwater and forests today, and the usual fixes (enforceable catch limits, tradable quotas, or clear ownership) all work by giving someone a reason to care about the resource lasting.

Sources: Cod Moratorium of 1992 (Canadian Encyclopedia), Canada ends cod moratorium after 30+ years.

Case Study: The Commons Are Not Always Doomed (Elinor Ostrom)

The word "tragedy" makes overuse sound inevitable, but it is not. The political economist Elinor Ostrom spent decades documenting communities that managed shared resources for generations without either government control or private ownership: Swiss alpine pastures, Japanese village forests, Spanish irrigation districts, fishing grounds in Turkey. For this work she won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2009, the first woman to do so.

Studying hundreds of cases, Ostrom found that the lasting arrangements shared certain features: the group had clear boundaries about who was in and who was out, the rules fit local conditions, the people affected helped make the rules, cheating was monitored and punished with escalating penalties, and there were cheap ways to settle disputes. Where those features were missing, systems tended to collapse; where they were present, communities could keep a commons healthy for centuries.

Why It Matters Outside Academia: Ostrom's lesson is that a shared resource has three possible fates, not two. It can be ruined (the open-access tragedy), privatized, or governed by its own users under good rules. Any shared thing you deal with, from an office kitchen to a neighborhood parking scheme to a group project, tends to survive on the same ingredients: clear membership, rules people had a hand in, and real consequences for the person who takes more than their share.

Sources: 6 design principles to sustain the commons.

A Modern Public Good: Open-Source Software

Much of the internet runs on software that is free to copy and that no one can be charged for: the Linux operating system, web servers, the libraries bundled inside almost every app and website. This is about as pure a public good as the modern economy has. One company using an open-source library does not use it up (non-rival), and the code is published for anyone to take (non-excludable).

A 2024 Harvard Business School working paper tried to put a number on it. The researchers estimated that it would cost about 4 billion dollars to recreate the most widely used open-source software just once, but that the value companies get from using it instead of building their own is on the order of 8.8 trillion dollars. (That larger figure is a hypothetical: it asks what firms would have to spend if open source vanished and they each had to rebuild everything in-house, so treat it as an illustration of scale rather than money actually changing hands. Not everyone accepts the method.) Strikingly, the study estimated that about 96 percent of that value came from just 5 percent of developers.

Why It Matters: This is the free-rider problem in a surprisingly happy form. Almost everyone uses open-source software; very few contribute to it, because they do not have to. It gets built anyway, through a mix of volunteers, companies that depend on it, and foundations, which is a reminder that public goods do sometimes get provided without a government, just not reliably or evenly.

Sources: The Value of Open Source Software (SSRN working paper), Simon Willison's summary.

The Takeaway

Two simple questions, "does my using it use it up?" and "can non-payers be kept out?", sort most of the economy's trickiest goods. Public goods (no and no) get underprovided because of free-riding, which is why they are usually tax-funded. Common-pool resources (yes and no) get overused because no one bears the full cost, which is why fisheries, water and forests so often need limits or shared rules. Neither outcome is a law of nature: good institutions, whether a government program or a community's own rules, can and do change the result.
