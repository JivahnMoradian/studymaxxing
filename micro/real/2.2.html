Elasticities

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Elasticity is a measure of how touchy buyers (or sellers) are about price. Some things you cut back on the instant they get pricier; others you keep buying no matter what. That difference decides who can raise prices, who gets taxed, and who gets hurt.

Key Terms

  • Elastic Demand: Buyers are sensitive to price. A small price rise drives a big drop in how much they buy, usually because there is an easy substitute or the purchase is a treat they can skip. Example: a particular brand of soda when the one next to it is cheaper.
  • Inelastic Demand: Buyers are not very sensitive to price. Even a big price rise barely changes how much they buy, usually because the thing is a necessity or has no good substitute. Example: a life-sustaining medicine, or gasoline for someone who must drive to work.
  • Why It Varies: Demand is more elastic when there are close substitutes, when the item eats up a big share of your budget, when it is a luxury rather than a necessity, and when you have more time to adjust. Demand is more inelastic in the opposite cases, and especially in the short run.
  • The Revenue Trap: Raising a price does not always raise total revenue. If demand is elastic, a higher price scares off so many buyers that revenue falls. If demand is inelastic, a higher price brings in more money because few buyers leave. This is the single most practical use of elasticity for anyone who sells anything.
  • Income Elasticity: Whether you buy more or less of something as your income grows. Restaurant meals and travel rise sharply with income (luxuries); instant noodles and bus rides can fall as income rises (people trade up).

Think About It

1. Your gym raises its monthly fee by 20% and almost nobody quits. What does that say, and should the gym have done it sooner?

Short Answer

Demand was inelastic: members were not very price-sensitive, probably out of habit and inconvenience of switching. Because few left, the price rise raised the gym's revenue, so from a pure revenue standpoint, yes, it could have charged more earlier. Gyms famously rely on members who rarely cancel.

2. Why can governments reliably raise money by taxing cigarettes, gasoline, and alcohol, but not by taxing, say, a specific brand of potato chips?

Short Answer

Cigarettes, gas, and alcohol have inelastic demand (habit, necessity, no close substitute), so a tax raises the price without scaring off many buyers, and the revenue flows in. A single chip brand has elastic demand because shoppers just grab a different bag, so a tax on it would mostly kill its sales rather than raise money.

3. Insulin and a weekend movie ticket both cost money. Which buyer is more likely to cut back when the price rises, and why?

Short Answer

The moviegoer, easily: a movie is a skippable luxury with endless substitutes (stream something, stay home), so its demand is elastic. A diabetic who needs insulin has almost no substitute and cannot simply stop, so demand is highly inelastic, which is exactly why insulin pricing became such a fierce public issue.

Case Study: Why Cigarette Taxes Work (and What They Reveal)

Governments tax cigarettes partly to raise money and partly to discourage smoking, and both goals lean on the same fact: the demand for cigarettes is inelastic, but not perfectly so. Reviews of the research put the price elasticity of demand for cigarettes at roughly -0.4 to -0.5 in wealthy countries. In plain terms, a 10% rise in price tends to cut the amount smoked by only about 4% to 5%. That stubbornness is why a cigarette tax reliably raises revenue: most smokers keep buying.

But the small response is still a response, and it is larger among two groups: younger people and lower-income smokers, who have less money to absorb the price and are more likely to quit or never start. That is the public-health logic behind tobacco taxes. Estimates vary, and some economists argue the usual figures overstate how much smoking actually falls, so the exact numbers are debated.

Why It Matters Outside Smoking: Elasticity explains who bears a tax. When a product has inelastic demand, a tax mostly lands on the buyer as a higher price, because the buyer does not walk away. When demand is elastic, sellers cannot pass the tax along without losing customers, so they eat more of it. Before you assume a "tax on companies" is paid by companies, ask how easily their customers could say no.

Sources: NBER paper on cigarette price elasticity.

Case Study: Netflix Finds the Edge of Elastic Demand, 2011

In July 2011 Netflix restructured its plans in a way that, for many customers who wanted both DVD-by-mail and streaming, raised the effective price from about $10 to about $16 a month, roughly a 60% jump. The company assumed its customers were loyal and would mostly absorb it. They were not. By late October, Netflix reported it had lost about 800,000 U.S. subscribers in a single quarter, more than it had expected, and its stock fell about 35% in a day. A plan to split off the DVD business into a separate service called "Qwikster" made the backlash worse and was quickly scrapped.

The episode is a live demonstration of elastic demand. For a discretionary subscription with easy alternatives (cancel, switch, go back to renting), a steep price rise drove a large share of customers away, which is exactly what "elastic" means. Notably, Netflix kept the higher prices, judged the long-run demand for streaming to be strong, and recovered, which shows elasticity can differ in the short run and the long run.

Why It Matters Outside Streaming: Any business weighing a price increase is really guessing at its customers' elasticity. Raise the price of something people can easily drop, and you can lose more in lost customers than you gain per sale. Raise the price of something people are hooked on or depend on, and revenue climbs. The same 60% increase that cost Netflix hundreds of thousands of subscribers would barely dent demand for a medicine or a toll road.

Sources: Christian Science Monitor on the stock drop, NPR/KUNC on the price hike and Qwikster.

The Takeaway

Elasticity is the hidden variable behind a lot of everyday money questions. It tells a company whether a price increase will make or lose money, tells a government which goods make dependable tax targets, and tells you which of your own expenses will quietly balloon because you will keep paying no matter what. When you hear about a price rise or a new tax, the useful question is not "how big is it?" but "how easily can the buyer say no?" The harder it is to say no, the more the buyer pays.
