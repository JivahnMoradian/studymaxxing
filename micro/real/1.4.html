Marginal Analysis & Consumer Choice

Real-World Relevance: High
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

Good decisions are made "at the margin": you weigh the next little bit, not the whole thing, and you ignore money already spent and gone. Two habits (think in increments, forget sunk costs) quietly fix a lot of bad reasoning.

Key Terms

  • Marginal: The effect of one more. One more slice, one more hour, one more dollar spent. Big decisions are really chains of small marginal ones, and the right question is almost always "is the next unit worth it?", not "is this thing good?".
  • Marginal Benefit vs. Marginal Cost: The value of one more unit against what that one more unit costs you. Keep going while the next unit's benefit beats its cost; stop when they meet. That simple rule quietly governs how much of almost anything you should do.
  • Diminishing Marginal Utility: Each additional unit of the same thing tends to give you less extra satisfaction than the one before. The first slice of pizza is bliss; the fifth is a chore. This is why you stop, and why you would only buy more at a lower price.
  • Sunk Cost: Money, time or effort already spent that you cannot recover whatever you do next. Because it will not change based on your decision, it should carry zero weight in that decision. Acting as if it does is the "sunk cost fallacy".

The Big Picture

People tend to think in totals ("I already paid for this", "we've come so far") when they should think about what happens next. The marginal habit cuts through it: the past is spent and gone, so the only things that matter now are the benefit and cost of the next step. It sounds obvious, yet the mistake is so common it has its own name: the sunk cost fallacy, also known as the Concorde fallacy after a famous aircraft (more on that below). It has even been measured on real theatergoers paying real money.

Think About It

1. You paid $40 for a concert ticket, and now feel sick on the night. You cannot resell it. Should the $40 affect whether you go?

Short Answer

No. The $40 is gone either way; it is a sunk cost. The only question is whether, starting from right now, a night out sick beats a night resting. If resting is better, stay home; dragging yourself out "so the money isn't wasted" just adds a miserable evening to the loss.

2. At an all-you-can-eat buffet, how much "should" you eat?

Short Answer

Keep eating only while the next plate is still enjoyable (marginal benefit positive), not until you have "gotten your money's worth". The entry fee is sunk the moment you pay it; each extra plate costs you nothing more in dollars, so the only thing to weigh is whether more food still feels good. Diminishing marginal utility usually says stop well before you feel ill.

3. You are 40 minutes into a two-hour movie and it is dull. Does "I've already watched 40 minutes" argue for staying?

Short Answer

No. The 40 minutes are gone whatever you do. Compare the next 80 minutes of a dull film against the next 80 minutes doing something you would enjoy. If leaving wins, leave. The time already spent is a reason to stop throwing good time after bad, not to continue.

Case Study: The Plane That Named a Mistake

When people describe refusing to abandon a failing project because of everything already poured into it, they often call it the "Concorde fallacy". The name comes from the supersonic airliner built jointly by Britain and France in the 1960s and 70s. Long before it entered service, it was clear the plane would never be commercially viable, the development bills ran into the billions and demand was thin, yet both governments kept funding it. A large part of the reason was precisely that so much had already been spent, and neither side wanted to be the one to write the investment off (political and legal commitments made quitting hard too).

That is the sunk cost trap in its purest form: continuing because of what is already gone, rather than deciding on what is still to come. And it is not just governments. In a well-known 1985 field experiment, researchers at the Ohio University Theater randomly sold season tickets at three prices: full price ($15), a small discount ($13), and a large discount ($8). Over the following months, the people who had paid full price went to more plays than those who got a discount, even though all of them held identical tickets. The only difference was how much each had sunk into the purchase, and that alone changed their behavior, exactly what a rational actor is "supposed" to ignore.

Why It Matters Outside Aerospace and Theaters: You feel this pull constantly. Finishing a boring book because you are halfway, staying in a dead-end commitment because of the years invested, pouring more into a failing renovation because of what it has already cost. The cure is one question: "Knowing what I know now, would I choose this starting fresh today?" If no, the money and time already gone are not a reason to continue.

Sources: Sunk cost and the Concorde fallacy (Wikipedia), Arkes and Blumer 1985 theater study (Coglode research summary).

The Takeaway

Two portable rules come out of this chapter. First, decide at the margin: weigh the next slice, hour or dollar, not the whole thing, and stop when the next unit costs more than it is worth (diminishing satisfaction means that moment comes sooner than you think). Second, ignore sunk costs: money and time already spent are gone and should never, by themselves, talk you into doing more. Together they are among the most practical ideas economics has to offer.
