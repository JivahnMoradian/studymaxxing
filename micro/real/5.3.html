5.3

Least-Cost & Profit-Max Inputs

Real-World Relevance: Low
Read the theory → Practice →

In One Line

When a business can get the same job done by people, machines, or an outside contractor, it leans toward whichever gives the most output per dollar, and shifts the mix as prices change. This is mostly a managers' tool, but it is the logic behind self-checkout, ATMs, and offshoring.

Key Terms

  • The Least-Cost Rule: To produce a given amount as cheaply as possible, a firm balances its inputs so that the last dollar spent on each (on workers, on machines, on contractors) buys the same amount of extra output. If a dollar spent on a machine produces more than a dollar spent on labor, the firm shifts toward machines until the two even out.
  • Capital-Labor Substitution: Replacing people with equipment (or the reverse). Firms do more of it when the price of one input moves: cheaper machines or rising wages push toward automation; cheap labor pushes the other way.
  • Bang per Buck, Not Price Alone: The cheapest input is not automatically the best one. A machine that costs more but produces far more per dollar can still be the economical choice, and an input is only worth adding while the extra revenue it brings in beats its price.

Think About It

1. Wages for cashiers rise while the price of self-checkout machines falls. What is a grocery chain likely to do?

Short Answer

Shift toward machines. When labor gets more expensive relative to the equipment that can substitute for it, the output-per-dollar calculation tips toward automation, so the chain installs more self-checkout lanes and hires fewer cashiers for the same volume of sales.

2. Why might a company keep workers even when a machine is cheaper per hour?

Short Answer

Because cost per hour is not the whole story. If the workers produce more or better output per dollar spent (handling odd cases, serving customers, doing things the machine cannot), their bang per buck can still be higher. Firms compare output per dollar across inputs, not just the sticker price of each.

Where You Would Run into This

You will almost never do this calculation yourself; it lives in operations and finance departments. But you meet its results constantly. When banks installed ATMs, many expected tellers to vanish. Instead the number of tellers held up and even grew for years: cheaper machines made each branch cheaper to run, so banks opened more branches, and tellers shifted from counting cash to selling loans and accounts. By one well-known account, the number of tellers per branch in urban markets fell from about 20 to 13 between 1988 and 2004, while the number of branches rose sharply. Self-checkout is a newer version of the same decision, and here the mix is tilting the other way: the US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects cashier employment to decline about 6% between 2025 and 2035, from roughly 3.1 million jobs, and attributes the drop partly to self-service checkout and online sales. The lesson for a worker is that automation does not have a fixed verdict; whether it cuts your job or reshapes it depends on whether the machine substitutes for what you do or makes you more valuable.
