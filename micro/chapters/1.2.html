UNIT 1 — BASIC ECONOMIC CONCEPTS

Opportunity Cost & the PPF

Practice these problems →

Opportunity Cost

The Opportunity Cost of a choice is the value of the next-best alternative given up. It's the real cost of any decision, and it's why scarcity forces trade-offs. Opportunity cost is measured in terms of the other good you could have had, not in dollars.

The Production Possibilities Curve (PPC/PPF)

A Production Possibilities Curve shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce when all resources are used fully and efficiently. It illustrates scarcity, trade-offs, opportunity cost, efficiency, and growth.

Positions relative to the curve:

  • On the Curve → efficient and attainable; all resources fully and efficiently employed.
  • Inside the Curve → attainable but inefficient: resources are unemployed or misallocated.
  • Outside the Curve → Unattainable with current resources and technology.

Drawing It Yourself: put one good on each axis (label them, e.g. Good A on the horizontal axis and Good B on the vertical). Draw the bowed-out curve from one axis to the other; mark the axis intercepts (the maximum of each good if all resources go to it). Then label a point on the curve (efficient), one inside (inefficient), and one outside (unattainable).

Shape: Constant vs. Increasing Opportunity Cost

  • A Straight-Line PPF means constant opportunity cost: resources are equally suited to producing both goods, so the trade-off never changes.
  • A Bowed-Outward (Concave) PPF means increasing opportunity cost: as you make more of one good, you give up ever-larger amounts of the other, because resources are not equally suited to both. This is the law of increasing opportunity cost, and it's the realistic case.

To read opportunity cost off a PPF table, take the change in one good divided by the change in the other between two rows.

Economic Growth

Anything that expands productive capacity shifts the entire PPF outward: more resources (labor, capital), a better-educated workforce, or improved technology. A point outside today's curve can become attainable after growth.

A technology gain or new resource that helps only one good pivots the curve outward along that good's axis alone: the other good's intercept stays put.

Drawing It Yourself: keep the same axes. For overall growth, draw a second curve entirely outside the first (both intercepts move out). For a one-good improvement, hold one intercept fixed and swing only the other intercept outward — the curve pivots rather than shifting.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Opportunity Cost is the slope
For a PPF, the marginal opportunity cost of good X (in terms of good Y) equals the absolute value of the slope of the PPF at that point. We call this the Marginal Rate of Transformation (MRT):

MRT = |Δ YΔ X|

For a straight-line PPF the slope is constant, so the MRT is constant. For a bowed-out PPF the slope steepens as you move toward more X: a geometric statement of increasing opportunity cost.

Efficiency, Formally
A point on the PPF is productively efficient (you can't make more of one good without making less of the other). Which efficient point a society should choose is a question of allocative efficiency — producing the mix people value most — and can't be answered by the PPF alone.
