UNIT 3 — PRODUCTION, COST & PERFECT COMPETITION

The Perfect Competition Model

Practice these problems →

Assumptions

A Perfectly Competitive market has: many buyers and many sellers, an identical (homogeneous) product, price-taking firms (no one can influence the price), free entry and exit, and perfect information. Real markets only approximate this, but it's the benchmark for efficiency.

The Firm Is a Price Taker

Because its product is identical to everyone else's and it's tiny relative to the market, a competitive firm can sell all it wants at the market price but nothing above it. Its demand curve is horizontal at the market price, which means:

P = MR = AR = D

So the profit-max rule MR = MC becomes produce where P = MC.

The Side-by-Side View: Market Sets Price, Firm Takes It

The classic diagram pairs two graphs. On the left, market supply and demand cross to set the equilibrium price. On the right, that price becomes a horizontal line (the individual firm's demand = MR) at which the firm picks output where P = MC. The firm cannot move the price; it can only choose quantity. Reading the two graphs together is the standard exam task: find the market price first, then carry it over as the firm's P = MR line.

Short-Run Outcomes

At the profit-maximizing quantity (where P = MC), compare price to ATC:

  • P > ATC → economic profit.
  • P = ATC → break-even (normal profit). The Break-Even Point is minimum ATC.
  • P < ATC → economic loss.

Both diagrams above are short-run snapshots — under perfect competition, profit and loss can only be temporary. With free entry and exit, the long run forces the firm to the single outcome that can persist: price equal to minimum ATC, so economic profit is exactly zero (a normal profit). This is also the long-run equilibrium of the whole industry.

The Firm's Supply Curve (Shutdown Rule)

The shutdown rule from 3.3 is what pins down the competitive firm's supply curve. Recall the rule: in the short run fixed costs are sunk, so the firm keeps producing as long as price covers average variable cost.

  • P ≥ AVC → produce (at the output where P = MC); the shutdown point is minimum AVC.
  • P < AVC → shut down.

Putting that together with "produce where P = MC," the firm's short-run supply curve is its MC curve above minimum AVC.

Long-Run Equilibrium and Efficiency

Free entry and exit drive economic profit to zero in the long run: profits attract entrants (price falls), losses cause exit (price rises), until P = minimum ATC. At that long-run equilibrium, perfect competition achieves both:

  • Productive Efficiency: output is produced at minimum ATC.
  • Allocative Efficiency: P = MC, so the last unit's value to buyers equals its marginal cost.
Go Deeper: College-Level

Why do firms in perfect competition make zero economic profit in the long-run? Zero economic profit still means a normal accounting profit — owners earn exactly their opportunity cost, so there's no incentive to enter or leave.
The mechanism is free entry and exit: when price sits above minimum ATC, the positive economic profit lures new firms in, market supply expands, and price is pushed down; when price sits below, losses drive firms out, supply contracts, and price rises.
The only resting point is P = minimum ATC (= minimum LRAC), where economic profit is exactly zero.

Two caveats sharpen the result. First, it depends on the competitive assumptions — free entry and exit and firms with access to the same technology; block entry (as with 4.1's monopoly) and profits can persist. Second, if a firm controls a genuinely scarce input (a uniquely good location, a rare talent), it can keep earning a surplus in the long run — but economists count that as a rent to the scarce factor, part of its true opportunity cost, rather than pure economic profit, so the zero-economic-profit condition still holds once that input is priced properly.

Long-Run Industry Supply
In a constant-cost industry, input prices don't change as the industry expands, so the long-run supply curve is horizontal at min LRAC. In increasing-cost industries (entry bids up input prices) it slopes up. This determines how a permanent demand increase splits between price and quantity in the long run.

Calculus toolkit: differentiating cost & revenue

Some problems hand you a total cost or total revenue function and ask for MC or MR. Both are just derivatives, and you only need one rule — the power rule:

  • The derivative of a constant (like a fixed cost) is 0.
  • The derivative of ax is a (a line's slope is its coefficient).
  • The derivative of ax² is 2ax.
  • Differentiate a sum one term at a time.

Marginal cost is the derivative of total cost. For TC(q) = 2q² + 10q + 50, MC = dTCdq = 4q + 10 (the +50 fixed cost differentiates to 0, so fixed costs never affect MC).

Marginal revenue is the derivative of total revenue. If demand is P = 100 − 2Q, then TR = P·Q = 100Q − 2Q², so MR = dTRdQ = 100 − 4Q — twice the slope of demand, which is exactly why MR lies below price for a price-setter (see 4.1).

So the whole trick: whenever a problem gives you a TC or TR formula, differentiate to get MC or MR, then apply the usual rule (P = MC, or MR = MC).
