UNIT 6 — MARKET FAILURE & THE ROLE OF GOVERNMENT

Public Goods & Common Resources

Practice these problems →

Two Defining Characteristics

Goods are classified by two yes/no properties:

  • Rival in Consumption: one person's use reduces what's available to others (a sandwich is rival; a radio broadcast is not).
  • Excludable: you can prevent non-payers from using it (a movie ticket excludes; open-ocean fish do not).

The Four Types of Goods

Excludable Non-Excludable
Rival Private goods (food, clothing) Common-pool resources (ocean fish, public grazing land)
Non-Rival Club goods (cable TV, a toll road, streaming) Public goods (national defense, a lighthouse)

Public Goods and the Free-Rider Problem

A Public Good is non-rival and non-excludable. Because you can't exclude non-payers, people have an incentive to free-ride — enjoy the good without paying — so the private market under-provides (often provides zero of) public goods. This is why government typically funds things like national defense through taxes.

Common Resources and the Tragedy of the Commons

A Common-Pool Resource is rival but non-excludable. Since no one can be excluded, each user ignores the cost their use imposes on everyone else, so the resource is overused and depleted: the tragedy of the commons (overfishing, deforestation, traffic congestion). It's essentially a negative externality on a shared resource.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Efficient provision of a public good = vertical summation
For a private good we sum individual demand curves horizontally (add quantities at each price).
For a public good — everyone consumes the same quantity simultaneously — we sum vertically, adding each person's marginal benefit at each quantity.
The efficient quantity sets that summed marginal social benefit equal to marginal cost: ΣMB = MC.
The free-rider problem is precisely that no individual, acting alone, will provide to that point.
