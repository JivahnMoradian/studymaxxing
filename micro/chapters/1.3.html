UNIT 1 — BASIC ECONOMIC CONCEPTS

Comparative Advantage & Trade

Practice these problems →

Absolute vs. Comparative Advantage

  • Absolute Advantage: producing more of a good with the same resources (or using fewer resources per unit). One producer can have an absolute advantage in both goods.
  • Comparative Advantage: producing a good at a lower opportunity cost than someone else. A producer can have a comparative advantage in only one of two goods (opportunity costs are reciprocals). Comparative advantage — not absolute advantage — determines who should specialize and trade.

Finding Opportunity Cost: Output vs. Input Problems

The data can come two ways, and the formula flips:

  • Output Problems (how much you produce per hour/worker): opportunity cost of a good = "other good ÷ this good." Bigger output number = better, so whoever produces more has the absolute advantage.
  • Input Problems (how many hours/resources to make one unit): opportunity cost of a good = "this good ÷ other good." Smaller input number = better, so whoever uses fewer inputs has the absolute advantage.

A memory aid: Output → Other over self; Input → the good's own time over the other.

Who Specializes, and Gains from Trade

Each party specializes in the good where its opportunity cost is lower (its comparative advantage), then trades for the other. Total output rises, and both can consume beyond what they could produce alone: the gains from trade. This holds even when one party is absolutely better at everything.

Example: why comparative advantage matters for trade

Your 5-year-old brother takes 2 hours to clean the house and 10 hours to do your econ homework (he doesn't know the material). You take 1 hour to clean the house and 1 hour to do the homework. You have an absolute advantage in both: you're faster at everything.

But compare opportunity costs. For you, doing the homework costs 1 hour of cleaning you give up; for your brother, cleaning the house costs very little homework-time he'd never do well anyway. Your brother is only a little worse at cleaning but hopeless at the homework, so he is comparatively less bad at cleaning, giving him the comparative advantage in house-cleaning, while you have the comparative advantage in econ homework.

So even though you're better at both, it makes sense for you to specialize in the homework and your brother to specialize in cleaning: total output is higher than if each of you split your time.

Terms of Trade

A trade benefits both sides only if the agreed price (the terms of trade) lies between the two producers' opportunity costs for that good. Each party must get the good it buys for less than it would cost to make at home; outside that range, one side is better off not trading.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Why the range is bounded by the two opportunity costs
For a trade of good X, the importer won't pay more than its own opportunity cost of making X (else it would self-produce), and the exporter won't accept less than its opportunity cost (else it loses). The overlap of those two limits is exactly the mutually beneficial range, and any price inside it splits the gains from trade between the two parties, with the split depending on bargaining.

Comparative Advantage and the PPF
With constant opportunity costs, each producer's PPF is a straight line whose slope is its opportunity cost. Specialization + trade lets each party consume at a point outside its own PPF: the clearest picture of the gains from trade.
