UNIT 2 — SUPPLY AND DEMAND

Elasticities

Practice these problems →

An Elasticity measures how sensitive one variable is to another. Every elasticity has the same shape:

Elasticity = %Δ (Response Variable)%Δ (Driver Variable)

To get a percent change, you divide the difference in values by the original value:

%Δ X = X2 - X1X1

With the midpoint (arc) method, you divide by the average of the two values instead of the starting value, so you get the same answer whichever direction you move:

%Δ X = X2 - X1(X1 + X2)/2

Price Elasticity of Demand (PED)

How sensitive quantity demanded is to the good's own price:

PED = %Δ Qd%Δ P

By the law of demand PED is negative; economists often quote its absolute value. Two ways to compute it:

  • From Two Points: take %ΔQ / %ΔP (choose the simple or midpoint method).
  • From a Demand Function Q = a − bP: use PED = (dQ/dP)·(P/Q).

The first step with any demand function is to identify dQ/dP: the slope of the demand curve (how much quantity changes for a $1 change in price). With calculus, dQ/dP is just the derivative of Q with respect to P; for a linear demand curve Q = a − bP it is simply the constant dQ/dP = −b. So PED = −b·(P/Q). The slope −b is constant, but PED still changes as you move along the line because the ratio P/Q changes.

Watch out for inverse demand. Economists often write demand with price alone on the left: P = a − bQ (this is the inverse demand function, the form you actually graph, since P is on the vertical axis). To find elasticity you usually want Q as a function of P, so rearrange it: solve P = a − bQ for Q to get Q = (a − P)/b, which makes dQ/dP = −1/b. Being fluent at flipping between the two forms Q = f(P) and P = f(Q) is a core skill, so several problems in the practice and drill sections make you do it.

Classifying PED
Using the absolute value of PED, there are five cases (all values below are |PED|):

  • |PED| = 0: perfectly inelastic; the demand curve is vertical.
  • 0 < |PED| < 1: inelastic; the demand curve is steep.
  • |PED| = 1: unit elastic (this is where revenue is maximized).
  • |PED| > 1: elastic; the demand curve is flat / shallow.
  • |PED| = ∞: perfectly elastic; the demand curve is horizontal.

What Makes Demand More Elastic? More available substitutes, the good being a larger share of the budget, it being a luxury rather than a necessity, and a longer time horizon (more time to adjust).

Elastic and Inelastic Regions of One Demand Curve

A single straight-line demand curve is not simply "elastic" or "inelastic" overall — it is both, in different regions. As you move down the curve, |PED| falls: the upper-left half is elastic (|PED| > 1), the exact midpoint is unit elastic (|PED| = 1), and the lower-right half is inelastic (|PED| < 1).

The reason is the formula PED = (dQ/dP) · (P/Q): the slope dQ/dP is constant along a straight line, but the ratio P/Q is large near the top (high price, low quantity) and small near the bottom — so elasticity shrinks as you slide down.

Total-Revenue Test

Because TR = P × Q, elasticity tells you how to move revenue:

  • Demand inelastic (|PED| < 1): price and revenue move together → raise price to raise revenue.
  • Demand elastic (|PED| > 1): price and revenue move oppositely → lower price to raise revenue.
  • Unit Elastic (|PED| = 1): revenue is maximized.

Price Elasticity of Supply (PES)

PES = %Δ Qs%Δ P

Normally positive (law of supply). More elastic supply means producers can ramp output up or down easily when price changes.

What Makes Supply More Elastic?

  • Time Horizon: supply is far more elastic in the long run, when firms can build capacity and new firms can enter; it is often nearly vertical (inelastic) in the very short run.
  • Spare (Unused) Capacity: idle machines and workers let firms expand output quickly.
  • Ability to Store Inventory: goods that keep well can be stockpiled and released as price rises.
  • Mobility of Inputs: the easier it is to move resources into (or out of) making the good, the more elastic supply is.

Income Elasticity of Demand (YED)

YED = %Δ Qd%Δ Income
  • YED > 0 → Normal good. Of these: 0 < YED < 1 = necessity (as income increases, demand increases, but less than proportionally); YED > 1 = luxury (as income increases, demand increases more than proportionally).
  • YED < 0 → Inferior good.

Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (XED)

XED = %Δ Qd of Good A%Δ P of Good B

Substitutes: two goods are substitutes when one can be used in place of the other, so buying more of one means buying less of the other. Example: Coke and Pepsi (or tea and coffee).

Complements: two goods are complements when they are used together, so buying more of one means buying more of the other. Example: printers and ink cartridges (or hot dogs and buns).

If the price of good B goes up, then by the law of demand its quantity demanded goes down. So:

  • If A and B are substitutes, the demand for A goes up (buyers switch toward A) → so XED > 0 for substitutes.
  • If A and B are complements, the demand for A goes down (B is used less, so less A is needed too) → so XED < 0 for complements.
  • |XED| > 1 means a strong relationship; |XED| < 1 means a weak one.
Go Deeper: College-Level

Point Elasticity as a Derivative
For a continuous demand curve Q(P), point elasticity is

PED = dQdP · PQ

For a constant-elasticity demand curve Q = A·P−ε, PED equals −ε everywhere (unlike the linear case, where it varies along the curve).

Why unit elasticity maximizes revenue — formally
Revenue equals price times quantity, TR = P·Q, and marginal revenue is its derivative with respect to quantity.
To maximize revenue we set that derivative (MR) to zero (from calculus).
Starting from the definition and substituting TR = P·Q:

MR = dTRdQ = d(PQ)dQ

From calculus' product rule, and since P and Q move together along the demand curve (P is a function of Q), we get:

P + QdPdQ = P + P·QP·dPdQ = P(1 + 1PED)

The middle step just multiplies and divides by P so the elasticity appears: recall PED = (dQ/dP)·(P/Q), so its reciprocal 1/PED = (dP/dQ)·(Q/P) is exactly the term in the brackets.
Revenue is maximized where MR = 0, which requires 1 + 1/PED = 0, i.e.
PED = −1 (unit elastic).
So marginal revenue is zero precisely at unit elasticity — the link between the revenue test and the MR = 0 condition you'll use in Unit 4.
