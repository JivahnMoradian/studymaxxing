UNIT 1 — BASIC ECONOMIC CONCEPTS

Scarcity, Factors & Systems

Practice these problems →

Scarcity: The Core Problem

Scarcity is the fundamental economic problem: human wants are unlimited, but the resources to satisfy them are limited. Because of scarcity, every society must make choices — and every choice means giving something up. Economics is the study of how people and societies allocate scarce resources.

Scarcity forces every economy to answer three basic questions:

  • What to produce?
  • How to produce it?
  • For Whom to produce it (who gets it)?

Factors of Production

Resources used to produce goods and services fall into four categories:

  • Land — natural resources (soil, water, minerals, timber). Payment: rent.
  • Labor — human effort, physical and mental. Payment: wages.
  • Capital — manufactured tools used to produce other goods (machines, factories, equipment). Payment: interest. (Note: "capital" here means physical capital, not money.)
  • Entrepreneurship — the initiative to combine the other three, take risks, and innovate. Payment: profit.

Economic Systems

Societies answer the three questions differently depending on who owns resources and makes decisions:

  • Market Economy: decisions are made by private individuals and firms through voluntary exchange; prices coordinate activity (the "invisible hand"). Resources are privately owned.
Example: market economy

In the modern United States, private firms decide what to produce and prices set in markets guide most activity: from which phones get made to how much coffee costs. The government sets rules but doesn't plan output.

  • Command (Planned) Economy: a central authority owns resources and decides what, how, and for whom.
Example: command economy

The Soviet Union used central planning: government ministries set five-year output targets and fixed prices, deciding how much steel, grain, and consumer goods factories would produce and who received them — with no market prices to coordinate supply and demand.

  • Mixed Economy: a blend of markets and government intervention. Virtually all real economies are mixed.

Positive vs. Normative, and the Two Branches

  • A Positive statement is factual and testable ("A minimum wage raises unemployment among low-skill workers"). A Normative statement is a value judgment about what ought to be ("The government should raise the minimum wage"). Look for should/ought/better: those signal normative claims.
  • Microeconomics studies individual decision-makers — households, firms, and single markets. Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole — inflation, unemployment, growth. This course is micro.
Go Deeper: College-Level

Rational choice and thinking at the margin
Economics models people as rational agents who weigh costs and benefits and respond to incentives. The key move is that rational agents decide at the margin — comparing the benefit of one more unit of an activity to its cost — rather than in all-or-nothing terms. This marginal-decision framework (formalized in 1.4) underlies essentially every model in the course.

Opportunity Cost is the true cost
To an economist, the cost of any choice is what you forgo — the next-best alternative given up — not just the money spent. This is why "there's no such thing as a free lunch": even a gift of time or resources has an opportunity cost.
