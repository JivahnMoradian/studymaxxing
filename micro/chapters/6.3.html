Asymmetric Information

A market has asymmetric information when one party knows more than the other. Its two key negative consequences are distinguished by when the hidden information matters:

  • Adverse Selection: hidden characteristics before a deal. The informed party self-selects in a way that harms the uninformed: used-car sellers know which cars are "lemons"; the sickest people are most eager to buy health insurance.
  • Moral Hazard: hidden actions after a deal. Being protected changes behavior: an insured driver takes more risks; a borrower uses a loan more recklessly.

The "Lemons" Problem is the classic example of adverse selection: if buyers can't tell good used cars from bad, they'll only pay an average price; owners of good cars withdraw, lowering average quality, pushing the price down further, and the market can unravel. Remedies include signaling (warranties, certifications), screening, and regulation requiring disclosure.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Signaling and Screening
Markets develop private fixes for adverse selection. In signaling, the informed party credibly reveals quality: a used-car warranty, a firm's costly advertising, or (in Michael Spence's model) education as a signal of worker ability. The signal only works if it is more costly for the low-quality type to fake. In screening, the uninformed party designs a set of choices that makes the informed party reveal its type: an insurer offers a menu of plans (high-deductible/low-premium versus low-deductible/high-premium) so that low-risk and high-risk customers sort themselves.

The Principal–agent Problem
Moral hazard is the heart of the principal–agent problem: a principal (an employer, shareholder, or insurer) cannot fully observe the actions of an agent (a worker, manager, or policyholder) whose interests differ from theirs. The usual response is to tie the agent's reward to an observable outcome, which realigns incentives: performance pay and stock options for managers, deductibles and co-pays for the insured, tips for servers.

Efficiency-Wage Idea
One more consequence of hidden effort: firms may deliberately pay above the market-clearing wage so that workers fear losing a good job and therefore shirk less. This "efficiency wage" is a reason real-world labor markets can settle with persistent unemployment rather than at the simple competitive equilibrium.
