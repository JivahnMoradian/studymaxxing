UNIT 4 — IMPERFECT COMPETITION

Monopoly

Practice these problems →

What a Monopoly Is

A Monopoly is a single seller of a product with no close substitutes, protected by barriers to entry: economies of scale (a natural monopoly), control of a key resource, patents/licenses, or government franchise. The firm is a price maker — it faces the entire downward-sloping market demand curve.

Why Marginal Revenue Is Below Price

To sell one more unit, a monopolist must lower the price on every unit (it can't price-discriminate in the basic model). So the extra revenue from a unit is its price minus the revenue lost on all the earlier units it now sells cheaper. That makes MR < P at every quantity past the first.

For a linear demand curve, there's a handy rule: if demand is P = a − bQ, then marginal revenue has the same intercept and twice the slope:

P = a - bQ  ⟹  MR = a - 2bQ

Finding the Monopoly Price and Quantity

  1. Set MR = MC and solve for the monopoly quantity Qm.
  2. Plug Qm into the demand curve (not MR) to get the price Pm buyers will pay.

Profit is then:

π = (Pm - ATC) × Qm = TR(Q) - TC(Q)

The two forms are equivalent (since TR = P·Q and TC = ATC·Q). Use whichever variant is most appropriate for the problem: the first when you're handed a price and an ATC, the second when you have revenue and cost as functions of output.

Monopoly vs. Competition, and the Deadweight Loss

A competitive industry would produce where P = MC. The monopolist produces less and charges more, so some mutually beneficial trades don't happen — creating a deadweight loss. With constant MC:

DWL = ½ × (Pm - MC) × (Qc - Qm)

A monopoly is therefore allocatively inefficient (P > MC) and typically productively inefficient (it doesn't produce at minimum ATC).

Regulating a Natural Monopoly

A Natural Monopoly has such large economies of scale that ATC keeps falling over the whole relevant range (think water or electricity distribution). Because ATC is declining, MC lies below ATC throughout. A regulator who wants to limit the firm's market power has two classic options:

  • Marginal-Cost Pricing (Socially Optimal): force P = MC. This is allocatively efficient — output expands to the point where price equals the marginal cost of the last unit. But since MC < ATC here, setting P = MC means P < ATC, so the firm takes a loss and must be given a subsidy to stay open. With constant marginal cost, P = MC means revenue exactly covers variable cost, so the loss is just the fixed cost: profit = −FC.
  • Average-Cost Pricing (Fair-Return): force P = ATC. The firm earns zero economic profit (a normal profit) and needs no subsidy, but because P = ATC > MC, some allocative inefficiency remains (a small deadweight loss).

So the regulator trades off full efficiency (marginal-cost pricing, but a subsidy is required) against letting the firm break even on its own (average-cost pricing, with a bit of inefficiency left).

Examples: why are they called "natural" monopolies?

They're called natural because the market naturally ends up with one firm — not because of patents or government favors, but because the technology makes a single large supplier the cheapest outcome. The giveaway is a huge fixed cost (the network) and a tiny cost per extra user, so average cost keeps falling the more one firm serves. Laying a second set of water pipes, power lines, or rail tracks alongside the first would just duplicate that enormous fixed cost and raise average cost for everyone. Classic examples are utilities and networks: water and sewage, electricity transmission, natural-gas pipelines, local rail, and (historically) landline telephone. Because competition is impractical, governments usually respond either by regulating the private firm's prices (the two rules above) or by running it as a public utility.

Antitrust and Regulation

When a single firm isn't the efficient outcome, governments try to protect competition instead of tolerating the monopoly. This is the job of antitrust law (called competition law outside the US). Its three main tools are: blocking mergers that would concentrate too much market power, breaking up or constraining firms that have monopolized a market, and banning anticompetitive conduct such as price-fixing, bid-rigging, and collusion between supposed rivals (the cartel behavior of 4.4). In the US the foundational statutes are the Sherman Act (1890) and Clayton Act (1914), enforced by the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission. The aim isn't to punish being big or successful — it's to stop firms from acquiring or abusing market power in ways that raise prices and shrink output below the competitive level. For natural monopolies, where competition genuinely can't work, the response is price regulation (as above) rather than breaking the firm up.

Go Deeper: College-Level

MR, Price, and Elasticity
Marginal revenue relates to the price elasticity of demand (PED, the same elasticity from 2.2):

MR = P(1 + 1PED)

where PED is the (negative) price elasticity. A monopolist always operates on the elastic portion of demand (|PED| > 1), because where demand is inelastic MR is negative: raising price would raise revenue and cut cost, so it never stops there.

The Lerner Index of Market Power
The markup of price over marginal cost is:

L = P - MCP = -1PED

It is 0 under perfect competition (P = MC) and rises as demand becomes less elastic: a compact measure of monopoly power.
