UNIT 2 — SUPPLY AND DEMAND

Demand, Supply & Equilibrium

Practice these problems →

Demand

Demand is how much of a good consumers are willing and able to buy at each price, holding everything else constant (ceteris paribus).

Law of Demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls — so the demand curve slopes downward. (Note: price is on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal.)

A change in the good's own price moves you along the curve — this is a change in quantity demanded. A change in anything else shifts the whole curve — this is a change in demand. The non-price shifters of demand:

  • Income: for a normal good, higher income shifts demand right; for an inferior good, higher income shifts it left.
  • Tastes / Preferences: advertising, trends, new information.
  • Price of Related Goods: a rise in the price of a substitute shifts demand right; a rise in the price of a complement shifts it left.
  • Expectations: if buyers expect higher prices (or scarcity) later, demand rises now.
  • Number of Buyers in the market.
Normal vs. inferior goods

A Normal Good is one you buy more of as your income rises (demand shifts right) — most goods, like restaurant meals or new clothes. An Inferior Good is one you buy less of as your income rises (demand shifts left), because you switch to better alternatives — classic examples are instant noodles, generic brands, or bus travel. The labels describe how demand responds to income, not the quality of the good.

Supply

Supply is how much of a good producers are willing and able to sell at each price, ceteris paribus.

Law of Supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises — so the supply curve slopes upward.

As with demand, a change in the good's own price is a movement along the curve (change in quantity supplied); anything else shifts the curve (change in supply). The non-price shifters of supply:

  • Input (Resource) Prices: wages, materials, energy. Higher input costs shift supply left.
  • Technology / Productivity: improvements shift supply right.
  • Supply Shocks: crop failures, natural disasters, oil shocks (left).
  • Taxes & Subsidies on production (tax → left, subsidy → right).
  • Number of Sellers in the market.
  • Expectations of future prices.

Watch the Wording. "Demand increased" always means the curve moved right. "Quantity demanded increased" means a move along the curve because of a price change. Mixing these up is the single most common early mistake.

From Individual to Market Curves

The curves above are market curves. A market demand curve is the horizontal sum of every individual buyer's demand: at each price, add up the quantities each person is willing to buy. Market supply is the horizontal sum of every firm's supply. "Horizontal" means you add the quantities (the horizontal axis) at a given price — you never add prices. This is exactly why adding more buyers shifts market demand right and adding more sellers shifts market supply right.

Equilibrium

Equilibrium is the price (P*) and quantity (Q*) where the demand and supply curves intersect, where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. To find it from equations, set Qd = Qs and solve for P, then plug P back in to get Q.

Drawing It Yourself: put price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal. Draw demand (D) sloping down and supply (S) sloping up; label both curves. Mark the intersection, then drop dashed lines to the axes to label the equilibrium price P* and quantity Q*.

The Price Mechanism (How the Market Self-Corrects)

Prices act as signals that push a market back toward equilibrium — Adam Smith's "invisible hand":

  • If the price is too high (above P*), quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded → a surplus (excess supply). Unsold goods pile up, so sellers cut the price, which raises quantity demanded and lowers quantity supplied until the market clears.
  • If the price is too low (below P*), quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied → a shortage (excess demand). Buyers compete for scarce goods, so the price is bid up, which lowers quantity demanded and raises quantity supplied until the market clears.

Predicting the Effect of Shifts

Single shifts are unambiguous:

Shift Price Quantity
Demand Increases (Right) ↑ ↑
Demand Decreases (Left) ↓ ↓
Supply Increases (Right) ↓ ↑
Supply Decreases (Left) ↑ ↓

Demand Shifts
Say income rises for a normal good: demand shifts right, and both price and quantity rise. A fall in demand does the reverse.

Supply Shifts
A better technology or cheaper inputs shifts supply right (price falls, quantity rises); higher input costs shift it left (price rises, quantity falls).

Double Shifts leave one variable ambiguous — whichever of price/quantity depends on the relative sizes of the two shifts:

Both Shift Price Quantity
Demand ↑ & Supply ↑ ambiguous ↑
Demand ↓ & Supply ↓ ambiguous ↓
Demand ↑ & Supply ↓ ↑ ambiguous
Demand ↓ & Supply ↑ ↓ ambiguous

Go Deeper: College-Level

Where the Demand Curve Comes from
In intro-college micro, the market demand curve is derived from individual utility maximization subject to a budget constraint. A consumer picks the bundle where the budget line is tangent to the highest reachable indifference curve: i.e. where the marginal rate of substitution equals the price ratio. Tracing how that optimal bundle changes as one good's price changes generates that consumer's demand curve; summing across consumers gives market demand. (Full treatment lives in the Unit 1 college-depth module on consumer theory.)

The two forces behind that downward slope — the income and substitution effects of a price change, and the rare Giffen good exception — are developed in detail in the Unit 1 consumer-choice module (1.4), the natural home for consumer theory.

Related goods in production. Beyond input prices, a firm's supply of one good depends on the prices of other goods it could make with the same resources. Goods that compete for those resources are substitutes in production — a farmer who can plant corn or soybeans will shift land away from corn (supply of corn left) when the soybean price rises. Goods that are made together are complements in production (or joint products) — beef and cowhide, or crude oil and natural gas — so anything that raises output of one automatically raises the supply of the other.
