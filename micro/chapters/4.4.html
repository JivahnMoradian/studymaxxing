UNIT 4 — IMPERFECT COMPETITION

Oligopoly & Game Theory

Practice these problems →

Oligopoly

An Oligopoly is a market dominated by a few large firms (if just two, a duopoly), with high barriers to entry and products that may be identical or differentiated. The defining feature is interdependence: each firm's best move depends on what its rivals do, so firms must think strategically. Firms have an incentive to collude — act together like a monopoly — but each also has an incentive to cheat on any agreement.

Game Theory Basics

We analyze strategic choices with a payoff matrix. In each cell the two numbers are the payoffs (Row player, Column player).

  • A Dominant Strategy is a choice that is best for a player no matter what the rival does. (A player may have none.)
  • A Nash Equilibrium is an outcome where each player is doing the best they can given the other's choice, so no one wants to unilaterally switch. If both players have a dominant strategy, the pair of dominant strategies is the Nash equilibrium.

To find a Nash equilibrium, check each player's best response to the other's move; a cell where both are best-responding is a Nash equilibrium.

The Prisoner's Dilemma and Collusion

The classic oligopoly story is a prisoner's dilemma: both firms would be better off cooperating (e.g. both keeping prices high, like a cartel), but each has a dominant strategy to defect (cut price / overproduce), so they end up at a worse outcome for both. This is exactly why cartels (such as OPEC) are unstable: every member gains by secretly cheating on the agreed quota, so collusion tends to break down.

Worked example: the Cold War arms race

The US–USSR arms race is a textbook prisoner's dilemma.
Each superpower chose between Health (spend on its people — the cooperative move) and Nukes (build weapons — the defect move).
Payoffs are (US, USSR), higher = better:

USSR: Health USSR: Nukes
US: Health (3, 3) (0, 4)
US: Nukes (4, 0) (1, 1)

Where These Payoffs Come from
If both sides invest in Health, neither wins the Cold War, but both enjoy good infrastructure and prosperous citizens — a high payoff of 3 each. If the US builds Nukes while the USSR invests in Health, the US wins the Cold War and is thrilled (payoff 4), while the defenseless USSR loses badly (payoff 0) — and vice versa. If both build Nukes, neither wins the war and both have skimped on their people, so both end up low (payoff 1 each) — though still slightly better than outright losing the Cold War (0).

Find the US's Best Response
If the USSR picks Health, the US compares Health (3) vs Nukes (4)
→ build Nukes. If the USSR picks Nukes, the US compares Health (0) vs Nukes (1)
→ build Nukes. Nukes is better either way, so Nukes is the US's dominant strategy. By symmetry, Nukes is the USSR's dominant strategy too.

The Nash Equilibrium is therefore (Nukes, Nukes) with payoff (1, 1) — the bolded cell, where neither side can do better by switching alone.
Yet both would be happier at (Health, Health) = (3, 3).
Each is dragged to the worse outcome by the temptation to gain an edge (4) and the fear of being left defenseless (0).
That gap between the cooperative (3, 3) and the equilibrium (1, 1) is the dilemma — and it's why arms-control treaties (a way to enforce cooperation) were so valuable to both sides.

Comparing the Four Market Structures

Now that all four structures are on the table, here is the whole unit at a glance:

Perfect Competition Monopolistic Competition Oligopoly Monopoly
Number of Firms Very many Many A few One
Product Identical Differentiated Identical or differentiated Unique
Price vs. MC P = MC P > MC P > MC P > MC
Long-Run Economic Profit Zero Zero Can be positive Can be positive
Barriers to Entry None Low High Very high
Efficient? Allocatively and productively efficient Neither (markup + excess capacity) No No

Two patterns worth memorizing: only perfect competition has P = MC (allocative efficiency); and only the two "easy-entry" structures (perfect and monopolistic competition) are driven to zero economic profit in the long run — high barriers let oligopolists and monopolists keep profits.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Repeated Games Sustain Cooperation
In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defection dominates. But when the game is repeated indefinitely, strategies like tit-for-tat (cooperate until the rival cheats, then punish) can make cooperation a rational equilibrium: the threat of future punishment outweighs the one-time gain from cheating. This is the formal basis for why some cartels and tacit price agreements persist.

Mixed Strategies
Some games have no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium and require mixed strategies: each player randomizes over their options so that no rival can exploit a predictable pattern.

Cournot Competition (Quantity-Setting), Worked

In the Cournot model, two firms simultaneously choose quantities, each taking the rival's output as given.
Take inverse demand P = a − bQ where Q = q₁ + q₂.
We no longer assume costs are zero: each firm has a total cost TC(qᵢ), and its marginal cost is the derivative of that, MC = dTC/dqᵢ.
For a linear cost TC(qᵢ) = c·qᵢ + FC (a per-unit cost c plus a fixed cost FC) this gives a constant MC = c — the fixed cost drops out of MC because dFC/dqᵢ = 0, so it never affects the equilibrium quantities, only each firm's profit.

Firm 1's profit is π₁ = (a − bq₁ − bq₂)·q₁ − TC(q₁).
Taking the rival's q₂ as fixed and setting marginal profit to zero, a − 2bq₁ − bq₂ − MC = 0, gives firm 1's best-response (reaction) function:

q1 = a - MC - bq22b

By symmetry firm 2's is the mirror image.
Setting q₁ = q₂ = q* and solving:

q* = a - MC3b, Q = 2(a-MC)3b, P = a - bQ

The key is that MC enters the formula — it is whatever dTC/dq gives.
The familiar textbook "divide the intercept by 2 (or 3)" rule is just the special case MC = 0: then q₁ = (a − bq₂)/(2b), which is exactly revenue maximization.
That shortcut only works when marginal cost happens to be zero; in general you must subtract MC first.

Worked Example. Let P = 120 − Q (so a = 120, b = 1) and TC(q) = 30q, so MC = dTC/dq = 30.
Then

q* = 120 - 303 = 30 each, Q = 60, P = 120 - 60 = $60.

Each firm earns (P − MC)·q* = (60 − 30)×30 = $900.
Compare the benchmarks at this MC: a monopoly/cartel makes Q = (a − MC)/2b = 45 at P = $75;
perfect competition drives P to MC = $30 with Q = 90.
Cournot's Q = 60 again sits between them.
The firms would jointly earn more by colluding than in the Cournot equilibrium — the prisoner's-dilemma tension that makes cartels unstable.

Bertrand Competition (Price-Setting)

If instead the two firms set prices on an identical product, the logic flips.
Each can capture the whole market by undercutting the rival by a penny, so they keep undercutting until P = MC and economic profit is zero — the competitive outcome with just two firms.
This stark result is the Bertrand paradox: whether oligopoly looks like near-monopoly (Cournot) or near-competition (Bertrand) depends on whether firms compete in quantities or prices.

Stackelberg Competition (Quantity Leader), Worked

The Stackelberg model keeps the same demand (P = a − bQ) and the same general cost (MC = dTC/dq), but one firm moves first.
The leader chooses q₁ already knowing how the follower will react.
The follower sees q₁ as fixed and plays its Cournot best response:

q2 = a - MC - bq12b

The leader substitutes the follower's reaction into its own profit before maximizing — this is what makes it different from Cournot. Start from the leader's profit (now carrying a fixed cost FC) and plug the follower's reaction in for q₂:

π1 = (a - bq1 - bq2 - MC) q1 - FC, q2 = a - MC - bq12b

Substituting collapses the bracket to half its size, so the leader's profit and its derivative become:

π1 = 12(a - MC - bq1) q1 - FC, dπ1dq1 = 12(a - MC) - bq1 = 0

The fixed cost vanishes under the derivative (dFC/dq₁ = 0), so it does not change the output — solving gives the leader's quantity, and the follower's follows from its reaction:

q1 = a - MC2b, q2 = a - MC4b

Again MC is carried through as dTC/dq; the MC = 0 case (q₁ = a/2b) is just the special case.

Worked Example. Keep P = 120 − Q (a = 120, b = 1) and this time TC(q) = 20q, so MC = 20.
The follower's reaction is q₂ = (120 − 20 − q₁)/2 = (100 − q₁)/2.
Substituting into the leader's profit (120 − q₁ − q₂ − 20)q₁ = (50 − q₁/2)q₁ and maximizing gives

q1 = 120 - 202 = 50, q2 = 100 - 502 = 25.

Total Q = 75, price P = 120 − 75 = $45.
The leader earns (45 − 20)×50 = $1,250 and the follower (45 − 20)×25 = $625.
Moving first is a genuine advantage: the leader produces twice as much as the follower and earns double the profit (the first-mover advantage).
Total output (75) lands between the Cournot and competitive levels — so a Stackelberg market is a little more competitive than Cournot.
