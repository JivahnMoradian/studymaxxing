UNIT 4 — IMPERFECT COMPETITION

Price Discrimination

Practice these problems →

What It Is

Price Discrimination is charging different prices to different buyers for the same good, based on differences in their willingness to pay: not on differences in cost. It lets a firm with market power capture more of the surplus that a single price would leave with consumers.

Three Conditions Required

A firm can price-discriminate only if all three hold:

  1. It has market power (a downward-sloping demand curve: not a price taker).
  2. It can identify and separate buyers by their willingness to pay (or price elasticity).
  3. It can prevent resale (arbitrage) between the low-price and high-price groups.

The Degrees

  • First-Degree (Perfect): charge each buyer exactly their maximum willingness to pay.
  • Second-Degree: price varies by quantity/version purchased (bulk discounts, tiered plans).
  • Third-Degree: different groups pay different prices (student/senior discounts, peak vs. off-peak). The group with more inelastic demand is charged the higher price.

Perfect Price Discrimination: The Surprising Efficiency

A perfect (first-degree) price discriminator charges every buyer their maximum willingness to pay. Two striking results:

  • It produces the efficient quantity — the same output as perfect competition, where P = MC on the last unit — so there is no deadweight loss.
  • But all consumer surplus is captured by the producer: consumer surplus falls to zero, converting the former CS and the old deadweight-loss triangle into producer surplus.

So perfect price discrimination is efficient (no DWL) but highly unequal (consumers keep none of the surplus): a useful reminder that efficiency and fairness are different things.

Examples of (near) perfect price discrimination

True first-degree discrimination is an idealized benchmark — in reality no firm knows every buyer's exact maximum. But several practices come close: a car dealer or market-stall haggler who sizes up each customer and negotiates them up to their personal limit; college financial aid, where each family pays a different net tuition based on what they can afford (the sticker price is almost never what anyone actually pays); personalized online pricing that uses your browsing history, device, or location to tailor the price you see; and auctions, where each bidder reveals and pays close to their own willingness to pay. Each chips away at consumer surplus the way the graph above shows.

First-Degree (Perfect) — Recap

Charge each buyer their exact maximum willingness to pay.
Output reaches the efficient level (P = MC on the last unit), there's no deadweight loss, but the firm captures all the surplus (CS = 0).
It's the theoretical ceiling the other two degrees only approach.

Second-Degree Price Discrimination

Second-Degree discrimination charges different prices by the quantity or version a buyer chooses, without knowing who each buyer is. Instead of identifying groups directly, the firm designs a menu — block/quantity discounts (cheaper per unit as you buy more), tiered plans (basic / standard / premium), or versioning (student vs. pro software) — and lets buyers self-select. High-demand buyers reveal themselves by choosing the larger quantity or richer version. The firm captures the stepped area below demand: more than a single price, less than the full first-degree triangle.

Examples of second-degree pricing

Bulk/Quantity Discounts (a lower per-unit price on a 24-pack than a single can); utility and data tariffs with declining block rates; warehouse-club multipacks (a lower unit price the more you buy); software and streaming tiers (Basic / Standard / Premium), where the firm never asks your income: you sort yourself by picking a plan; and loyalty punch cards ("10th coffee free" is a quantity discount in disguise). The first examples sort buyers by quantity, the tier examples by version/quality — but all rely on buyers self-selecting from a menu, which is what makes them second- rather than third-degree. (Airline pricing, by contrast, is usually treated as third-degree — sorting business from leisure travelers for the same seat — so it appears there, not here.)

Third-Degree Price Discrimination

Third-Degree discrimination charges different groups different prices, when the firm can identify an observable signal of elasticity (age, student status, time of purchase, location). It sets MR = MC separately in each market:

MR1 = MR2 = MC

Using MR = P(1 + 1/PED), the market with less elastic demand gets the higher price — formally:

P1P2 = 1 + 1/PED21 + 1/PED1

So the inelastic group pays more. Marginal cost is the same in both markets; only the demand elasticities differ, which is what drives the price gap.

Examples of third-degree pricing

Student and Senior Discounts (more price-sensitive
→ lower price); peak vs. off-peak fares for trains, movies, and electricity; business vs. leisure airfares (last-minute business travelers are inelastic and pay more for the same seat); regional/geographic pricing of the same product; and coupons, which quietly separate the price-sensitive (who clip them) from those who won't bother.
