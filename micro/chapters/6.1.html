Market Failure and Externalities

A Market Failure is a situation where the free market, left alone, produces an inefficient quantity. There are four main types:

  • Monopoly / Market Power (covered in Unit 4)
  • Externalities (this section)
  • Public Goods (6.2)
  • Asymmetric Information (6.3)

The rest of this unit works through the last three. An Externality is a cost or benefit that spills over onto a third party not involved in the transaction: the classic cause of market failure.

Private vs. Social Costs and Benefits

The market weighs only private costs and benefits; efficiency requires the social ones:

MSC = MPC + MEC MSB = MPB + MEB

where MEC/MEB are the marginal external cost/benefit. The market settles where MPB = MPC; the efficient quantity is where MSB = MSC.

Negative Externalities → Overproduction

With a negative externality (pollution, second-hand smoke), production or consumption imposes an external cost, so MSC > MPC (or MSB < MPB). The market overproduces relative to the efficient quantity, creating a deadweight loss. The fix is a Pigouvian tax equal to the marginal external cost, which raises private cost up to social cost and shrinks output to the efficient level. The graphs below show both forms: a negative production externality (left, supply side) and a negative consumption externality (right, demand side). Both overproduce.

Positive Externalities → Underproduction

With a positive externality (vaccines, education), there's an external benefit, so MSB > MPB (or MSC < MPC). The market underproduces. The fix is a Pigouvian subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit, which raises output to the efficient level. Again both forms appear below: a positive consumption externality (left, demand side) and a positive production externality (right, supply side). Both underproduce.

Production vs. Consumption Externalities — Which Curve Diverges

An externality comes from one side of the market, and that tells you which curve splits into a private and a social version:

  • Production Externality — the spillover comes from making the good, so it sits on the cost/supply side: MSC diverges from MPC, while demand (MSB = MPB) is unaffected. Example: a factory's pollution (negative) or a beekeeper's hives helping nearby orchards (positive).
  • Consumption Externality — the spillover comes from using the good, so it sits on the benefit/demand side: MSB diverges from MPB, while supply (MSC = MPC) is unaffected. Example: second-hand smoke (negative) or a flu vaccine (positive).

Combine this with the sign of the externality:

Negative (External Cost) Positive (External Benefit)
Production MSC above MPC → overproduce MSC below MPC → underproduce
Consumption MSB below MPB → overproduce MSB above MPB → underproduce

The rule of thumb: negative → market overproduces; positive → market underproduces, regardless of which side the externality is on.

Tip: how to draw any externality graph

Two questions, in order, tell you exactly what to draw:

  1. Consumption or Production? This picks the side that splits into a private and a social curve. A Consumption externality moves the demand side (MSB separates from MPB); a production externality moves the supply side (MSC separates from MPC). The other curve stays put.
  2. Positive or Negative? This picks the direction. A Positive externality shifts the social curve to the right (society values the good more than the market does, so the efficient quantity is larger); a negative externality shifts it to the left (society values it less, so the efficient quantity is smaller).

So, for example, "negative consumption" → split the demand curve (consumption), with MSB to the left of MPB (negative): the market overproduces.

Correcting Externalities: Pigouvian Taxes and Subsidies

The standard government fix is to make the decision-maker face the full social cost or benefit, closing the gap between the private and social curves. A Pigouvian Tax on a negative externality, set equal to the marginal external cost, raises private cost up to MSC and shrinks output to the efficient quantity. A Pigouvian Subsidy on a positive externality, set equal to the marginal external benefit, raises output up to the efficient quantity. The point isn't to raise revenue or hand out money, it's to make the market internalize the spillover so that MPB = MPC lands at the same place as MSB = MSC.

Real-world example: a carbon tax

Burning fossil fuels emits CO₂, whose damage (climate change) falls on third parties worldwide, not on the buyer or seller: a textbook negative production externality. A Carbon Tax is a Pigouvian tax that puts a price per ton of CO₂ emitted, so polluters now pay for the damage they cause and cut emissions to the efficient level. Several countries use one (Sweden's, introduced in 1991, is among the highest in the world), and a related tool, cap-and-trade, reaches the same goal by capping total emissions and letting firms trade permits. The revenue is often returned to households or used to fund clean energy. The debate is usually about the right price per ton (the marginal external cost of carbon is hard to measure), not about whether the externality exists.

Finding the Deadweight Loss

The externality's DWL is the triangle between the social and private curves, spanning the gap between the market and efficient quantities. Its height is the vertical gap between MSC and MSB measured at the market quantity, and its base is the distance between the two quantities:

DWL = ½ × |Qmarket - Qefficient| × (Vertical Gap Between MSC and MSB at Qmarket)
Go Deeper: College-Level

The Coase Theorem
When property rights are clearly defined and transaction costs are low, private parties can bargain to the efficient outcome on their own: regardless of who initially holds the right. So externalities don't always require government action; they require it when bargaining is too costly (many parties, hard-to-measure harms). This reframes pollution as a problem of missing markets/property rights, not just "market failure," and motivates tradable-permit ("cap and trade") systems that create a market in the externality.
