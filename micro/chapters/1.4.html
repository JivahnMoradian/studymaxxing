UNIT 1 — BASIC ECONOMIC CONCEPTS

Marginal Analysis & Consumer Choice

Practice these problems →

Thinking at the Margin

Rational decision-makers compare the benefit and cost of one more unit of an activity — the marginal benefit and marginal cost — rather than deciding all-or-nothing.

  • Marginal Benefit (MB) = the added benefit from one more unit. It typically falls as you consume more (diminishing marginal benefit/utility).
  • Marginal Cost (MC) = the added cost of one more unit. It typically rises.

The Optimal Rule: keep doing the activity as long as MB ≥ MC, and stop at the quantity where MB = MC. That quantity maximizes net benefit (total benefit − total cost). Going further (MB < MC) subtracts from net benefit.

Ignore Sunk Costs
A Sunk Cost is money or time already spent that you can't get back no matter what you choose now. Because it doesn't change with your next decision, it is not a marginal cost and should play no role in the MB vs. MC comparison. Example: you paid $12 for a movie ticket (sunk). Twenty minutes in, the film is terrible. Staying won't refund the $12, so the only things that matter now are the marginal benefit of continuing versus the marginal benefit of leaving to do something better — walk out if MB(leaving) > MB(staying). Rational decision-makers look only at future (marginal) costs and benefits.

Utility and Diminishing Marginal Utility

Utility is economists' name for the satisfaction or benefit a person gets from consuming something. It's deliberately a made-up metric: there's no real-world unit of happiness, so economists use arbitrary units called "utils" purely to rank and compare choices (is this bundle worth more to you than that one?). The absolute number is meaningless; only the comparison matters.

Total Utility (TU) is the overall satisfaction from everything you consume; Marginal Utility (MU) is the extra utility from one more unit. The Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility says each additional unit adds less extra utility than the one before — the first slice of pizza is bliss, the fourth much less so. So total utility keeps rising but flattens out, while marginal utility declines. This falling MU is exactly why demand curves slope down: you'll only buy another unit if the price drops to match its lower marginal value.

The Consumer's Utility-Maximizing Rule

A consumer with a fixed income chooses the bundle that maximizes utility. The condition is equal marginal utility per dollar across all goods:

MUxPx = MUyPy

while spending all income. Intuition (the "spend your last dollar" rule): if one good gives more utility per dollar than another, shift spending toward it; its MU falls as you buy more, until the ratios equalize. Only then can no reallocation make you better off.

From Diminishing Marginal Utility to the Demand Curve

Diminishing marginal utility is the engine behind the law of demand. Think of each unit's marginal benefit in dollars — the most you'd willingly pay for it (your willingness to pay). Because MU falls, that dollar value falls with each extra unit. A consumer buys any unit whose marginal benefit (in dollars) is at least the price, so:

  • At a high price, only the first few high-value units clear the bar → small quantity demanded.
  • At a low price, more units clear the bar → larger quantity demanded.

Tracing quantity demanded against each price gives a downward-sloping demand curve. In fact, the demand curve is the marginal-benefit curve: the price of the last unit bought equals its marginal benefit.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Indifference Curves and the Budget Line
A consumer's preferences are drawn as indifference curves — each shows all bundles giving equal utility, and curves farther from the origin are preferred. The Budget Constraint shows what's affordable:

Px · X + Py · Y = Income

which is a straight line whose slope is the negative price ratio:

Slope = -PxPy

The optimum is where the budget line is tangent to the highest reachable indifference curve — where the marginal rate of substitution (the slope of the indifference curve) equals the price ratio:

MRS = MUxMUy = PxPy

Cross-multiplying this tangency condition rearranges it into exactly the marginal-utility-per-dollar rule from above (MUx/Px = MUy/Py), so the indifference-curve approach and the "spend your last dollar" rule are the same condition, just written two ways.

Income and Substitution Effects
When the price of a good falls, the quantity demanded of that good changes through two separate channels:

  • Substitution Effect: the good is now cheaper relative to other goods, so the consumer swaps toward it. A price drop always makes the good relatively cheaper, so the substitution effect always increases the quantity demanded of that good (and a price rise always decreases it).
  • Income Effect — the price drop also raises the consumer's real purchasing power (the same income now buys more). Which way this pushes quantity demanded depends on the type of good:
    • Normal Good: higher real income raises quantity demanded, so the income effect reinforces the substitution effect. Quantity demanded clearly rises when the price falls.
    • Inferior Good: higher real income lowers quantity demanded, so the income effect opposes the substitution effect. Quantity demanded still usually rises, because the substitution effect normally dominates.

Giffen Goods
A Giffen Good is the rare, extreme case of an inferior good where the income effect is so large that it outweighs the substitution effect. The result is a good whose quantity demanded rises when its price rises — an upward-sloping demand curve that violates the law of demand. The classic (and debated) examples are staple foods — potatoes or bread — that eat up most of a poor household's budget: when the staple's price rises, people become so much poorer in real terms that they drop the small luxuries they had and buy even more of the cheap staple just to get enough calories.
