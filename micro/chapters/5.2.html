UNIT 5 — FACTOR MARKETS

Competitive Labor Markets & Shifts

Practice these problems →

Market vs. Firm

The Market Demand for labor is the sum of all firms' MRP curves; the market supply of labor slopes upward (higher wages draw more workers in). The equilibrium wage and quantity of labor are set where market supply meets market demand.

A Single Competitive Firm, however, is a wage taker: it's tiny relative to the market, so it can hire all the labor it wants at the going wage. Its labor-supply curve is horizontal at the market wage, which is why the marginal resource cost is MRC = wage for that firm.

What Shifts Labor Demand

Because labor demand is MRP = MP × MR, anything that changes a worker's productivity or the value of output shifts it:

  • Product Demand/Price: higher product price raises MRP → labor demand shifts right.
  • Productivity: better training or capital raises MP → demand right.
  • Price of Other Inputs: if a substitute input (e.g. a machine) gets cheaper, labor demand may fall; if a complementary input gets cheaper and output expands, labor demand may rise.

What Shifts Labor Supply

  • Number of Workers (population, immigration): more workers shift supply right.
  • Preferences / Non-Wage Factors (working conditions, amenities).
  • Wages in Other Markets: higher pay elsewhere pulls workers away, shifting this market's supply left.

Reading the Outcome

A rightward shift in labor demand raises both wage and employment; a rightward shift in labor supply lowers the wage but raises employment (the usual supply-and-demand logic from Unit 2, now in the labor market).

Go Deeper: College-Level

The Backward-Bending Labor Supply Curve
For an individual, a higher wage has a substitution effect (work more — leisure is now costlier) and an income effect (work less — you can afford more leisure). At low wages the substitution effect dominates and supply slopes up; at very high wages the income effect can dominate, producing a backward-bending individual labor supply curve. Market supply is still typically upward-sloping because new entrants outweigh this.

Substitute vs. complement inputs — why "it depends." A change in the price of another input works through two opposing channels. If capital is a substitute for labor, a cheaper machine makes firms replace workers (the substitution effect pushes labor demand left), but cheaper machines also lower costs and can expand output (the output effect pushes labor demand right); the net direction depends on which dominates. If capital is a complement to labor (workers need the machines), there is no substitution effect — cheaper capital expands output and raises labor demand (right), while costlier capital shrinks it (left).
