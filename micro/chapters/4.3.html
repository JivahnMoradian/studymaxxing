UNIT 4 — IMPERFECT COMPETITION

Monopolistic Competition

Practice these problems →

The Market Structure

Monopolistic Competition has many firms selling differentiated (but similar) products, with low barriers to entry and exit. Product differentiation — through branding, quality, or location — gives each firm a little market power, so each faces its own downward-sloping demand curve (unlike the horizontal demand of perfect competition). Examples: restaurants, clothing brands, hair salons.

The Short Run

Each firm maximizes profit like any price-searcher: produce where MR = MC, then read the price off its demand curve. In the short run a firm can earn an economic profit (P > ATC) or a loss (P < ATC) — exactly like a firm in perfect competition (and like a monopoly too): the short-run outcome depends only on where ATC sits relative to the price. The Long Run: Entry and Exit Erase Profit

Because entry and exit are easy, profits attract new differentiated competitors (which steal away demand, shifting each firm's demand curve left) and losses drive firms out (shifting the survivors' demand right). This continues until each firm earns zero economic profit, where its demand curve is just tangent to its ATC curve. So P = ATC in the long run, but at a point where ATC is still falling, not at its minimum.

Why It's Inefficient

At the long-run equilibrium:

  • P > MC → Allocatively Inefficient (the markup means some valued units aren't produced).
  • Production is not at minimum ATC → Productively Inefficient; the gap between the firm's output and the minimum-ATC output is called excess capacity.

These inefficiencies are the "price" paid for product variety: the thing consumers get that perfect competition doesn't offer. (A side-by-side comparison of all four market structures appears at the end of 4.4, once oligopoly has been covered.)

Go Deeper: College-Level

The Tangency Condition
Long-run equilibrium occurs where the firm's downward-sloping demand curve is tangent to its ATC curve. Since the demand curve slopes down, the tangency can only happen on the downward-sloping part of ATC — never at the minimum — which is exactly why monopolistically competitive firms carry excess capacity and a positive price–marginal-cost markup even in long-run equilibrium.
