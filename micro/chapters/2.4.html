UNIT 2 — SUPPLY AND DEMAND

Government Intervention

Practice these problems →

This module applies the tools from 2.2 (elasticity) and 2.3 (surplus & DWL). Every intervention below can be read as "who gains, who loses, and how big is the deadweight loss."

Price Controls

A Price Ceiling is a legal maximum price (e.g. rent control). It only matters if it's binding — set below equilibrium — in which case quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied and you get a shortage. A ceiling above equilibrium is non-binding and does nothing.

A Price Floor is a legal minimum price (e.g. the minimum wage). It only matters if binding — set above equilibrium — producing quantity supplied greater than quantity demanded, a surplus. (In a labor market, that surplus of labor is unemployment.) A floor below equilibrium is non-binding.

Both binding controls reduce the quantity actually traded below Q* and therefore create deadweight loss (see 2.3). They can also spawn side effects: black markets, queues, and quality erosion under ceilings; wasteful surpluses under floors.

Taxes

A Proportional (Ad Valorem) Tax is a percentage of the price (e.g. a 10% sales tax) — it rotates the curve. A Per-Unit (Excise) Tax is a fixed amount per unit (e.g. $0.50/gallon of gas) — it shifts the curve parallel by the tax amount. AP graphing focuses on the excise case precisely because the parallel shift is much easier to draw than a rotation. Excise taxes are often levied on goods deemed harmful ("sin taxes" on tobacco, alcohol, gasoline).

A per-unit tax drives a wedge between what buyers pay and what sellers receive, shrinking the quantity traded. In surplus terms, it carves a rectangle of tax revenue out of the old consumer and producer surplus and destroys a deadweight-loss triangle from the trades that no longer happen:

Solving an Excise Tax Algebraically:

  • Tax on sellers: they only keep P − T, so replace P with (P − T) in the supply equation, then solve Qd = Qs.
  • Tax on buyers: they pay P + T in total, so replace P with (P + T) in the demand equation, then solve.

Either way you find the price buyers pay, the price sellers receive (differing by exactly T), and the new (lower) quantity.

Imposition vs. Incidence

Imposition = who legally hands over the tax. Incidence = who actually bears the burden. A central result: imposition doesn't matter — the market outcome is identical whether you tax buyers or sellers.

Incidence Depends on Elasticity
The more inelastic side of the market bears the larger share, because it's less able to escape the tax:

  • Demand more inelastic than supply → Consumers bear more.
  • Supply more inelastic than demand → Producers bear more.

Subsidies

A Subsidy is the mirror image of a tax — the government pays per unit, shifting the relevant curve toward more output. A subsidy to sellers: replace P with (P + S) in the supply equation (sellers effectively receive the market price plus the subsidy). A subsidy to buyers: replace P with (P − S) in the demand equation (buyers effectively pay the market price minus the subsidy). Just as with a tax, it doesn't matter which side legally receives the subsidy — the equilibrium quantity and the split of the benefit come out the same either way. Result: buyers pay less, sellers receive more, quantity rises above Q*. The government's cost is S × Q, and (absent an externality to correct) a subsidy also creates deadweight loss from over-production.

Trade Policy: Tariffs and Quotas

With trade, a country faces a world price. The standard setup is a small country that can buy or sell as much as it wants at that price, so the world supply it faces is perfectly elastic (horizontal) at the world price, and its own trade is too small to move the world price. If the world price is below the domestic equilibrium, the country imports (domestic consumers buy more than domestic firms supply); if it is above, the country exports.

  • A Tariff is a tax on imports. It raises the domestic price toward the equilibrium, so domestic production rises, domestic consumption falls, imports shrink, and the government earns tariff revenue = tariff × (quantity imported). DWL arises from both the production and consumption distortions.

- A Quota is a quantity limit on imports. It restricts supply and raises the domestic price much like a tariff, but the extra revenue (the "quota rent") goes to whoever holds the import licenses, not necessarily the government.

Go Deeper: College-Level

Statutory vs. economic incidence — the formula
The share of a per-unit tax borne by each side depends only on the two elasticities:

Consumer Share = PESPES + |PED|
Producer Share = |PED|PES + |PED|

Setting the two elasticities equal gives a 50/50 split — exactly the symmetric linear case in the practice set. The formula makes precise the rule that the relatively inelastic side pays more: whichever side has the smaller elasticity sits in the numerator relative to the sum, so it carries the larger share.

Tax Wedge & DWL
The DWL of a small tax grows roughly with the square of the tax rate and is larger when both curves are more elastic: the basis for the public-finance principle of taxing inelastic goods ("broad base, low rate").

Ramsey Rule
To raise a given amount of revenue with the least total deadweight loss, tax goods in inverse proportion to their elasticity: higher rates on inelastic goods, lower rates on elastic ones. Inelastic goods barely change quantity when taxed, so taxing them raises revenue with little distortion. (In practice this clashes with equity, since inelastic necessities like basic food take up more of a poor household's budget, making such taxes regressive.)
