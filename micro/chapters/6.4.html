Inequality and the Role of Government

Unlike the other topics in this unit, income inequality is not itself a market failure. A competitive market can be perfectly efficient and still produce a very unequal distribution of income. But efficiency is not the only thing societies care about: an outcome where many people benefit little from the economy may be judged undesirable on fairness grounds. That is why governments around the world try to reduce inequality even when markets are working efficiently. How much to redistribute is a normative question, separate from the positive question of whether the market is efficient.

Measuring Inequality: The Lorenz Curve

A Lorenz Curve plots the cumulative share of income (vertical axis) earned by the cumulative share of households, ranked poorest to richest (horizontal axis). The 45° line represents perfect equality (the bottom 20% of people earn 20% of income, and so on). The more the Lorenz curve sags below the diagonal, the more unequal the distribution. A point like (0.40, 0.15) reads: "the bottom 40% of households earn 15% of total income."

The Gini Coefficient

The Gini Coefficient summarizes the Lorenz curve in one number:

Gini = Area Between the Lorenz Curve and the 45° LineTotal Area Under the 45° Line

It ranges from 0 (perfect equality — Lorenz curve is the diagonal) to 1 (perfect inequality — one household has all the income). A Higher Gini means more inequality.

Taxes and Redistribution

Tax systems are described by how the average rate changes with income:

  • Progressive: average rate rises with income (e.g. a graduated income tax). Reduces inequality.
  • Proportional (Flat): the same average rate at all incomes.
  • Regressive: average rate falls as income rises (e.g. a sales tax, which takes a bigger share of a poor household's income).

Governments reduce inequality through progressive taxes and transfer payments (welfare, food assistance, social insurance). Common sources of inequality include differences in human capital/education, ability and effort, inheritance/wealth, discrimination, and market power.

The Equality–efficiency Trade-Off
Redistribution can blunt incentives to work, save, and invest. The economist Arthur Okun described government redistribution through the metaphor of a "leaky bucket": moving income from rich to poor loses some along the way through administrative costs and weakened incentives. Economists debate the size of the leak, but the framework explains why the optimal amount of redistribution is a normative question balancing equity against efficiency, not something the Gini coefficient alone can settle.

Computing the Gini from Areas
The total area under the 45° line is always a triangle with base 1 and height 1, so its area is ½. Call the area between the diagonal and the Lorenz curve A. Then

Gini = A1/2 = 2A

If you only know a few Lorenz points, approximate the area under the Lorenz curve with trapezoids, call it B; then A = ½ − B and Gini = 1 − 2B.
For example, with quintile cumulative points (0.2, 0.05), (0.4, 0.15), (0.6, 0.30), (0.8, 0.55), (1.0, 1.0), summing the five trapezoid areas gives B ≈ 0.31, so Gini ≈ 1 − 0.62 = 0.38.

Marginal vs. Average Tax Rate
Progressivity is defined by the average rate (tax ÷ income), not the marginal rate (the rate on the last dollar). A tax with a single flat marginal rate but a large exempt amount is still progressive, because the average rate rises with income even though the marginal rate is constant.
