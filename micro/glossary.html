Glossary

Plain-English definitions of the key terms from every chapter (106 entries). Click a chapter tag to open the full lesson.

ABCDEFGILMNOPRSTVWZ
A
A Change in Price vs. a Change in Demand2.1 →
If a coffee gets pricier and you buy less, that is you sliding along the same demand. If something else changes (your income, a health scare, the price of tea) and you would now buy a different amount at every price, that is demand itself moving. The distinction sounds fussy but it is the heart of reading any price story in the news.
Absolute Advantage1.3 →
Being able to make something using fewer resources (or make more of it) than someone else. The faster, cheaper producer has it. One person or country can have an absolute advantage in everything.
Accounting Profit vs. Economic Profit3.3 →
Accounting profit is revenue minus the explicit (out-of-pocket) costs, the number on a tax return. Economic profit subtracts the implicit costs too, so it is always the smaller of the two and is the one that tells you whether the venture truly beats your next-best alternative.
Adverse Selection6.3 →
The trouble that comes from hidden information before a deal. The people most eager to make a deal are often the ones you least want: the sickest buy the most insurance, the worst cars are the ones owners most want to sell. The informed side "selects" into the market in a way that works against the other side.
Antitrust (Competition Law)4.1 →
The body of law used to block anticompetitive mergers, stop price-fixing, and rein in firms that abuse a dominant position.
Asymmetric Information6.3 →
One party to a deal knows something important that the other does not. The seller usually knows more about the product; the buyer of insurance usually knows more about their own risks.
B
Barrier to Entry4.1 →
Whatever stops new competitors from showing up and undercutting the price. The main ones are a patent or license, control of a scarce resource, a government franchise, or costs so large that one firm is cheapest.
C
Cap-and-Trade6.1 →
Instead of taxing each unit of pollution, the government sets a hard limit (the cap) on the total allowed, hands out or sells permits adding up to that cap, and lets firms buy and sell them. Firms that can cut cheaply do so and sell their spare permits; firms that find it expensive buy permits instead. The cap fixes the quantity of pollution; the market sets the price.
Capital-Labor Substitution5.3 →
Replacing people with equipment (or the reverse). Firms do more of it when the price of one input moves: cheaper machines or rising wages push toward automation; cheap labor pushes the other way.
Collusion and Cartels4.4 →
When the few firms agree, openly or quietly, to act like a single monopoly, holding output down and prices up. A formal agreement is a cartel. Price-fixing cartels are illegal in the US and most rich countries; OPEC is a cartel of governments, which is a different legal animal.
Command (Planned) Economy1.1 →
A central authority, not prices, decides what to make and who receives it. The former Soviet Union is the standard example: ministries set output targets and fixed prices.
Commodity3.4 →
A good that is essentially the same whoever makes it, so buyers choose purely on price. For example, a barrel of a given grade of crude, or a bushel of corn. These are the closest real-world cousins of the perfect competition model's "identical product."
Common-Pool Resource6.2 →
Rival but non-excludable, the dangerous combination. Ocean fish, groundwater and public grazing land are used up as people take them, yet no one can easily be kept out. Everyone has a reason to grab their share before someone else does.
Comparative Advantage1.3 →
Being able to make something at a lower opportunity cost than someone else, that is, giving up less of other things to make it. This is the one that matters, and here is the twist: you can only have a comparative advantage in some things, never all of them, because doing more of one thing always means doing less of another.
Consumer Surplus2.3 →
The gap between the most you would have been willing to pay and what you actually paid. If you would have paid $50 for a concert ticket and got it for $30, you walked away with $20 of consumer surplus.
D
Deadweight Loss2.3 →
Value that is destroyed, not just moved around, when something stops worthwhile trades from happening. A tax, a price control, or a monopoly can each shrink the number of trades below what buyers and sellers would freely do, and the surplus from those missing trades is lost to everyone.
Demand2.1 →
How much of something buyers want to buy at each price. As a rule, the cheaper it is, the more people buy; the pricier it is, the less.
Derived Demand5.1 →
Firms don't want workers for their own sake. They want the revenue the workers' output earns. So the demand for any kind of labor rises and falls with demand for the thing those workers make. When people stop buying a product, the jobs that make it go first.
Diminishing Marginal Returns3.1 →
The point past which each extra worker, hour or dose adds less than the previous one, because they are all sharing the same fixed resource. Note the word marginal: total output can still be rising even as each new addition helps less.
Diminishing Marginal Utility1.4 →
Each additional unit of the same thing tends to give you less extra satisfaction than the one before. The first slice of pizza is bliss; the fifth is a chore. This is why you stop, and why you would only buy more at a lower price.
Diminishing Returns5.1 →
As you pile more workers onto the same space and equipment, each one tends to add a little less than the last, so MRP falls as the workforce grows. That is why labor demand slopes downward: firms hire more only as the wage drops.
Dominant Strategy4.4 →
A choice that is your best move no matter what the other side does. Not every game has one.
E
Elastic Demand2.2 →
Buyers are sensitive to price. A small price rise drives a big drop in how much they buy, usually because there is an easy substitute or the purchase is a treat they can skip. Example: a particular brand of soda when the one next to it is cheaper.
Equilibrium Price2.1 →
The price at which the amount buyers want exactly matches the amount sellers offer, so the market "clears" with no leftover and no waiting list.
Equilibrium Wage5.2 →
The wage at which the number of workers wanted equals the number willing to work. Above it there is a surplus of workers (unemployment in that market); below it, a shortage employers struggle to fill.
Excess Capacity4.3 →
These firms typically operate below the size that would make them cheapest per unit. The half-empty restaurant at 3 p.m. is the visible face of it, and the price we pay for having lots of variety to choose from.
Excludable6.2 →
It is practical to stop people who do not pay from using it. A cinema seat is excludable (you need a ticket); the open ocean's fish are not.
Explicit Costs3.3 →
The out-of-pocket payments a business actually makes: wages, rent, materials, utilities. These are what an accountant records.
Explicit vs. Implicit Cost1.2 →
Explicit costs are the dollars you actually hand over (tuition, rent). Implicit costs are the things you forgo without a receipt, like the wages you did not earn while studying. Good decisions count both.
Externality6.1 →
A side effect of producing or consuming something that falls on a third party who is neither the buyer nor the seller. It can be negative (pollution, noise, congestion) or positive (a vaccine that also protects the people around you).
F
Factors of Production1.1 →
The four things every good or service is built from. Land (natural resources), labor (human effort), capital (tools, machines and buildings, not money), and entrepreneurship (the person who combines the others and takes the risk).
First-Degree (Personalized) Pricing4.2 →
The seller tries to charge each buyer their individual maximum. A haggling car dealer, a college financial-aid office, and some online "personalized" prices come closest.
Fixed Cost3.2 →
A cost you owe no matter how much you produce or sell, at least for now: rent, insurance, a loan payment, the lease on a plane. It is there even if output is zero.
Fixed Cost vs. Sunk Cost3.2 →
Related but not the same. A fixed cost is just one that does not vary with output; some fixed costs can still be escaped (you can cancel a monthly subscription). A cost is sunk only when it is already gone and unrecoverable. Rent for next month is fixed but not yet sunk; last month's rent is sunk.
Fixed vs. Variable Input3.1 →
In the short run, something is stuck (the size of the kitchen, the number of machines, the acres of land). The things you can still change, like the number of staff on shift, are the variable inputs. The whole chapter is about what happens when you add more of the variable input to a fixed one.
Free Entry and Exit3.4 →
In this model nothing stops new firms joining when profits are good or leaving when they are bad. That churn is what, over time, competes away any lasting profit. Examples of entry barriers include intellectual property like patents, licensing requirements in fields like law and medicine, and high fixed costs that prevent most small businesses from entering.
Free-Rider Problem6.2 →
When you cannot be excluded, you have little reason to pay. If enough people reason this way, no private company can make money providing the good, so it does not get made. This is why public goods usually have to be paid for through taxes.
G
Gains from Trade1.3 →
The extra output that appears when each party does what it is comparatively best at and trades for the rest. Total production goes up, and both sides can end up with more than if each did everything alone.
Gini Coefficient6.4 →
The Lorenz curve boiled down to a single number between 0 and 1. Zero means everyone has identical income; 1 means one person has everything. Higher means more unequal. Most countries land somewhere between about 0.25 and 0.65.
I
Implicit Costs3.3 →
The value of things the owner already owns and pours into the business instead of using elsewhere, above all their own time and savings. No check is written, but the opportunity is real and lost.
Income Elasticity2.2 →
Whether you buy more or less of something as your income grows. Restaurant meals and travel rise sharply with income (luxuries); instant noodles and bus rides can fall as income rises (people trade up).
Income Inequality6.4 →
The degree to which income is spread unevenly across people or households. It is a separate question from how rich a country is on average; two countries with the same average income can have very different gaps between rich and poor.
Inelastic Demand2.2 →
Buyers are not very sensitive to price. Even a big price rise barely changes how much they buy, usually because the thing is a necessity or has no good substitute. Example: a life-sustaining medicine, or gasoline for someone who must drive to work.
L
Labor Demand5.2 →
How many workers employers want at each wage. It slopes down (cheaper labor, more hiring) and it is derived from demand for the product: it rises when the thing those workers make becomes more valuable.
Labor Market Concentration5.4 →
A measure of how few employers do the hiring in a place and occupation. The more concentrated the market, and the harder it is for workers to move, the more wage-setting power employers have.
Labor Supply5.2 →
How many people are willing to work at each wage. It slopes up: higher pay draws more people in, pulls them from other jobs, and tempts them to work more hours.
Lorenz Curve6.4 →
A graph that lines everyone up from poorest to richest and plots what share of total income the bottom X percent earn. If the bottom 40 percent earned exactly 40 percent of income, the line would be a straight diagonal (perfect equality). The more the curve sags below that diagonal, the more unequal the distribution.
Low Barriers to Entry4.3 →
Almost anyone with some savings can open a cafe or a clothing label. That ease is exactly why good profits don't last: success invites imitators.
M
Marginal1.4 →
The effect of one more. One more slice, one more hour, one more dollar spent. Big decisions are really chains of small marginal ones, and the right question is almost always "is the next unit worth it?", not "is this thing good?".
Marginal Benefit vs. Marginal Cost1.4 →
The value of one more unit against what that one more unit costs you. Keep going while the next unit's benefit beats its cost; stop when they meet. That simple rule quietly governs how much of almost anything you should do.
Marginal Cost3.2 →
The cost of doing exactly one more unit, one more meal, one more passenger, one more download. This is the number that should drive "should I make one more?" decisions, and it is often much smaller than people expect.
Marginal Factor Cost5.4 →
The true cost of hiring one more worker. If attracting an extra worker means raising the wage, and the employer has to pay that higher wage to everyone already on staff, the real cost of that one hire is more than their wage. So a dominant employer holds back on hiring to keep the wage down.
Marginal Product3.1 →
The extra output you get from one more unit of input, such as the extra meals a sixth cook can turn out in the same kitchen. It is the "was that worth it?" number.
Marginal Revenue Product (MRP)5.1 →
The extra revenue one more worker brings in, which is their extra output multiplied by the price it sells for. This is the most a profit-minded employer will rationally pay that worker. Pay someone more than the revenue they generate and you lose money on them.
Marginal Revenue vs. Marginal Cost3.3 →
Marginal revenue is the money one more sale brings in; marginal cost is what that sale costs to make. The profit-maximizing rule is to keep going as long as the next sale brings in at least what it costs, and stop when they are equal.
Market Economy1.1 →
Prices, set by buyers and sellers, decide what gets produced and who gets it. No one is in charge; a high price signals "make more of this," a low one "make less." The modern United States is mostly this.
Market Income vs. Disposable Income6.4 →
Market income is what you earn before the government touches it (wages, interest, business profit). Disposable income is what you actually have to spend after taxes are taken out and transfers are added in. Inequality is almost always lower measured on disposable income, and the gap between the two measures is exactly how much redistribution a country does.
Mixed Economy1.1 →
A blend of the two, which is what every real country actually is. The question is never "markets or government" but how much of each, and where.
Monopolistic Competition4.3 →
A market with many firms selling similar but not identical products, and easy entry and exit. Each firm has a little pricing power from being different, but plenty of close substitutes next door.
Monopoly4.1 →
A single seller of a product with no close substitute. Because there is nowhere else to go, the seller sets the price rather than taking it from the market.
Monopsony5.4 →
A market with one dominant buyer. A monopoly is a single seller with power over its selling price; a monopsony is a single buyer, here a buyer of labor, with power over the wage it pays. The classic picture is the one big employer in a town, but it also arises for specialized workers who have few places to take their skills.
Moral Hazard6.3 →
The trouble that comes from hidden behavior after a deal. Being protected changes how carefully people act. An insured driver may park more carelessly; a company bailed out once may take bigger risks next time. The behavior is hidden from whoever bears the cost.
N
Nash Equilibrium4.4 →
An outcome where no one can do better by changing their move alone, given what everyone else is doing. It is where strategic situations tend to settle, even when a better deal for all exists.
Natural Monopoly4.1 →
A market where one firm can serve everyone more cheaply than several could, because the upfront network (pipes, wires, track) costs a fortune and each extra customer costs almost nothing. Water, local electricity delivery, and gas pipelines are the classic cases.
Negative Externality6.1 →
The spillover is a cost. Because the person causing it does not pay for it, the market produces too much of it. Example: a power plant's air pollution.
Normal Profit3.3 →
The break-even line for economic profit. A business earning zero economic profit is not failing; it is covering every cost including the owner's opportunity cost, so the owner is doing exactly as well as they would have elsewhere.
O
Oligopoly4.4 →
A market dominated by a few large firms, with high barriers to entry. The telltale sign is interdependence: each firm has to guess and react to what the others will do.
Opportunity Cost1.2 →
The value of the next-best thing you gave up when you made a choice. The cost of an evening on the couch is the workout, the side gig or the friend you did not see. Money is only part of it, and sometimes not the main part.
P
Patent4.1 →
A time-limited legal monopoly (20 years from filing in the US) that lets an inventor, often a drug company, be the only seller while it lasts. It is a deliberate trade: higher prices now in exchange for the invention existing at all.
Pigouvian Tax6.1 →
A tax set equal to the damage an activity does to others, named after economist Arthur Pigou. The idea is not mainly to raise money but to make the polluter feel the full cost, so they cut back to the amount society actually wants. A carbon tax is the best-known example.
Positive Externality6.1 →
The spillover is a benefit others get for free. Because the person creating it is not paid for it, the market produces too little. Example: getting vaccinated, or restoring an old building that makes a street nicer.
Price Ceiling2.4 →
A legal maximum price, set to protect buyers. Rent control is the classic example. A ceiling only "bites" if it is set below what the market price would be, and when it does, more people want the good than is available, so you get a shortage (and often waiting lists, or quality that slips).
Price Discrimination4.2 →
Selling the same (or nearly the same) good at different prices to different buyers, aimed at charging more to people who will pay more. It is legal and common, and not about cost differences.
Price Floor2.4 →
A legal minimum price, set to protect sellers or workers. The minimum wage is the classic example (a floor under the price of labor). A floor only bites if it is above the market price, and when it does, more is offered for sale than buyers want, so you get a surplus. In a labor market, a surplus of workers means unemployment.
Price Taker3.4 →
A seller so small, with a product so identical to everyone else's, that it cannot nudge the market price; it just accepts whatever the market sets and decides only how much to produce. A wheat farmer is a price taker; Apple, setting iPhone prices, is not.
Price Taker vs. Market Power5.1 →
A firm that sells at a fixed going price values a worker at output times that price. A firm that has to cut its price to sell more earns less on each extra unit, so its workers' MRP is lower and it hires fewer of them.
Price-Gouging Law2.4 →
A rule that bans sharp price increases during emergencies. It is a temporary price ceiling, and it carries the same trade-off: it holds prices down for those who can buy, but can worsen shortages and discourage extra supply from rushing in.
Prisoner's Dilemma4.4 →
A situation where each side has a private incentive to "defect" (cut price, overproduce, confess), so both defect and both end up worse off than if they had cooperated. It is the core reason cartels are unstable.
Producer Surplus2.3 →
The mirror image of consumer surplus, but for sellers: the gap between the lowest price a seller would have accepted and the price they actually got.
Product Differentiation4.3 →
The thing that makes your coffee shop not quite the same as the one across the street (e.g. recipe, location, decor, brand, service…). It is what lets each firm charge a bit more than a pure commodity seller could.
Production Possibilities Frontier (PPF)1.2 →
A simple picture of a trade-off. It shows the most of two things you can have at once given fixed time or resources. Pick more of one and you slide along the curve, giving up some of the other. "Guns versus butter", a country choosing between military and civilian output, is the classic version.
Progressive Tax6.4 →
One where higher earners pay a larger share of their income (a graduated income tax). It narrows the gap. The opposite, a regressive tax, takes a bigger share from lower earners (a sales tax does this, because the poor spend more of what they earn).
Public Good6.2 →
Non-rival and non-excludable at once. National defense is the classic case: it protects everyone in the country whether or not they chip in, and one person being defended does not reduce anyone else's protection.
R
Rival6.2 →
My using it leaves less for you. A sandwich is rival; if I eat it, you cannot. A radio broadcast is not rival; my listening does not reduce your reception.
S
Scarcity1.1 →
The basic fact that wants are effectively unlimited while time, money, workers and materials are limited. Scarcity is why every choice has a cost, and why "free" rarely means costless.
Screening6.3 →
When the uninformed side sets up choices that make the other side reveal itself. An insurer offering a cheap high-deductible plan alongside a pricey low-deductible one lets low-risk and high-risk customers sort themselves out by which they pick.
Second-Degree (Self-Selection) Pricing4.2 →
The seller can't tell who you are, so it offers a menu, bulk discounts or Basic/Standard/Premium tiers, and lets you sort yourself by what you pick.
Shortage vs. Surplus2.1 →
A shortage is when the price is too low, so buyers want more than is available (empty shelves, waiting lists). A Surplus is when the price is too high, so sellers are stuck with unsold goods. Shortage means not enough to go around; surplus means too much.
Signaling6.3 →
When the informed side proves its quality in a way that is hard to fake. A used-car dealer offering a warranty is signaling the car is sound, because only someone confident in the car would offer to cover repairs.
Subsidy2.4 →
The reverse of a tax, a government payment per unit that lowers the price buyers pay and raises what sellers receive, pushing the quantity up. Someone has to fund it: the taxpayer.
Sunk Cost1.2 →
Money or time already spent that you cannot get back. It is not an opportunity cost of anything you do next, so it should not sway the decision in front of you. (More on this in 1.4.)
Supply2.1 →
How much of something sellers want to sell at each price. Usually the higher the price, the more sellers are willing to produce and offer.
T
Tax Incidence2.4 →
Who actually bears the cost of a tax, as opposed to who legally pays it. A crucial, counterintuitive result: it does not matter whether the law taxes the buyer or the seller; the real burden lands on whichever side is less able to walk away (the more inelastic side). Tax a good people must have, and buyers pay most of it regardless of who writes the check.
Terms of Trade1.3 →
The agreed exchange rate between the two goods. A trade helps both sides only when the price sits between their two opportunity costs; inside that band, both come out ahead.
The Least-Cost Rule5.3 →
To produce a given amount as cheaply as possible, a firm balances its inputs so that the last dollar spent on each (on workers, on machines, on contractors) buys the same amount of extra output. If a dollar spent on a machine produces more than a dollar spent on labor, the firm shifts toward machines until the two even out.
The Shutdown Decision3.4 →
When a producer is losing money, the short-run question is not "am I profitable?" but "does the price at least cover my variable (keep-the-lights-on) costs?" If yes, keep producing and chip away at the fixed costs you owe anyway; if no, stop, because producing would lose even more than simply sitting idle.
Third-Degree (Group) Pricing4.2 →
Different observable groups pay different prices, such as students, seniors, or off-peak travelers. The rule of thumb: the group that is more price-sensitive is offered the lower price.
Total Surplus2.3 →
The whole pie of value a market creates, consumer surplus plus producer surplus (plus any government revenue when there is a tax). A free, competitive market tends to make this pie as large as possible, because every trade that makes both sides better off actually happens.
Tragedy of the Commons6.2 →
When a shared resource has no gatekeeper, each user takes as much as they can because the cost of overuse is spread across everyone, while the benefit of grabbing more is all theirs. The predictable result is that the resource gets exhausted.
Transfers6.4 →
Payments from government to households, such as pensions, unemployment benefits, food assistance and child benefits. Together with progressive taxes, transfers are the main tool governments use to reduce inequality.
V
Variable Cost3.2 →
A cost that rises and falls with how much you do: ingredients, fuel, hourly wages, packaging. No output, no variable cost.
W
Wage Taker5.2 →
A single small employer that is too minor to move the market wage, so it simply pays the going rate. Most firms are wage takers; the exception, a dominant local employer, is the monopsony case in 5.4.
What Shifts Demand5.2 →
Anything that changes how much revenue a worker generates. Booming product demand, higher productivity, or a complementary tool getting cheaper all push labor demand up and wages up.
What Shifts Supply5.2 →
Anything that changes how many people are available. Immigration or population growth, people moving in from other regions or occupations, or a drop in pay elsewhere all push supply up, which tends to lower the wage while raising employment.
Willingness to Pay4.2 →
The most a given buyer would hand over before walking away. In price discrimination, the whole game is to charge each buyer the maximum price they would be willing to pay, instead of setting one price for all.
Z
Zero Economic Profit (Long Run)4.3 →
Once enough rivals pile in, the typical firm just covers all its costs, including a normal return for the owner's time and money, and no more. It can survive, but it isn't getting rich.
