A. Price Controls

Problem 1. In market M1 (Qd = 100 − P, Qs = P; equilibrium P* = 50, Q* = 50) the government sets a price ceiling of $30. Is it binding? Compute the resulting shortage or surplus. Then graph it out.

Show solution

$30 < $50, so it's binding.
Qd = 100 − 30 = 70;
Qs = 30.
Qd >
Qs → shortage of 40 units.

Problem 2. In market M1 (Qd = 100 − P, Qs = P; equilibrium P* = 50, Q* = 50) the government sets a price floor of $70. Binding? Shortage or surplus? Then graph it out.

Show solution

$70 > $50, so binding.
Qd = 30;
Qs = 70.
Qs >
Qd → surplus of 40 units.

Problem 3. In market M1 (Qd = 100 − P, Qs = P; equilibrium P* = 50, Q* = 50) the government sets a price floor of $40. What happens?

Show solution

$40 < $50, so the floor is non-binding: the market stays at P* = 50, Q* = 50.
No effect.

Problem 4. A minimum wage is set above the equilibrium wage in a labor market. Using supply/demand language, what does the resulting surplus represent?

Show solution

A binding wage floor → quantity of labor supplied exceeds quantity demanded → a surplus of labor, which is unemployment.

(Problems 5–7 use market N1: Qd = 200 − 4P, Qs = 6P − 100.)

Problem 5. Find the equilibrium price and quantity in market N1. Then graph it out.

Show solution

200 − 4P = 6P − 100 → 300 = 10P → P* = 30, Q* = 80.
(Check: 6(30) − 100 = 80 ✓)

Problem 6. A price ceiling of $20 is set in market N1 (Qd = 200 − 4P, Qs = 6P − 100; equilibrium P* = 30, Q* = 80). Binding? Shortage or surplus, and how large? Then graph it out.

Show solution

$20 < $30, so binding.
Qd = 200 − 80 = 120;
Qs = 120 − 100 = 20.
Qd >
Qs → shortage of 100 units.

Problem 7. A price floor of $40 is set in market N1 (Qd = 200 − 4P, Qs = 6P − 100; equilibrium P* = 30, Q* = 80). Binding? Shortage or surplus, and how large? Then graph it out.

Show solution

$40 > $30, so binding.
Qd = 200 − 160 = 40;
Qs = 240 − 100 = 140.
Qs >
Qd → surplus of 100 units.

Like a tax, a binding price control cuts the quantity traded below Q*, so it also creates a deadweight-loss triangle, but with no government revenue rectangle (the price gap is a transfer between buyers and sellers, not revenue).

Problem 8. A binding price ceiling of $30 is imposed in market M1 (Qd = 100 − P, Qs = P). Find the quantity traded and the deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

At $30: Qs = 30 (sellers set the traded quantity since Qs <
Qd), Qd = 70 → shortage, quantity traded = 30.
At Q = 30, buyers value the good at P = 100 − 30 = 70, sellers' cost is P = 30;
wedge = 40.
Lost quantity = 50 − 30 = 20.
DWL = ½ × 20 × 40 = $400.

Problem 9. A binding price floor of $70 is imposed in market M1 (Qd = 100 − P, Qs = P). Find the quantity traded and the deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

At $70: Qd = 30 (buyers set the traded quantity), Qs = 70 → surplus, quantity traded = 30.
Same wedge as Problem 8: at Q = 30 the demand price is 70 and supply price is 30, wedge = 40, lost quantity = 20.
DWL = ½ × 20 × 40 = $400.

Problem 10. In market M5 (Qd = 90 − P, Qs = 2P, equilibrium P* = 30, Q* = 60), a binding price ceiling of $15 is imposed. Find the quantity traded and the deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

At $15: Qs = 2(15) = 30 (sellers set traded quantity), Qd = 75 → shortage, quantity traded = 30.
At Q = 30: demand price = 90 − 30 = 60, supply price = 30/2 = 15, wedge = 45.
Lost quantity = 60 − 30 = 30.
DWL = ½ × 30 × 45 = $675.

Problem 11. A price ceiling of $12 is imposed in a market with Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = 3P. Find (a) the free-market equilibrium price and quantity, (b) the quantity actually traded under the ceiling, and (c) the deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

(a) 100 − 2P = 3P → P* = 20, Q* = 60.
(b) At $12 (below P*, so binding): Qs = 3(12) = 36, Qd = 100 − 24 = 76 → shortage; sellers set the traded quantity, so Q = 36.
(c) At Q = 36 the demand price is (100 − 36)/2 = 32 and the supply price is 36/3 = 12, a wedge of 20 over the 24 lost units (60 − 36).
DWL = ½ × 20 × 24 = $240.

Problem 12. A price floor of $40 is imposed in a market with Qd = 120 − 2P and Qs = 2P. Find (a) the equilibrium price and quantity, (b) the quantity traded under the floor, and (c) the deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

(a) 120 − 2P = 2P → P* = 30, Q* = 60.
(b) At $40 (above P*, binding): Qd = 120 − 80 = 40, Qs = 80 → surplus; buyers set the traded quantity, so Q = 40.
(c) At Q = 40 the demand price is (120 − 40)/2 = 40 and the supply price is 40/2 = 20, wedge 20 over 20 lost units.
DWL = ½ × 20 × 20 = $200.

B. Taxes

(Problems 13–16 use market M1: Qd = 100 − P, Qs = P; equilibrium P* = 50, Q* = 50.)

Problem 13. A $20 per-unit excise tax is placed on sellers. Find the price buyers pay, the price sellers receive, and the new quantity. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Sellers keep P − 20, so supply becomes Qs = P − 20.
Set Qd = Qs: 100 − P = P − 20 → 120 = 2P → P (buyers pay) = $60.
Sellers receive 60 − 20 = $40.
Q = 100 − 60 = 40 units.

Problem 14. From Problem 13, compute the tax revenue and the deadweight loss.

Show solution

Revenue = T × Q = 20 × 40 = $800.
DWL = ½ × T × (Q* − Q_tax) = ½ × 20 × (50 − 40) = $100.

Problem 15. From Problem 13, how much of the $20 tax is borne by consumers vs. producers? Why this split?

Show solution

Consumers' price rose 50 → 60 = $10; producers' receipts fell 50
→ 40 = $10.
The burden splits 50/50 because demand and supply have equal (and opposite) slopes here: equal elasticities at equilibrium.

Problem 16. Now place the same $20 tax on buyers instead of sellers. Show the outcome is identical.

Show solution

Buyers pay P + 20 in total, so demand becomes Qd = 100 − (P + 20) = 80 − P (P = price sellers receive).
Set 80 − P = P → P (sellers receive) = $40; buyers pay 40 + 20 = $60;
Q = 40.
Identical to Problem 13: imposition doesn't change the outcome.

Problem 17. In market M2 (Qd = 120 − 2P, Qs = 2P, equilibrium P* = 30, Q* = 60), a $10 excise tax on sellers is imposed. Find the buyer price, seller price, and new quantity. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Qs = 2(P − 10) = 2P − 20.
Set 120 − 2P = 2P − 20 → 140 = 4P → P (buyers) = $35, sellers get $25, Q = 120 − 70 = 50 units.

Problem 18. From Problem 17, compute the tax revenue and the deadweight loss.

Show solution

Revenue = 10 × 50 = $500.
DWL = ½ × 10 × (60 − 50) = $50.

Problem 19. In market M5 (Qd = 90 − P, Qs = 2P, equilibrium P* = 30, Q* = 60), a $15 excise tax on sellers is imposed. Find the buyer price, seller price, and new quantity. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Qs = 2(P − 15) = 2P − 30.
Set 90 − P = 2P − 30 → 120 = 3P → P (buyers) = $40, sellers get $25, Q = 90 − 40 = 50 units.

Problem 20. From Problem 19, split the $15 tax between buyers and sellers. Why is it not 50/50 here?

Show solution

Buyers' price rose 30 → 40 = $10; sellers' receipts fell 30
→ 25 = $5 (totals $15 ✓).
It's uneven because supply (slope +2) is more elastic than demand (slope −1): the more elastic side escapes more of the tax, so the relatively inelastic buyers bear the larger share.

Problem 21. In market T1 (Qd = 100 − 2P, Qs = 3P; equilibrium P* = 20, Q* = 60), a $10 per-unit excise tax on sellers is imposed. Find the buyer price, seller price, new quantity, tax revenue, and deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Sellers keep P − 10, so Qs = 3(P − 10) = 3P − 30.
Set 100 − 2P = 3P − 30 → 130 = 5P → P (buyers) = $26, sellers get $16, Q = 100 − 2(26) = 48 units.
Tax revenue = 10 × 48 = $480.
DWL = ½ × 10 × (60 − 48) = $60.

Problem 22. In market T2 (Qd = 80 − P, Qs = P − 20; equilibrium P* = 50, Q* = 30), an $8 per-unit excise tax on sellers is imposed. Find the buyer price, seller price, new quantity, tax revenue, and deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Sellers keep P − 8, so Qs = (P − 8) − 20 = P − 28.
Set 80 − P = P − 28 → 108 = 2P → P (buyers) = $54, sellers get $46, Q = 80 − 54 = 26 units.
Tax revenue = 8 × 26 = $208.
DWL = ½ × 8 × (30 − 26) = $16.

(Problems 23–24 revisit market M1 (Qd = 100 − P, Qs = P) with the $20 excise tax on sellers from Problems 13–16: buyers pay $60, sellers receive $40, quantity 40. Before the tax, P* = 50, Q* = 50.)

Problem 23. Compute consumer surplus and producer surplus after the tax. Then graph it out.

Show solution

CS is now the triangle below demand and above the buyer price of $60, out to Q = 40: CS = ½ × 40 × (100 − 60) = ½ × 40 × 40 = $800.
PS is the triangle above supply and below the seller price of $40, out to Q = 40: PS = ½ × 40 × (40 − 0) = $800.

Problem 24. Using Problem 23, find the loss of CS and the loss of PS, and verify that (CS loss + PS loss) = (tax revenue + DWL).

Show solution

Before the tax CS = PS = ½ × 50 × 50 = $1,250 each.
So loss of CS = 1,250 − 800 = $450; loss of PS = 1,250 − 800 = $450; total lost surplus = $900.
Tax revenue = 20 × 40 = $800;
DWL = ½ × 20 × (50 − 40) = $100.
Revenue + DWL = $800 + $100 = $900 ✓.
The two sides' losses are exactly the revenue that is transferred plus the DWL that is destroyed.

Problem 25. In market M2 (Qd = 120 − 2P, Qs = 2P, equilibrium P* = 30, Q* = 60), the $10 excise tax on sellers from Problem 17 makes buyers pay $35, sellers receive $25, and quantity 50. Find the loss of CS and loss of PS, and verify the accounting against tax revenue and DWL. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Before: CS = PS = ½ × 60 × 30 = $900 each.
After: CS = ½ × 50 × (60 − 35) = ½ × 50 × 25 = $625
→ loss $275.
PS = ½ × 50 × (25 − 0) = $625
→ loss $275.
Total lost surplus = $550.
Tax revenue = 10 × 50 = $500;
DWL = ½ × 10 × (60 − 50) = $50.
Revenue + DWL = $550 ✓.

Problem 26. A $15 per-unit excise tax on sellers is imposed in a market with Qd = 120 − P and Qs = 2P. Find (a) the equilibrium price and quantity, (b) the buyer price, seller price, and new quantity, (c) the tax revenue, and (d) the deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

(a) 120 − P = 2P → P* = 40, Q* = 80.
(b) With the tax, Qs = 2(P − 15) = 2P − 30; set 120 − P = 2P − 30 → 150 = 3P → buyers pay $50, sellers receive $35, Q = 70.
(c) Tax revenue = 15 × 70 = $1,050.
(d) DWL = ½ × 15 × (80 − 70) = $75.

Problem 27. A $6 per-unit excise tax on sellers is imposed in a market with Qd = 80 − 2P and Qs = 2P. Find (a) the equilibrium price and quantity, (b) the buyer price, seller price, and new quantity, (c) the tax revenue, and (d) the deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

(a) 80 − 2P = 2P → P* = 20, Q* = 40.
(b) Qs = 2(P − 6) = 2P − 12; set 80 − 2P = 2P − 12 → 92 = 4P → buyers pay $23, sellers receive $17, Q = 34.
(c) Tax revenue = 6 × 34 = $204.
(d) DWL = ½ × 6 × (40 − 34) = $18.

C. Tax Incidence & Elasticity

Problem 28. A tax is placed on insulin, for which demand is highly inelastic and supply is relatively elastic. Who bears most of the burden?

Show solution

The more inelastic side bears more. Demand is inelastic
→ consumers bear most of the tax.

Problem 29. A tax is placed on a good whose supply is nearly perfectly inelastic (e.g. land) while demand is elastic. Who bears the burden?

Show solution

Supply is the inelastic side → producers (landowners) bear nearly the entire tax.

Problem 30. A good's demand and supply are equally elastic at equilibrium. How is a per-unit tax split between buyers and sellers?

Show solution

Equal elasticities → the burden is split 50/50.

Problem 31. Explain the difference between a proportional (ad valorem) tax and a per-unit (excise) tax, and which one shifts the supply curve in a parallel fashion.

Show solution

A proportional tax is a percentage of price, so it rotates the curve (the gap grows with price). A per-unit (excise) tax is a fixed dollar amount per unit, so it shifts the curve parallel by exactly the tax amount. The excise tax is the parallel shift.

D. Subsidies

(Problems 32–33 use market M1: Qd = 100 − P, Qs = P; equilibrium P* = 50, Q* = 50.)

Problem 32. The government grants sellers a $30 per-unit subsidy. Find the price buyers pay, the price sellers receive, and the new quantity. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Sellers now receive P + 30, so supply becomes Qs = P + 30.
Set 100 − P = P + 30 → 70 = 2P → P (buyers pay) = $35.
Sellers receive 35 + 30 = $65.
Q = 100 − 35 = 65 units.

Problem 33. From Problem 32, what is the total cost of the subsidy to the government?

Show solution

Cost = S × Q = 30 × 65 = $1,950.

Problem 34. In market M2 (Qd = 120 − 2P, Qs = 2P, equilibrium P* = 30, Q* = 60), the government grants sellers a $12 subsidy. Find the buyer price, seller price, and new quantity. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Sellers receive P + 12, so Qs = 2(P + 12) = 2P + 24.
Set 120 − 2P = 2P + 24 → 96 = 4P → P (buyers) = $24, sellers get $36, Q = 120 − 48 = 72 units.

Problem 35. From Problem 34, what is the total cost of the subsidy?

Show solution

Cost = S × Q = 12 × 72 = $864.

Problem 36. In market S1 (Qd = 120 − 3P, Qs = 2P; equilibrium P* = 24, Q* = 48), the government grants sellers a $10 per-unit subsidy. Find the buyer price, seller price, new quantity, the deadweight loss, and the total cost of the subsidy. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Sellers receive P + 10, so Qs = 2(P + 10) = 2P + 20.
Set 120 − 3P = 2P + 20 → 100 = 5P → P (buyers pay) = $20, sellers get $30, Q = 120 − 3(20) = 60 units.
Cost = S × Q = 10 × 60 = $600.
DWL (over-production) = ½ × 10 × (60 − 48) = $60.

Problem 37. In market S2 (Qd = 90 − P, Qs = 2P − 30; equilibrium P* = 40, Q* = 50), the government grants sellers a $9 per-unit subsidy. Find the buyer price, seller price, new quantity, the deadweight loss, and the total cost of the subsidy. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Sellers receive P + 9, so Qs = 2(P + 9) − 30 = 2P − 12.
Set 90 − P = 2P − 12 → 102 = 3P → P (buyers pay) = $34, sellers get $43, Q = 90 − 34 = 56 units.
Cost = S × Q = 9 × 56 = $504.
DWL (over-production) = ½ × 9 × (56 − 50) = $27.

Problem 38. A $10 per-unit subsidy to sellers is granted in a market with Qd = 100 − 2P and Qs = 3P. Find (a) the equilibrium price and quantity, (b) the buyer price, seller price, and new quantity, (c) the total cost to the government, and (d) the deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

(a) 100 − 2P = 3P → P* = 20, Q* = 60.
(b) Sellers receive P + 10, so Qs = 3(P + 10) = 3P + 30; set 100 − 2P = 3P + 30 → 70 = 5P → buyers pay $14, sellers receive $24, Q = 72.
(c) Cost = 10 × 72 = $720.
(d) Over-production DWL = ½ × 10 × (72 − 60) = $60.

Problem 39. A $9 per-unit subsidy to sellers is granted in a market with Qd = 90 − P and Qs = 2P. Find (a) the equilibrium price and quantity, (b) the buyer price, seller price, and new quantity, (c) the total cost to the government, and (d) the deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

(a) 90 − P = 2P → P* = 30, Q* = 60.
(b) Qs = 2(P + 9) = 2P + 18; set 90 − P = 2P + 18 → 72 = 3P → buyers pay $24, sellers receive $33, Q = 66.
(c) Cost = 9 × 66 = $594.
(d) Over-production DWL = ½ × 9 × (66 − 60) = $27.

E. Tariffs & Quotas

(For Problems 40–42: domestic Qd = 120 − 2P, domestic Qs = 2P − 20; world price = $20.)

Problem 40. Under free trade at the world price of $20, how much do domestic consumers buy, how much do domestic firms produce, and how large are imports?

Show solution

At P = 20: Qd = 120 − 2(20) = 80; domestic Qs = 2(20) − 20 = 20;
imports = Qd − Qs = 60 units.

Problem 41. The government imposes a $10 tariff, raising the domestic price to $30. Find new domestic consumption, production, imports, and the tariff revenue. Then graph it out.

Show solution

At P = 30: Qd = 120 − 60 = 60; domestic Qs = 2(30) − 20 = 40;
imports = 60 − 40 = 20 units.
Tariff revenue = $10 × 20 = $200.
(Domestic production rose, consumption fell, imports shrank.)

Problem 42. How does an import quota of 20 units differ from the $10 tariff above in terms of who collects the extra money?

Show solution

Both restrict imports to 20 and raise the domestic price to $30. But under a tariff the per-unit markup is government revenue ($200); under a quota that same markup is the quota rent, captured by whoever holds the import licenses (foreign or domestic sellers), not necessarily the government.

(For Problems 43–44: domestic Qd = 90 − P, domestic Qs = 3P − 30; world price = $15.)

Problem 43. Under free trade at $15, find domestic consumption, domestic production, and imports.

Show solution

At P = 15: Qd = 90 − 15 = 75; domestic Qs = 3(15) − 30 = 15;
imports = 75 − 15 = 60 units.

Problem 44. A $5 tariff raises the domestic price to $20. Find the new consumption, production, imports, and tariff revenue. Then graph it out.

Show solution

At P = 20: Qd = 70; domestic Qs = 3(20) − 30 = 30;
imports = 70 − 30 = 40 units.
Tariff revenue = $5 × 40 = $200.

A tariff creates two DWL triangles, each ½ × (quantity change) × tariff: a production distortion (domestic firms expand output inefficiently as price rises) and a consumption distortion (consumers cut back as price rises).

(Problems 45–46 use the market from Problems 40–41: domestic Qd = 120 − 2P, domestic Qs = 2P − 20, world price $20. Free trade: Qd = 80, Qs = 20, imports = 60. A $10 tariff raises the domestic price to $30: Qd = 60, Qs = 40, imports = 20.)

Problem 45. Compute the total deadweight loss of the $10 tariff. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Production-distortion triangle: domestic output rises from 20 to 40 (base = 20), height = tariff = 10
→ ½ × 20 × 10 = $100.
Consumption-distortion triangle: domestic consumption falls from 80 to 60 (base = 20), height = tariff = 10
→ ½ × 20 × 10 = $100.
Total DWL = 100 + 100 = $200.

Problem 46. For the market domestic Qd = 90 − P, domestic Qs = 3P − 30, world price $15 (from Problems 43–44), a $5 tariff raises the domestic price to $20. Compute the total deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

Free trade: Qd = 75, Qs = 15.
With the tariff (P = 20): Qd = 70, Qs = 30.
Production distortion: output rises 15
→ 30 (base = 15), height = 5 → ½ × 15 × 5 = $37.50.
Consumption distortion: consumption falls 75
→ 70 (base = 5), height = 5 → ½ × 5 × 5 = $12.50.
Total DWL = 37.50 + 12.50 = $50.

Problem 47. In the $10-tariff market of Problem 45, identify every group whose surplus changes and say who gains and who loses.

Show solution

Domestic consumers lose (price rose $20
→ $30, so consumer surplus falls).
Domestic producers gain (they receive the higher $30 price and sell more).
The government gains tariff revenue = $10 × 20 imported = $200.
The net loss to the country is the two DWL triangles totaling $200: the producer gain and government revenue together fall short of the consumer loss by exactly that amount.

Problem 48. A country has domestic demand Qd = 150 − 3P and domestic supply Qs = P + 10, with a world price of $20. An $8 tariff is imposed. Find (a) the no-trade (autarky) equilibrium, (b) free-trade consumption, production, and imports, (c) the quantities, imports, and tariff revenue under the tariff, and (d) the total deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

(a) Autarky: 150 − 3P = P + 10 → P = 35, Q = 45.
(b) At the world price $20: Qd = 90, Qs = 30, imports = 60.
(c) The tariff raises the domestic price to $28: Qd = 66, Qs = 38, imports = 28; tariff revenue = 8 × 28 = $224.
(d) Two triangles — production ½ × (38 − 30) × 8 = 32, consumption ½ × (90 − 66) × 8 = 96; total DWL = $128.

Problem 49. A country has domestic demand Qd = 80 − 2P and domestic supply Qs = 3P − 20, with a world price of $10. A $6 tariff is imposed. Find (a) the autarky equilibrium, (b) free-trade consumption, production, and imports, (c) the quantities, imports, and tariff revenue under the tariff, and (d) the total deadweight loss. Then graph it out.

Show solution

(a) Autarky: 80 − 2P = 3P − 20 → P = 20, Q = 40.
(b) At $10: Qd = 60, Qs = 10, imports = 50.
(c) The tariff raises the price to $16: Qd = 48, Qs = 28, imports = 20;
revenue = 6 × 20 = $120.
(d) Production ½ × (28 − 10) × 6 = 54; consumption ½ × (60 − 48) × 6 = 36; total DWL = $90.
