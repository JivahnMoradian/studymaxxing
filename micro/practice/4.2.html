4.2

Price Discrimination

Read the theory → Drill 4.2 →

A. Conditions & Classification

Problem 1. List the three conditions a firm needs to price-discriminate.

Show solution

(1) Market power (downward-sloping demand), (2) ability to identify/separate buyers by willingness to pay, and (3) ability to prevent resale between groups.

Problem 2. Classify each as first-, second-, or third-degree price discrimination: (a) student and senior discounts, (b) a "buy 10, get a lower per-unit price" deal, (c) a dealer who haggles each customer to their personal maximum.

Show solution

(a) Third-degree (by group). (b) Second-degree (by quantity). (c) First-degree / perfect (each buyer's max).

Problem 3. Airlines charge last-minute business travelers more than vacationers who book early. Which group has more inelastic demand, and which pays more?

Show solution

Business travelers have more inelastic demand, so they are charged the higher price (third-degree price discrimination).

B. Perfect Price Discrimination

(Problems 4–6 use demand P = 100 − 2Q with constant MC = 20, the market from 4.1.)

Problem 4. What quantity does a perfect (first-degree) price discriminator produce?

Show solution

It produces the efficient quantity where P = MC: 100 − 2Q = 20
→ Q = 40 (the same as the competitive output, not the single-price monopoly's 20).

Problem 5. What is consumer surplus under perfect price discrimination, and what is the deadweight loss?

Show solution

Consumer surplus = $0 (each buyer pays their maximum willingness to pay).
Deadweight loss = $0 (the efficient quantity is produced).
All surplus becomes producer surplus.

Problem 6. Compared with the single-price monopoly (Q = 20) from 4.1, is perfect price discrimination better or worse for efficiency? For consumers? Then graph it out.

Show solution

Better for efficiency — output rises to the efficient 40 and the deadweight loss disappears.
Worse for consumers — they keep none of the surplus (CS = 0), whereas under the single-price monopoly they retained some.

C. Second- and Third-Degree Price Discrimination (College)

Problem 13. Classify each as first-, second-, or third-degree price discrimination: (a) a streaming service with Basic / Standard / Premium tiers, (b) a wholesaler whose price per unit falls the more you order, (c) a museum's student discount.

Show solution

(a) Second-degree (by version — buyers self-select a tier). (b) Second-degree (by quantity). (c) Third-degree (by observable group).

Problem 14. Second-degree price discrimination works even though the firm can't tell each buyer's type. What feature of the pricing menu makes that possible?

Show solution

Self-selection. The firm offers a menu of quantities/versions and lets buyers choose; it designs the menu so that high-value buyers voluntarily pick the larger quantity or richer version (they reveal their type through their choice), rather than the firm identifying them directly.

Problem 15. A mobile plan charges $30 for the first 5 GB and $2 per GB after that. Compared with a single per-GB price, does this block structure raise or lower the firm's revenue from heavy data users, and why?

Show solution

It raises revenue from heavy users. The high up-front block captures a lot of the surplus of someone who values data highly, while the lower marginal rate still encourages extra use — the firm extracts more than a single uniform price would.

Problem 16. Why does second-degree price discrimination capture less total surplus than first-degree (perfect) discrimination?

Show solution

Under first-degree the firm knows and charges each buyer's exact maximum. Under second-degree it does not know each buyer's type, so it must offer a menu that leaves high-value buyers some surplus (an "information rent") to keep them from picking the cheaper option. That retained surplus is why the firm captures less than the full triangle.

Problem 17. Give two real-world examples of second-degree price discrimination and name the "version" or "quantity" that separates the tiers.

Show solution

Any two of: bulk or family packs (quantity), software editions (Home / Pro — a feature/version split), declining-block utility rates (quantity), warehouse-club multipacks (quantity), loyalty punch cards ("10th coffee free" — quantity).

Problem 18. A monopolist sells the same good in two separated markets at the same marginal cost. Market A's demand is inelastic; Market B's is elastic. Which market is charged the higher price?

Show solution

Market A (the more inelastic market) gets the higher price.
Since MR = P(1 + 1/PED) must equal the common MC in both markets, the less elastic market needs a higher price to bring its MR down to MC.

Problem 19. A third-degree price discriminator faces PED = −1.5 in Market A and PED = −3 in Market B, with the same MC in both. Using the price ratio

PAPB = 1 + 1/PEDB1 + 1/PEDA

find the ratio of prices and say which market pays more.

Show solution
PAPB = 1 + 1/(-3)1 + 1/(-1.5) = 1 - 1/31 - 2/3 = 2/31/3 = 2

So the price in Market A is twice the price in Market B — the inelastic Market A is charged twice as much as the elastic Market B.

Problem 20. A cinema charges adults $15 and students $9 for the same film, with students being the more price-sensitive group. Is this third-degree price discrimination, and what must the cinema be able to prevent for it to work?

Show solution

Yes — it charges different observable groups different prices based on elasticity. For it to work the cinema must prevent resale (arbitrage): a student must not be able to buy cheap tickets and resell them to adults (hence ID checks at entry).

Problem 21. A monopolist faces Market 1: P₁ = 100 − Q₁ and Market 2: P₂ = 80 − 2Q₂, with MC = 20 in both. Find the profit-maximizing quantity and price in each market.

Show solution

Set MR = MC in each.
Market 1: MR₁ = 100 − 2Q₁ = 20
→ Q₁ = 40, P₁ = 100 − 40 = $60.
Market 2: MR₂ = 80 − 4Q₂ = 20
→ Q₂ = 15, P₂ = 80 − 2(15) = $50.
The firm charges a higher price ($60) in Market 1.

Problem 22. In your own words, why does the inelastic-demand market end up with the higher price under third-degree price discrimination?

Show solution

Because marginal revenue is MR = P(1 + 1/PED).
To make MR equal the same marginal cost in both markets, the market whose demand is less elastic (|PED| small, so 1/PED is a larger negative, making the bracket smaller) must carry a higher price.
Buyers who won't cut back much when the price rises get charged more.

Problem 23. A monopolist faces Market 1: P₁ = 120 − 2Q₁ and Market 2: P₂ = 90 − Q₂, with MC = 20 in both. Find the profit-maximizing quantity and price in each market.

Show solution

Set MR = MC in each.
Market 1: MR₁ = 120 − 4Q₁ = 20
→ Q₁ = 25, P₁ = 120 − 2(25) = $70.
Market 2: MR₂ = 90 − 2Q₂ = 20
→ Q₂ = 35, P₂ = 90 − 35 = $55.
The firm charges the higher price ($70) in Market 1, the less elastic market at these quantities.

Problem 24. A monopolist faces Market 1: P₁ = 80 − Q₁ and Market 2: P₂ = 140 − 2Q₂, with MC = 20 in both. Find the profit-maximizing quantity and price in each market.

Show solution

Set MR = MC in each.
Market 1: MR₁ = 80 − 2Q₁ = 20
→ Q₁ = 30, P₁ = 80 − 30 = $50.
Market 2: MR₂ = 140 − 4Q₂ = 20
→ Q₂ = 30, P₂ = 140 − 2(30) = $80.
Here the higher price ($80) falls in Market 2 — whichever market supports the higher price once MR = MC is solved.

Problem 25. A monopolist faces Market 1: P₁ = 150 − 3Q₁ and Market 2: P₂ = 130 − 5Q₂, with MC = 30 in both. Find the profit-maximizing quantity and price in each market.

Show solution

Set MR = MC in each (double the Q-coefficient to get MR).
Market 1: MR₁ = 150 − 6Q₁ = 30
→ Q₁ = 20, P₁ = 150 − 3(20) = $90.
Market 2: MR₂ = 130 − 10Q₂ = 30
→ Q₂ = 10, P₂ = 130 − 5(10) = $80.
The higher price ($90) is in Market 1.

Problem 26. A monopolist faces Market 1: P₁ = 100 − 3Q₁ and Market 2: P₂ = 160 − 4Q₂, with MC = 40 in both. Find the profit-maximizing quantity and price in each market.

Show solution

Set MR = MC in each.
Market 1: MR₁ = 100 − 6Q₁ = 40
→ Q₁ = 10, P₁ = 100 − 3(10) = $70.
Market 2: MR₂ = 160 − 8Q₂ = 40
→ Q₂ = 15, P₂ = 160 − 4(15) = $100.
The higher price ($100) is in Market 2.
