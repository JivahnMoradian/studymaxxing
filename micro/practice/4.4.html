A. Prisoner's Dilemma (Pricing Game)

(Problems 1–5 use this payoff matrix. Each cell is (Firm A profit, Firm B profit) in $millions.)

B: High price B: Low price
A: High price (10, 10) (2, 15)
A: Low price (15, 2) (5, 5)

Problem 1. Does Firm A have a dominant strategy?

Show solution

If B prices High, A earns 10 (High) vs 15 (Low)
→ Low. If B prices Low, A earns 2 (High) vs 5 (Low)
→ Low. Low is best either way, so A's dominant strategy is Low price.

Problem 2. Does Firm B have a dominant strategy?

Show solution

By symmetry: if A High, B earns 10 vs 15
→ Low; if A Low, B earns 2 vs 5
→ Low. B's dominant strategy is also Low price.

Problem 3. What is the Nash equilibrium, and what are the payoffs?

Show solution

Both play their dominant strategy → (Low, Low), with payoffs (5, 5).

Problem 4. Both firms would earn 10 at (High, High). Why isn't that the equilibrium?

Show solution

(High, High) is not a Nash equilibrium: from it, either firm can switch to Low and jump from 10 to 15. Each has an incentive to cheat, so the cooperative outcome is unstable; they land at (Low, Low). This is the prisoner's dilemma.

Problem 5. Define a Nash equilibrium in your own words.

Show solution

An outcome where each player is making their best choice given what the other is doing, so no player can gain by changing strategy unilaterally.

B. A Game with Only One Dominant Strategy

(Problems 6–7 use this matrix. Cells are (A, B).)

B: Left B: Right
A: Up (3, 2) (1, 4)
A: Down (2, 1) (4, 3)

Problem 6. Does each player have a dominant strategy?

Show solution

B does: if A plays Up, B earns 2 vs 4
→ Right; if A plays Down, B earns 1 vs 3
→ Right. B's dominant strategy is Right. A does not: A prefers Up if B plays Left (3 > 2) but Down if B plays Right (4 > 1).

Problem 7. Find the Nash equilibrium.

Show solution

B will play its dominant strategy Right. A's best response to Right is Down (4 > 1). So the Nash equilibrium is (Down, Right) with payoffs (4, 3).

C. Collusion

Problem 8. What is a cartel, and why do cartels tend to break down?

Show solution

A cartel is a group of firms that collude to restrict output and raise price, acting like a single monopoly. Cartels tend to break down because each member has an incentive to cheat — secretly producing more or undercutting the agreed price to grab extra profit — which unravels the agreement (a prisoner's dilemma).

D. Cournot & Stackelberg Models (College)

(These quantitative oligopoly problems use the models from the college section above. You are given each firm's total cost TC(q); first take MC = dTC/dq, then apply the Cournot/Stackelberg formulas with that MC. In Cournot both firms choose quantity simultaneously; in Stackelberg the leader chooses first and the follower best-responds.)

Problem 28. (Cournot) A duopoly faces market demand P = 120 − Q (where Q = q₁ + q₂), and both firms have total cost TC(q) = 30q. Find MC, then each firm's output, total quantity, the price, and each firm's profit.

Show solution

MC = dTC/dq = 30. Firm 1 maximizes (120 − q₁ − q₂ − 30)q₁; its reaction function is q₁ = (120 − 30 − q₂)/2 = (90 − q₂)/2, and firm 2's is symmetric.
Setting q₁ = q₂ = q*: q* = (a − MC)/(3b) = (120 − 30)/3 = 30 each.
Total Q = 60, price P = 120 − 60 = $60.
Each profit = (60 − 30) × 30 = $900.

Problem 29. (Cournot) A duopoly faces P = 130 − Q, both with total cost TC(q) = 10q. Find MC, then each firm's output, total quantity, price, and each firm's profit.

Show solution

MC = dTC/dq = 10. Symmetric Cournot: q* = (a − MC)/(3b) = (130 − 10)/3 = 40 each.
Total Q = 80, price P = 130 − 80 = $50.
Each profit = (50 − 10) × 40 = $1,600.

Problem 30. (Cournot, different costs + fixed cost) A duopoly faces P = 120 − Q. Firm 1 has TC₁(q₁) = 20q₁ + 100, firm 2 has TC₂(q₂) = 40q₂ + 100. Find each firm's MC, output, total quantity, price, and each firm's profit.

Show solution

MC₁ = dTC₁/dq₁ = 20, MC₂ = 40 (the fixed $100 drops out of MC but is subtracted from profit).
Reaction functions: q₁ = (120 − 20 − q₂)/2 and q₂ = (120 − 40 − q₁)/2.
Solving together (or using q₁ = (a − 2·MC₁ + MC₂)/3, q₂ = (a − 2·MC₂ + MC₁)/3): q₁ = (120 − 40 + 40)/3 = 40; q₂ = (120 − 80 + 20)/3 = 20.
Total Q = 60, price P = 120 − 60 = $60.
Profit₁ = (60 − 20) × 40 − 100 = 1,600 − 100 = $1,500.
Profit₂ = (60 − 40) × 20 − 100 = 400 − 100 = $300.
The lower-cost firm produces more and earns more.

Problem 31. (Stackelberg) Demand is P = 100 − Q, both firms have total cost TC(q) = 200 (a pure fixed cost). Firm 1 is the leader. Find MC, then each firm's output, total quantity, price, and each firm's profit.

Show solution

MC = dTC/dq = 0 (the $200 is fixed, so it adds nothing to marginal cost — but it is still subtracted from profit).
Follower's reaction: q₂ = (100 − 0 − q₁)/2 = (100 − q₁)/2.
The leader substitutes this into its profit, (100 − q₁ − q₂)q₁ − 200 = (50 − q₁/2)q₁ − 200, and maximizes: q₁ = 50.
Then q₂ = (100 − 50)/2 = 25.
Total Q = 75, price P = 100 − 75 = $25.
Leader profit = 25 × 50 − 200 = $1,050; follower profit = 25 × 25 − 200 = $425 (the leader's first-mover advantage).

Problem 32. (Stackelberg) Demand is P = 140 − Q, both firms have total cost TC(q) = 20q, and firm 1 is the leader. Find MC, then each firm's output, total quantity, price, and each firm's profit.

Show solution

MC = dTC/dq = 20. Follower's reaction: q₂ = (140 − 20 − q₁)/2 = (120 − q₁)/2.
Leader maximizes (140 − 20 − q₁ − q₂)q₁ = (60 − q₁/2)q₁ → q₁ = (a − MC)/(2b) = 60.
Then q₂ = (120 − 60)/2 = 30.
Total Q = 90, price P = 140 − 90 = $50.
Leader profit = (50 − 20) × 60 = $1,800; follower profit = (50 − 20) × 30 = $900.

Problem 33. (Stackelberg, different costs + fixed cost) Demand is P = 100 − Q. The leader (firm 1) has TC₁(q₁) = 10q₁ + 100, the follower (firm 2) has TC₂(q₂) = 20q₂ + 100. Find each firm's MC, output, total quantity, price, and each firm's profit.

Show solution

MC₁ = 10, MC₂ = 20. Follower's reaction: q₂ = (100 − 20 − q₁)/2 = (80 − q₁)/2.
The leader substitutes this in: profit₁ = (100 − q₁ − q₂ − 10)q₁ − 100 = (90 − q₁ − (80 − q₁)/2)q₁ − 100 = (50 − q₁/2)q₁ − 100.
Maximizing (50 − q₁ = 0): q₁ = 50.
Then q₂ = (80 − 50)/2 = 15.
Total Q = 65, price P = 100 − 65 = $35.
Profit₁ = (35 − 10) × 50 − 100 = 1,250 − 100 = $1,150.
Profit₂ = (35 − 20) × 15 − 100 = 225 − 100 = $125.
