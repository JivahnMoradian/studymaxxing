6.2

Public Goods & Common Resources

Read the theory → Drill 6.2 →

A. Classifying Goods

Problem 1. Place each good in the grid (private / club / common-pool / public): (a) national defense, (b) a sandwich, (c) ocean fish, (d) cable TV.

Show solution

(a) Public good (non-rival, non-excludable). (b) Private good (rival, excludable). (c) Common-pool resource (rival, non-excludable). (d) Club good (non-rival, excludable).

Problem 2. Classify a congested public highway with no toll.

Show solution

Common-pool resource: it's non-excludable (no toll) but rival once congested (each extra car slows everyone down).

Problem 3. Define "rival in consumption" and "excludable."

Show solution

Rival: one person's consumption reduces the amount available to others. Excludable: it's possible to prevent people who don't pay from consuming the good.

B. The Two Problems

Problem 4. Why does the free market under-provide public goods?

Show solution

Because they're non-excludable, people can free-ride — consume without paying — so firms can't capture enough revenue to cover costs, and the market under-provides (often provides none). Government provision funded by taxes is the usual remedy.

Problem 5. Explain the tragedy of the commons using overfishing as an example.

Show solution

Ocean fish are a rival, non-excludable common-pool resource. Each fisher takes as many fish as possible because they can't be excluded and don't bear the full cost of depletion; collectively this overuses and exhausts the resource: the tragedy of the commons.

Problem 6. What type of good is a lighthouse, and why is it hard for a private firm to provide profitably?

Show solution

A public good: non-rival (one ship's use doesn't diminish another's) and non-excludable (you can't stop passing ships from seeing the light). A firm can't charge users, so free-riding makes private provision unprofitable.

Problem 7. Classify each good: (a) a streaming-service subscription, (b) a city park's open lawn, (c) a public radio broadcast, (d) a slice of pizza.

Show solution

(a) Club good (non-rival, excludable). (b) Public good if uncongested (non-rival, non-excludable); becomes a common-pool resource if crowded. (c) Public good (non-rival, non-excludable). (d) Private good (rival, excludable).

Problem 8. A fishery is being depleted because too many boats fish it. Name the problem and one policy that could fix it.

Show solution

The tragedy of the commons (overuse of a rival, non-excludable resource). Fixes include assigning property rights / tradable catch quotas (ITQs), licensing limits, or taxes on the catch: all of which make users bear the cost of depletion.

Problem 9. Why can excludability turn a would-be public good into a club good? Give an example.

Show solution

If a technology or rule lets you exclude non-payers, a non-rival good becomes excludable
→ a club good. Example: an uncongested highway is close to a public good, but adding tolls (exclusion) makes it a club good.

Problem 10. Explain why private markets tend to overuse common-pool resources but underprovide public goods.

Show solution

Both stem from non-excludability. For common-pool resources (rival), users can't be excluded and ignore the depletion cost they impose
→ overuse. For public goods (non-rival), non-payers can't be excluded, so people free-ride and firms can't profit
→ underprovision.
