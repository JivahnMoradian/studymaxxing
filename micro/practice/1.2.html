Opportunity Cost & the PPF

Read the theory → Drill 1.2 →

A. Opportunity Cost on a Linear PPF

(Problems 1–2: an economy can produce at most 40 units of wheat OR 20 units of steel, with a straight-line PPF.)

Problem 1. What is the opportunity cost of (a) 1 unit of steel, and (b) 1 unit of wheat?

Show solution

(a) Giving up 40 wheat buys 20 steel, so 1 steel = 40/20 = 2 wheat.
(b) 1 wheat = 20/40 = 0.5 steel.

Problem 2. If this economy currently produces 10 units of steel, how much wheat can it produce (on the PPF)?

Show solution

Each steel costs 2 wheat, so 10 steel costs 20 wheat.
Wheat = 40 − 20 = 20 units.

B. Opportunity Cost, Efficiency & Attainability from PPF Tables

Each table below gives a bowed-out PPF. For each one, work the opportunity-cost questions, then the efficiency/attainability questions right beneath it.

Table 1: capital goods vs. consumer goods (Problems 3–9).

Combination Capital goods Consumer goods
A 0 100
B 1 90
C 2 70
D 3 40
E 4 0

Problem 3. In Table 1, what is the opportunity cost of moving from B to C (the 2nd capital good), in consumer goods?

Show solution

Consumer goods fall from 90 to 70, so the opportunity cost is 20 consumer goods.

Problem 4. In Table 1, what is the opportunity cost of the 4th capital good (moving D → E)?

Show solution

Consumer goods fall from 40 to 0 → 40 consumer goods.

Problem 5. Does Table 1's PPF show constant or increasing opportunity cost? What shape is it, and why?

Show solution

The opportunity cost of each extra capital good rises (10, 20, 30, 40), so it shows increasing opportunity cost
→ a bowed-outward (concave) PPF. Cause: resources aren't equally suited to both goods, so producing more capital pulls in progressively less-suitable resources.

Problem 6. In Table 1, moving from A to E, what is the total opportunity cost of producing 4 capital goods?

Show solution

Consumer goods fall from 100 to 0 → a total opportunity cost of 100 consumer goods.

Problem 7. Using Table 1, a plan produces 2 capital goods and 50 consumer goods. Is it attainable? Efficient? Explain.

Show solution

At 2 capital goods Table 1 allows up to 70 consumer goods, so 50 is inside the curve: attainable but inefficient — some resources are unemployed or misallocated.

Problem 8. Using Table 1, a plan produces 3 capital goods and 60 consumer goods. Is it attainable?

Show solution

At 3 capital goods Table 1's maximum is 40 consumer goods; 60 > 40, so the point is outside the curve
→ unattainable with current resources and technology.

Problem 9. Using Table 1, describe the point 2 capital goods and 70 consumer goods.

Show solution

It lies on the PPF
→ efficient and attainable: all resources fully and efficiently employed.

C. Shifts & Growth

Problem 22. Name two developments that would shift the entire PPF outward.

Show solution

Any two of: an increase in resources (more labor or capital), a better-educated/more-skilled workforce, or improved technology.

Problem 23. A new technology improves productivity only in consumer-goods production. How does the PPF change?

Show solution

The curve pivots outward along the consumer-goods axis (the consumer-goods intercept rises) while the capital-goods intercept stays fixed: only one good's maximum expands.

Problem 24. An economy is currently producing at a point inside its PPF. Can it produce more of both goods without any growth in resources or technology? Explain.

Show solution

Yes. Because it's inside the curve, it has unemployed/idle resources; simply employing them efficiently moves it toward the curve, allowing more of both goods: no growth required.

Problem 37. On a straight-line PPF an economy can make at most 50 rice or 40 tea. If it produces 45 rice, how many tea can it make?

Show solution

Each rice costs 0.8 tea, so 45 rice uses 36 tea.
tea = 40 − 36 = 4 tea.
