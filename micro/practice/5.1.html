The Demand for Labor (MRP)

A. Building the MRP Schedule

Problem 1. Compute the marginal product of each worker.

Show solution

MP = ΔTP: 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5 (workers 1–6).

Problem 2. Compute the marginal revenue product of each worker.

Show solution

MRP = MP × $4: $40, $36, $32, $28, $24, $20.

B. The Hiring Decision

Problem 3. If the market wage is $28, how many workers does the firm hire?

Show solution

Hire while MRP ≥ wage.
MRP hits $28 at the 4th worker (MRP = MRC = 28); the 5th worker's MRP is $24 < $28.
So the firm hires 4 workers.

Problem 4. If the wage falls to $24, how many workers does the firm hire now?

Show solution

Now the 5th worker (MRP = $24) is worth hiring.
The firm hires 5 workers: illustrating that the downward-sloping MRP curve is the labor-demand curve.

C. Concepts

Problem 5. Explain what it means to say the demand for labor is a "derived demand."

Show solution

Firms don't want labor for its own sake: they want it because of the product it produces and the revenue that product earns. So labor demand is derived from demand for the output; if product demand rises, labor demand rises too.

Problem 6. Why does MRP = MP × P for a firm in a perfectly competitive product market (rather than MP × MR)?

Show solution

A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker, so P = MR.
Each extra unit of output sells at the market price, making MRP = MP × P.

D. MRP when the Firm Has Product-Market Power

Problem 7. A firm with monopoly power in its product market has the marginal-product schedule MP = [10, 8, 6] for workers 1–3. Because it must lower its price to sell more, its marginal revenue per unit falls to $5, $4, $3 as output expands. (a) Compute MRP for each worker. (b) If the wage is $20, how many workers does it hire?

Show solution

(a) MRP = MP × MR: worker 1 = 10 × $5 = $50; worker 2 = 8 × $4 = $32; worker 3 = 6 × $3 = $18.
MRP falls faster than under price-taking because both MP and MR fall.
(b) Hire while MRP ≥ wage ($20): workers 1 and 2 (MRP $50, $32); the 3rd (MRP $18) is not worth it → 2 workers.

Problem 8. Suppose instead a price-taking firm with the same MP schedule [10, 8, 6] sells at a constant P = $5. (a) Compute MRP for each worker. (b) At a wage of $20, how many does it hire, and why more than the firm in Problem 7?

Show solution

(a) MRP = MP × P = [10, 8, 6] × $5 = $50, $40, $30.
(b) All three workers have MRP ≥ $20 → 3 workers.
It hires more because, as a price taker, P = MR = $5 stays constant: each worker's output still sells at full price, so MRP does not fall as fast as for a firm with market power that must cut price to sell more.
