A. Marginal Benefit & Marginal Cost

(Problems 1–3 use this table for an activity.)

Quantity Marginal Benefit Marginal Cost
1 20 6
2 16 8
3 12 10
4 8 12
5 4 14

Problem 1. What quantity maximizes net benefit?

Show solution

Produce while MB ≥ MC.
Units 1–3 all have MB >
MC (20>6, 16>8, 12>10); the 4th has MB 8 <
MC 12.
Optimal quantity = 3.

Problem 2. Why shouldn't the decision-maker choose the 4th unit?

Show solution

Its marginal benefit (8) is less than its marginal cost (12), so the 4th unit reduces net benefit by 4. Only produce units where MB ≥ MC.

Problem 3. What is the total net benefit at the optimal quantity of 3?

Show solution

Sum of (MB − MC) over units 1–3 = (20−6) + (16−8) + (12−10) = 14 + 8 + 2 = 24.

B. Utility Maximization

(Problems 4–7: a consumer has income $9. Good X costs $2; Good Y costs $1.)

Unit MU of X MU of Y
1 16 8
2 14 7
3 12 6
4 10 5
5 8 4

Problem 4. Compute the marginal utility per dollar (MU/P) for the 3rd unit of X and the 3rd unit of Y.

Show solution

X: 12 / $2 = 6 per dollar.
Y: 6 / $1 = 6 per dollar.

Problem 5. Find the utility-maximizing bundle of X and Y.

Show solution

Buy in order of highest MU/P until income runs out.
The MU/P ladders are X: 8, 7, 6, 5, 4 and Y: 8, 7, 6, 5, 4.
Taking the top rungs (both goods through the "6" level) gives X = 3, Y = 3, costing 3×$2 + 3×$1 = $9 = income. ✓

Problem 6. Verify the utility-maximizing condition holds at X = 3, Y = 3.

Show solution

MUx/Px = 12/2 = 6;
MUy/Py = 6/1 = 6.
They're equal, and all $9 is spent, so the condition MUx/Px = MUy/Py is satisfied.

Problem 7. The MU of X falls 16 → 14 → 12 → 10 → 8 as more is consumed. What law is this, and why does it matter for demand?

Show solution

The law of diminishing marginal utility. Because each extra unit is worth less, consumers will only buy more at a lower price, which is why the demand curve slopes downward.

C. Spend-Your-Last-Dollar Reasoning

Problem 8. A consumer has spent all her income and currently has MUx/Px = 10 and MUy/Py = 6. Is she maximizing utility? If not, what should she do?

Show solution

Not maximizing: X delivers more utility per dollar.
She should buy more X and less Y.
As she does, MUx falls and MUy rises until MUx/Px = MUy/Py, at which point utility is maximized.

Problem 9. State the optimal decision rule for (a) a producer choosing output and (b) a consumer splitting a budget between two goods.

Show solution

(a) Produce where MB = MC (marginal benefit equals marginal cost).
(b) Choose quantities so that MUx/Px = MUy/Py while spending the entire budget.

D. Deriving Demand from Diminishing Marginal Utility

(Problems 10–13 use this schedule. A consumer's marginal benefit — the most she'd pay, in dollars — for successive slices of pizza falls as she eats more. She buys any slice whose marginal benefit is at least the price.)

Slice Marginal benefit (willingness to pay)
1st $6
2nd $5
3rd $4
4th $3
5th $2
6th $1

Problem 10. How many slices does she buy at a price of $4? At a price of $2?

Show solution

Buy every slice with marginal benefit ≥ price.
At $4: slices 1–3 (MB $6, $5, $4 all ≥ $4); the 4th is worth only $3 < $4.
Quantity = 3.
At $2: slices 1–5 (MB $6, $5, $4, $3, $2 all ≥ $2); the 6th is worth only $1 < $2.
Quantity = 5.

Problem 11. Build the full demand schedule: quantity demanded at each price $6, $5, $4, $3, $2, $1.

Show solution

At each price, count the slices worth at least that much:

Price Quantity demanded
$6 1
$5 2
$4 3
$3 4
$2 5
$1 6

Each price equals the marginal benefit of the last slice bought: the demand curve is just the marginal-benefit schedule.

Problem 12. The price rises from $2 to $5. What happens to quantity demanded, and what does this illustrate?

Show solution

At $2 she buys 5 slices; at $5 she buys 2 slices, so quantity demanded falls from 5 to 2. This illustrates the law of demand (higher price
→ lower quantity demanded), and its cause is diminishing marginal utility: later slices are worth less, so they're only purchased when the price is low enough.

Problem 13. Explain why, when you plot price on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, the points from Problem 11 form a downward-sloping line.

Show solution

Each extra slice has a lower marginal benefit than the one before (diminishing marginal utility). Since a slice is bought only when its marginal benefit is at least the price, adding another unit of quantity requires accepting a lower price. Lower price paired with higher quantity (and vice versa) is exactly a downward-sloping demand curve.

More Practice — Utility Maximization

Problem 24. Good X costs $4, Good Y costs $2, and income is $9. Using the marginal-utility-per-dollar rule, find the utility-maximizing bundle.

Unit MU of X MU of Y
1 14 15
2 14 10
3 9 6
4 9 5
5 8 4
Show solution

First convert each MU into marginal utility per dollar (MU ÷ price):

Unit MUX ÷ $4 MUY ÷ $2
1 3.5 7.5
2 3.5 5
3 2.25 3
4 2.25 2.5
5 2 2

Then buy the highest available MU/dollar at each step, as long as the leftover income can still afford a unit:

  1. Buy Y₁ — MU/$ = 7.5 (highest affordable) → $2 spent
  2. Buy Y₂ — MU/$ = 5 (highest affordable) → $4 spent
  3. Buy X₁ — MU/$ = 3.5 (highest affordable) → $8 spent

Utility-maximizing bundle: X = 1, Y = 2 (spending $8 of $9).
The remaining $1 can't buy another unit of either good, so stop.
